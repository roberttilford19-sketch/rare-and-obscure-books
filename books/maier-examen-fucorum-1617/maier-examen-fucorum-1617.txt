DEDICATION

**Who he was.** Michael Maier was a German physician and alchemist, born in 1568, who rose to be court physician to the Emperor Rudolf II at Prague — the strangest and most alchemy-haunted court in Europe. He is remembered as the author of Atalanta Fugiens (1617/18), the most beautiful emblem book the alchemical age ever produced: fifty engraved emblems, each with a Latin epigram and a musical fugue, made with the de Bry/Merian workshop. He wrote as an insider, a learned doctor who believed the art was real.

**His circumstances and death.** Maier spent his last years writing and publishing at a furious pace — a dozen major books in a few years — defending the true chymistry against both its mockers and its counterfeiters. He died at Magdeburg in 1622, about fifty-four years old [UNVERIFIED: exact circumstances of his death are not established from the sources checked for this edition]. The Examen Fucorum, printed in 1617, belongs to his last great burst of work.

**Why his work is vital — his logic proved, not praised.** Maier does something almost no alchemical author dared: he turns fraud-buster against his own camp. His logic is a working man's logic, and he proves it by demonstration, not by praise. First he lays down what the true artist must actually be — trained in languages and the arts of speech (because the old books speak in allegory), in geometry, arithmetic, astronomy, physics, and above all medicine, in the handcrafts of the assayer and the goldsmith, and in the contemplation of mineral nature itself, learned by travel to the mines. Then he gives the test: any man who cannot show these instruments, yet promises gold, is to be tried by the balance, the cupel, the strong waters, and the touchstone — and by watching his hands. The heart of the book is a numbered catalog of actual frauds, gathered from other authors and from report: crucibles with double bottoms hiding gold, wooden stirrers soaked in dissolved silver, mines salted with gold solution, the famous half-golden nail, horn-silver passed off as transmuted lead, and the powder-projectors who toured Europe — Bragadino, Keevenhiller, Iustenhover, and, in Maier's verdict, Edward Kelley himself, who "had nothing except the colour extracted from gold." Maier names names, gives the trick, and gives the test that unmasks it. That is the vital thing: not that he defended alchemy, but *how* — by handing the honest reader the very tools to catch the thief.

**His relation to this rescue.** The Boneyard exists for works left in the in-between — and this tract has waited in Latin since 1617, described by scholars but never carried whole into English. Maier wrote it, by his own account, after gathering bile in England against the alchemical drones, and at the urging of friends who had seen the manuscript: that the good might be warned and the wicked checked. It is rescued here in that same spirit — a fraud-buster's handbook, written by a believer, for the honest student's protection.

**Where a reader goes next.** Maier's Atalanta Fugiens (1617/18) for the art at its most beautiful; J. B. Craven, Count Michael Maier, Doctor of Philosophy and of Medicine, Alchemist, Rosicrucian, Mystic, 1568-1622 (1910) for the life as scholars have reconstructed it; John Ferguson's Bibliotheca Chemica for the bibliography of the age.

— *The Editor*

# THE EXAMINATION OF THE PSEUDO-CHYMICAL DRONES, DETECTED, AND SUCCINCTLY REFUTED FOR THE SAKE OF LOVERS OF THE TRUTH

*By Michael Maier, Count Palatine, Knight, Doctor of Medicine*

*Frankfurt: printed by Nicolaus Hoffmann, at the expense of Theodor de Bry, in the year 1617*

## THE AUTHOR'S EPIGRAM

So great a virtue thrives in the bee's chaste breast
that they would rather sink and suffer rigid death
than rouse the idle drones to battle,
or fail to lay the lazy cattle low with slaughter.
Caught thieving, they live on the spoils of the favored,
so worthless, and swell with daintiness and excess.
To the rich they pander with purses and flattery,
the babbling Chymists, who have nothing of the art.
With tongue and practiced hand, trained in the art of harm,
they turn every trick of heaven in their breast to plunder.
This breed the fowler catches in his nets with birdlime,
that they may seize gold — they stoke their furnaces with fraud.
Like night-owls they hold blind birds in the nets of wickedness,
that the raw recruit may keep a rude mind and eyes,
until, deceived, each grows credulous for himself, and soon
grasps at the riches he himself promised to others.
He has hay in his horn, whom the wise man shuns unasked;
to him Cadmus's brother is a kinsman, and yawning Orcus.

## TO THE MOST RENOWNED MAN, SECOND TO NONE IN LEARNING AND IN TROPHIES, MASTER JOACHIM HIRSCHBERGER, DOCTOR OF BOTH MEDICINES, MOST EARNEST STUDENT OF CHYMISTRY, HIS DEAR AND SINGULAR FRIEND,

*Michael Maier sends greeting.*

There is no doubt, most renowned sir, that it will seem quite untimely to you — a man buried in the weightiest business — that I should have sent you this *Examination of the Pseudo-Chymical Drones* and published it as if abruptly, since it is well known how much trouble such vermin cause to a great many, indeed to the whole world, more than enough. For although drones may seem specks in the world of bees, yet with the swiftness of their flight and their predatory boldness they often overwhelm the little bees bent over their honeyed combs, and strip and spoil them of their honey-making, as experience shows. But whatever spirit moved me to the deed and the plan, if those censors and you yourself hear me out, there is no reason to fear you will take offense at this cause. For when, a few years ago in England, I had gathered some bile against such alchymical drones — or rather pseudo-chymical ones — I could not rest until I set about sketching them with a hasty pen: partly to indulge my own mind for the moment, partly to kindle, as it were, a torch for all good men, lest they easily dash their foot on a stone in those dark crypts, or strike their head on a beam — that is, lest they be cheated by those bloodsuckers and hornets who try and strive not only to suck out the blood and the substance of their wealth, but to inflict the sharpest pains on mind and body.

When this little work, such as it is, came into the hands of certain men, they pressed me to make it public and commit it to print. To this proposal — since it could harm no honest man, could help a great many, and could even restrain and reform not a few who are bent on deceiving others, and lay open to all alike the ill will of ill will alone — I neither could nor would refuse. But since it seemed wrong to commit to the light so tender an offspring, hated by many besides and weighed down by Juno's hatreds, without a patron who knows how to tell brass from gold, I chose for myself one so great as to suffice for its defense. For as painters send the pictures they have painted not to the blind but to the keen-sighted and excellent in judgment of that kind, so I bequeath these trifles and toys of mine not to the rude and unskilled in chymistry, but to the most expert craftsman of the art — both that you may continue, as you have been wont, to defend with me the truth against the drones, and that I may obtain, in exchange (as of Glaucus and Diomedes), your chymical gems and little flowers, and the bond of a firmer friendship. To you, I say, I present and offer these gluttons, these pseudo-chymical drones, to be laughed at and treated as they deserve, that you may attend this Examination as its most impartial witness and judge — indeed preside over it — and see that no one, which I would not wish, is wronged. As you judge them hateful to all good men and to be guarded against, so you will count me among your most loving friends, as I in turn count you among mine. Farewell. Given at Frankfurt am Main, in the month of September, in the year 1616.

## PREFACE TO THE BENIGN READER

Come, benign reader, let us take up in common that well-known kind of bees which, idle and slothful itself, plunders the hives — or, for whatever false color, not of a genuine kind but of a deformed body, it is taken up with a fitting name, and hence we use it for wiles, frauds, and deceptions of every sort.

With respect to the first of these meanings, we judge the pseudo-chymists may aptly be called drones, since like lazy cattle they consume the expenses and wealth of others, and sustain themselves day by day with impostures and lies. As for their colors and fruitless labors, which they promise to the more imprudent as perfecters of themselves in vain hope, with respect to the second use of the same name we think they may also rightly be called by it: for they are not distinguished from the genuine or true adepts by those who set their whole face and business to deceive others; who profess nothing of the truth except when they most excellently assert themselves to lie; and on the contrary never utter greater lies than when they swear they speak the truth.

These drones have, however, in their first appearance something in common with the true bees or chymical craftsmen, so that from their buzzing or vocal noise you can scarcely tell them apart — that is, so that you can scarcely distinguish, from the mere sound of words, between the philosophical "lapis" of the ancients resounding and these men. But if you mark well, you will nevertheless perceive diagnostic differences by which they stand farther from the true adepts than contrary from contrary, or black from white. To sketch these in some measure (for to paint them all to the life is altogether impossible), for the profit and precaution of the good, snatched by a keen and sagacious wit from these Harpies and Sirens, we shall bring forward and refute a number of species of frauds and drones — partly drawn from the books of others, partly drawn from common report and complaint. From these chapters it will not be difficult to judge and pass sentence on many other inventions of deceivers, which are not yet known even by name, and which are daily brought forth by them for use.

You, whoever you are, benign reader, will gather from what is said what must be said, and from a few things perceive many; and you will cleverly discern the most enduring of all arts, Chymia, from the drones and trifles of impostors. For it is not to be laid to the charge of the most innocent art that it lies exposed to the wrongs of robbers and plunderers, but rather to the unseasonableness, the boldness, and the wickedness of these men — not of those who simply err in the art and have been deceived by others, but of those who assail, attempt, ensnare, and cheat others with the intent to deceive, for their own profit and care, and to the loss of the others. Since this kind of men is most harmful both to the commonwealth and to Chymia, it is above all to be recognized and guarded against.

But most to be blamed are those who confound the whole name of the Chymists, and either equate the most pestilent rogues with upright and learned men who, without harm to any, spend their toil and labor on the noblest of all arts, or on the contrary condemn the harmless with the harmful, the wholesome with the pestiferous, the most learned with the unlearned, and the best with the worst — as if there were no difference between virtue and vice, honor and disgrace. For those who judge thus show themselves stripped of all judgment, and differ little from fools who, when they see themselves or others cut by the edge of a knife, abstain from it all their life, so that they would rather gnaw bones with their teeth, like dogs, than rejoice in a knife. But who will condemn fire and water because those elements sometimes inflict the greatest damage on clamoring men? Who will proscribe all antidotes and the aids of medicines, because they are sometimes not only unhelpful but even deadly, if a dexterous use of them is not applied? It is the part of prudence to discern between good and bad, between lawful and unlawful, between true and false; whoever does otherwise claims for himself the part of an unjust judge.

No one, I think, when he sees his field luxuriant with the finest wheat, will nevertheless destroy and turn into dung the good and useful crops on account of the useless weeds mixed in with the barren oats and darnel; but he will separate the wheat from the chaff and other mixtures as is fitting — by threshing with the hard and winnowing with the light. By these two means he will set apart for the uses of men and for keeping bread what is for the uses of men, from the straw and the chaff. In the same way, in this art there is a great crop of pseudo-chymical drones who, though they are of the worst will and of almost no learning and of most impure motives, yet now and then dress themselves up so magnificently in habit, ornament, and speech that they turn all eyes upon themselves and are taken for true Chymists, even by those who have reached the top of the art. After they have insinuated themselves with princes or other wealthy men, they contemn and make light of all the truly learned — nor are they at length recognized from their deeds, by the learned, for the pestilent rogues they are. Is it then fitting to estimate all who are devoted to Chymia, with those already named, as of one flour? May this be imputed to the art, which should be ascribed not to the true craftsman but to the masked one, not to the good person? By no means, I think.

"But how," you ask, "shall I discern them from the true?" There are those who feign curiosity and live bacchanalian lives; there are those who reek with the foulest vices and outwardly are taken for the best; there are others skilled in the art by experiments and undoubted reasons, who yet are found to be deceivers as well; there are not a few who seem to abound in riches and to enjoy the fruits of the chymical art, yet have gotten their substance by theft or some other fraud, that they may show themselves off to the crowd to be gazed at and admired — which consumed, they change both habit and soil. For they are like owls to those bird-catching fowlers: many precious and costly things — golden and silver vessels, rings, chains, crowds of servants, and above the rest a great many cunning furnaces in the laboratory, furnished with utensils of every most useful kind. With these they lie in wait for other men's gold, which, received, they turn into bodily delights of every sort — and thus, as if by some circulation, they are always fowling for something new.

"How then," you ask, "shall I take care against them?" It is easier to keep oneself whole and untouched by open robbers and thieves than by such smooth-tongued deceivers, the Alchymists properly so called, who, whomever they touch with eyes or hands, are wont to bewitch and waste worse than the Marsi and the Psylli. This is the occasion and the sum of our writing, for which it was undertaken: namely, that I may show forth such drones in their own colors, to be laughed at by all good men, or rather to be pointed at with the finger, that men may abstain and guard themselves from them as from those who carry hay in their horn, and from the black little brothers of Cadmus. Although this province may seem arduous and difficult, since it conciliates to its author rather the envy of the wicked than the favor of the good, yet because it is joined with the honor of the art and the commendation of true craftsmen, if I saw that scarcely anyone, unless it were a Momus, rejected this web, such as it is, it ought not to have been refused.

Many before now have painted the true chymical craftsman well enough — among them Geber and other more recent men, from whose writings a comparison may easily be made and judgment passed on the pseudo-chymist. But since many things are required that pertain to the clear knowledge of the art, and even genuine things are usurped by the false, and besides these the manifold and numberless deceits of their own craft and imposture serve them — where one honesty and truth fails them — hence it was necessary not only to repeat a general recognition of such drones from the doctrine of contraries, but to institute a more special one, by which their deceptive byways and turnings aside from error may be shut in.

Among the rest, Albertus Magnus, in his little book on the errors of sophistical deceptions, more especially dissuades the craftsman from the art, though briefly, when he says: "Be not a deceiver of nations: for he who deceives his brother deceives his own soul and his own body, and will lose God and the world. Likewise beware lest you put your heart in sophistical things: for you will find no profit in them, but rather loss and an evil name, and they will lead you into misery and poverty: for there is not found in the thing what is not in it: for that is all sophistical which is called the first order according to Geber, and it is made false, unless perfect science were more obscure: for there are many in the world desiring one sophistical whitening or reddening rather than the perfect work, and all this because of the length of time: and because it seems to such deceivers that in a few days they can whiten Venus and redden Luna through the whole world, because of the great cupidity they have and the diabolical instigation, they dismiss the true science and perfection. And because a work begun imperfectly must be corrupted in every way, therefore I say that all those sophistications must be corrupted, and all this is certain. Wherefore we ask and also command that no wise man set his intention in sophistical things: but as we have done, so do you also." So much Albertus.

That the true craftsman, therefore, may be helped by the goods of nature, by wit, diligence, reading, operation, books, free will, riches and expenses — and there are commended to him temperance, piety, honesty, vigilance, and varied learning — so, on the contrary, the pseudo-chymist is marked by luxury, impiety, dishonesty, sloth, and ignorance: since those other things cannot be learned, even lightly, without the two fundamental disciplines of mathematics — geometry and arithmetic — known beforehand and possessed, so that Plato admitted no disciple into his school who had not been sufficiently instructed in the mathematical disciplines. Hence he prefixed to his lecture hall this epigram or edict: *let no one ignorant of geometry enter*. And Xenocrates, to the man who, imbued with neither geometry nor astronomy, desired to frequent his school, said: go hence, for you lack the handles and tools of philosophy.

If, then, geometry and arithmetic are necessary to astronomy, and these together to philosophy — namely physics, as it is established — and if physics itself, together with astronomy, can be known to anyone, then chymistry at least, as was said before, is open to the wise: this last is by far more difficult than all the rest. And with what arts and endowments, properly, ought such a craftsman to be furnished before he gives himself to the inquiry of chymistry, you ask? Truly, with manifold ones.

In wit let him be not stupid but docile; not slow but subtle: that amid the possibles and impossibles of nature and art he may discern with unerring judgment, and not always stick too closely to the bare letter. Let him excel in memory and imagination — to which, however, without the discourse of the intellect, he should not attribute or indulge too much. Let judgment, or a patient intellect, give or deny the casting vote on all these things. So much for the goods of the mind. As for the body and fortune, let him be patient of labor, whole and strong in limb, sufficiently furnished with wealth.

But since there is scarcely anyone so degenerate — even the stupidest — who does not attribute and ascribe all things to himself already learned, by a certain indulgence and flattery, since he can pass judgment on his own endowments, not on others', and imagines that another knows no more — indeed far less — than he himself knows: hence from certain tests and diagnostics he will discern his own erudition. Without doubt, whoever wishes and hopes to track out this art from his own doctrine, and not by some tumultuary experimentation of matters, will need the arts of speaking, in whose circuit the chymical art is comprehended: and above all Poetics, which was first introduced for no other subject than chymical allegories and fictions — as we have shown in our *Hieroglyphics* concerning all books and fables. For whoever does not penetrate the kernel of the thing enclosed will never arrive at these, but will always stick in the surface and the literal interpretation, which, even if it were well enough known, yet would not profit unless he also had thoroughly seen all the figures and allegories, or metaphors of speech, which the authors use most often. These are supplied not only by grammar and rhetoric, but also by poetics and the assiduous reading of the philologians, who, busy at least about the subtleties of words, extend their censure through the sayings of all. Besides, logic or dialectic with all its parts must not be hidden from the craftsman, since both the reasonings and the arguments of things are drawn from it and judged by it.

These four arts, then, as if trivial, we say are most necessary to the future true craftsman, as being the bases, as it were, of all the rest that treat of the essences of things.

Now let the pseudo-chymist be considered, who thinks his Herculean labors in tracking out the physical arcanum equal in number to his humors: how much does he excel in these puerile arts? He scarcely knows the Latin tongue itself, so that he seldom uses it, and then only corruptly — so far is he from apprehending the force, use, and metaphors of all words, which, since they are infinite in number, hence he understands little or nothing of the mind of the authors, since he scarcely perceives words in their common sense. But these men are confident enough that they are thoroughly versed in languages, when they can answer in Latin, after a fashion, to one or another word put to them in questioning — thinking that the arcana of the art do not lurk in Roman words but in any vulgar tongue, and therefore that the knowledge of other languages confers little or nothing, nor those arts of speaking. And hence they do not think it is the business of a learned man to know anything of chymical matters, but that these belong to themselves and their fellows alone, the Alchymists, the worst of men. But they are deceived in this, as in all other things. For the truth is to be caught from the consensus of all authors. But that consensus will be perceived by no one unless exercised in the aforesaid languages and arts of speaking; and since these are lacking to the pseudo-chymists, the first and chief instrument is believed to be lacking to them — that is, the hands or handles or ears by which they ought to uncover hidden things and catch what is offered.

Secondly, there are disciplines most necessary to this chymical discipline for understanding the things brought in by the authors for similitude. Such are the aforesaid sciences: geometry, arithmetic, astronomy, and physics — and above all medicine. For from these sciences and arts almost all authors draw their terms of speech, according to the analogies of their inductions, which not being understood, obscurity will remain in the mind of him who tracks out the truth lurking beneath them. For how often have they made mention of the celestial signs, of the motion of Sun and Moon, of eclipses, and the like? How often of prime matter and its reduction, of generation and corruption, of vegetable and animal seeds, and of innumerable things of that sort? Indeed, there is nothing in all of physics or the nature of things, nothing in all of medicine — whether precepts or axioms, cases or effects — that chymistry has not accommodated to itself, drawn from them. Therefore whoever is ignorant of all these will lack the second instrument necessary for grasping the truth of chymistry. But all these are so far from the mind of the pseudo-chymist that he does not even dream of them, much less conceive anything certain or true in his mind about them. Hence the philosophers say that those who approach sacred chymistry without knowledge of these sciences come as asses to hay, not knowing at what they point their snouts; indeed, that they are like blind archers who hope to pierce from afar a tiny bird whose chirping they scarcely hear — but in vain.

Thirdly, besides the sciences already named, comprehended under the circuit of philosophy or medicine, there are still others most useful — indeed quite necessary — to the future chymist. As, the *docimastic* art, which inquires by the magisterium of fire into the mixtures and various colors of minerals and metals — that is, which teaches how to institute the examination of all metals by the cineritium or cupel, by quartation or strong waters, by cementation, antimony, and the like; likewise how their colors or tinctures are made, the so-called smalts, how the potters' works and their vitrifications are done, how the painters' and dyers' divers colors — of which some are corporeal, some as if spiritual, some mixed and compounded of others, some simple. The works of goldsmiths too, and of workers in any metals, ought not to be unknown. These and the like greatly increase the chymist's knowledge and strengthen his judgment, lest he err as easily as another propped up by no knowledge of such things. Of the whole science of men the pseudo-chymist is very seldom solicitous; and if by chance he has seen or known anything of that manual work, yet from defect of discourse he cannot track out its most certain causes. Hence it is as much as if he did not know it. Or if he knows, he transfers it to his own undue practice. And thus there is lacking to him the third instrument, helping his actions and theory.

Fourthly, there remains the consideration of mineral nature, which comes to no one at home. Since it will seem to expedite the craftsman greatly if he undertakes journeys to the cradles of perfect metals and others, that he may see and hear many things under the earth and in those places from the workmen, which he could never have read — and if he had read, yet would not have understood or believed. This contemplation greatly changes the craftsman's wit, that he may understand the natural causes of the generation of metals, of whatever sort, and in what the perfect differ from the imperfect, and in what they agree. This was the cause why the ancients feigned a certain Lynceus of such sharpness of eyes that he could penetrate to the inmost places of metals, and observe where gold and silver lay hid, from which mines, and how it grew — as Aiguille(?) tells from the most ancient histories. For no one else is understood by him, if he is transferred to the true origin of the fiction (since it is not history), but the chymical craftsman, who contemplates metallic natures growing under the earth, and directs according to them the documents of the imitative art. Hence it is also said of him that he last saw the Moon and first on the same day in the sign of Aries — which has befallen few mortals. These words are to be taken not historically (as many falsely think) but allegorically, as declaring a most true and no small part of the art. Such altogether is the chymist; but the pseudo-chymist, who has drawn in with no keen eye any knowledge of metallic generation — unless perhaps a superficial one, which, with the rest absent, will be able to profit him little — knows without doubt that all metals take their origin from quicksilver and sulphur. But meanwhile he does not inquire what sort of quicksilver that is and what sort of sulphur, from which gold is generated. Let him know: they are not these vulgar ones, but metallic, and one educed from the other, with corruptible and separable sulphur set between, as of a father or fiery tinder, which procreates and kindles the metallic sulphur. All which, compared with the aforesaid, denote such a craftsman, as to mind and body, as is fit for the knowledge of the art to fall upon — not that all these things are of the essence and necessity of the art, and learned from another and had by experiment. For then ocular demonstration is more than all these sayings, said or to be said. But our discourse here is of the *inquisition* of the art by the manner of one's own tracking out, not by another's experience. Hence Geber in his Summa, chapter 1: "But know, my dearest son, that he who is ignorant in himself of natural principles is already far removed from our art: since he has no true root upon which to found his intention. And he who knows his natural principles, and all the causes of minerals, yet has not attained the true end and profit of this most hidden art, has yet an easier access to the principles of this art than he upon whose intention the ignorance of this our work falls in the manner aforesaid — and he is little removed from the entrance of the art. But he who truly knows the principles and causes of all minerals and of their generation, which consists in the intention of nature, is indeed little taken away from the complement of that work, without which nevertheless our science cannot be perfected: since art cannot imitate nature in all her works, but imitates her as rightly as it can." So much he — which are each to be understood of the theoretical part of this philosophy, in which the pseudo-chymist knows little or something superficial, and therefore is as if robbed of one eye in this part. But I fear that with the other he sees not much either, which is turned toward practice, and so is blinder still on that side.

But in practice, what operations can he exercise? Truly not philosophical ones, but deceptive ones, and pertaining to the art no more than straw and chaff do to the marrow of wheat. Six hundred are the species of furnaces, vessels, glasses, baths; not fewer the modes of calcinations, distillations, sublimations, fixations, incerations, reverberations, circulations, solutions, descensions, mixtures, coagulations, and the like. Who now shall enumerate all the vegetable, mineral, metallic, saline matters, the sulphurs, bodies, mercuries, spirits? Of all which, since there is a great variety even of simples, perfect — hence when they are mixed among themselves alternately and manifoldly, an immense number of those operations will proceed. From these the pseudo-chymist chooses some, wherein to exercise himself, or to draw out and bring into act his intention. But to him none of them will answer, as Lull(?) says: *Item, unless thou hast the understanding of the proper mode of the work of nature, and settest thyself to distill, and believest thou doest well with it...* [text defective] ...we shall now examine some frauds tried and now made common from among those experiments.

These rogues render to Nature, the mother of all things, this thanks: that they take to themselves and attribute what is hers — as what she created — most falsely claiming for their own works. To whom, without doubt, NATURE, if she could break silence, would answer in this manner:

"Is it not enough for thee, most pernicious drone, that, with hostile calumnies daily committed against the race of men, thou in every way deceivest and circumventest them in all manner of ways? Must thou also bring into act against me an intolerable theft, while thou claimest for thyself my gold concocted in the bowels of the earth, displayest it as thine, and criest it up, and by my generosity silently filched, adoptest it to thyself against my consent and nod — which is prohibited to thee by every right? I confess that I, Nature, can exert my powers and forces both through the earth and in mines beneath, and from the imperfect produce perfect metals: below of my own accord, above by the direction of a prudent craftsman. But thou and thy like are as far from such a craftsman as I require as heaven is from earth. I do not acknowledge thee and thy fellows, but hold you for enemies of mine and of all good men. To the true craftsman I have learned to extend in time the midwife's hand, and to him I bring forth at last golden fruits; but thou and thy fellows, pseudo-chymical drones, do me intimate violence, in that from dung and filth you presume to draw forth my most precious gifts — because it pleases you to try to ape the natural art, or nature made artificial, my vicar: that is, gnats against elephants. That my gift, conceded for the health and utility of men, you abuse in innumerable ways: it would have sufficed thee to return to the crafts neglected by thee, or to other feigned things, rather than to bespatter me with perpetual mud and sprinkle my lovers with gall. For thee a wooden cudgel were fitter, and a laborious block than a book; the burning of trees more decent than the Art of Arts, Chymia. In open operations serve me, not in these hidden ones, which far surpass thy grasp, exceed thy intent, and exclude thy endeavor." So much Nature.

The same pseudo-chymical drone has this, that he never knows that he knows nothing, and perfectly has it known: if a soothsayer sees a soothsayer, it is a marvel if he does not laugh — much more if one pseudo-chymist sees another. For this one thing they know truly: that they know nothing but lies — or certainly nothing. Which is ascribed to Socrates, the wisest, but in a far other mind. In which philosophical riddle they follow Anaxagoras rather, who said he knew this one thing: that he knew nothing. For they interpret it otherwise, and seem to be ignorant of this one false thing: that they are ignorant of all true things. For what is known to them besides what is unknown to all good men — that is, vices and lies? What is less familiar to them than what is most familiar to these — namely, truth and virtue? What the fishermen answered to Homer asking, *what have ye caught?* — this the worst answer to upright men: namely, *ignorance of arcane things*, that is, *opinion of their own science*. For since all other sciences and arts with their craftsmen are easily distinguished from their abuses, hence it comes to pass that their craftsmen are known to all and can be celebrated at the end of the art. Chymia, on the contrary, whose students neither desire to be known nor show to others the end of their science, but remain among themselves — even rejoicing that chymistry is everywhere ill spoken of by all as impossible. Whence true chymia, which does good to many, is yet forsaken by all as an orphan, and whatever the pseudo-chymists commit amiss, she suffers, as the Ilias of evils. But they are of a dog's nature who thus judge of the most innocent Virgin, true Chymia — while they not only tear and rend her with evil-speaking sarcasms, but if any damage has been inflicted on anyone by the pseudo-chymists, ascribe it to her: as if, from weakness of mind or ignorance, they bit the stone with which they were pelted — which, if they do it from impotence of mind or ignorance, is to be pardoned them, as to brutes; if from prejudice and knowledge, we invite them, as other Pyrgopolynices, to the contributions of our Golden Table, of which, if they were spectators, they would without doubt remit much of their conceived fury and put on milder affections.

Now, that I may make this fourth note of the pseudo-chymical drones attested to all, I shall produce from the observation of others various impostures, wiles, and frauds, which the pseudo-chymists have sometimes used or may hereafter use — as a certain Cunradius(?) in a German treatise has heaped together such, whose words, for the most part translated, we shall give: "Dear friend," he says, "do not oblige thy faithless friends. If there flies to thee some Heliochantharus(?) who says he can and will teach thee the art of making silver and gold, give him no faith. For the art is not so easy as one might dream. But if he promises that he will show thee a proof of his gold-making, by which thou mayest see the truth, thou mayest admit him — but so that he do it at his own proper expense. And believe nothing more than what thou seest thyself. Look into his and his fellows' hands, furnaces, instruments, tools, materials and species which he will use. Set no store by his boastful and magnificent words (which they know best), nor by the proud and superb dress of some (which with many needs no caution). If he also swears, and calls God to witness with raised fingers, do not for that alone trust him: for he who has flung away all honesty dares to do anything else. Examine and prove most diligently, by the balance, by water, by fire, by lead, by quartation and antimony, everything, even the least, whatever it be, which he uses — whether it be powder, water, oil, liquors, metals, as silver, copper, lead, etc. — whether and how much gold and silver they contain. Nay, even the wooden rods with which he stirs the work in melting, or in separating by the cupel: for those themselves have sometimes been imbued with a sharp water of solution of silver or gold, so that from their burning and the reduction of the ashes it has more than once been known that such rods hid silver and gold in themselves." So much Cunradius.

But in my judgment, even if all diligence be applied by another, that the craft and imposture of the pseudo-chymist be marked — yet since the evil mind and evil intent in a worthless man knows a thousand ways unknown to another, by which to deceive without any suspicion of guile — it were safer to attempt nothing with him who professes to project gold or silver of his own, or to promise the art of making them in any way. For even if he does not promise another that he will perform anything, yet if he contends that he knows or can make even the least silver or gold, with loss or without profit, it is enough for thee, whoever thou art that wilt lend him thine ears, that he is a deceiver. The cause is this: if he knows any such thing with no profit but rather loss, he nevertheless thinks, if he opens it to another, that the possibility of the art will thereby be seen, and that another will desire to know that same thing for a great price, who perhaps hopes to enjoy it with greater gain. But if he knows it with some profit, he has no need to open it to another, whatever causes he pretends. All those are most vain, and cunningly composed for deceiving men.

Such a rogue — to whom there is not hay but grass in his horn (and therefore to be beware of, denominated from the grass) — some years ago betook himself to the more celebrated cities, where there were many lovers of chymistry, and inquired among them whether there were anyone who knew how, from a mark — that is, eight ounces — of silver, to draw out more than two ounces of gold after solution in gradative water. He himself knew this, he said, but sought a greater profit, since he had it known that the whole of silver could by certain artifices be reduced through gradative waters into gold. Whoever gave faith to this most lying deceiver, as if he knew any such thing (which he the more easily obtained because he appeared in most ornate dress and collared with gold), though he seemed to make little of it, knew it to his own sufficiently great loss — and thus perchance gave occasion to the deceiver to learn only so much the first time from him, for an excellent price paid. By what frivolities he was afterwards befooled in this and other affairs became known through many, from whom I have it.

The Germans say it is a most vile art — as the proverb has it — which cannot cover frauds worthy of the halter. First, then, is to be said of this pseudo-chymical artifice, which, vile through itself, is yet most worthy of a brand or a Samian letter — if not of the Greek perpendicular double-joined.

The same author adds: powders of charcoal, imbued with a water of solution of gold, spread upon materials in the crucible so that a descent as it were is made, do much to this business. And crucibles serving for the melting of metals, with a double and thin upper bottom and a thicker lower one, within which gold is hidden, with a perforated iron instrument above, so that the gold flows out — such deceivers take care to prepare for their uses. And with these authors do not use them dextrously.

These scarabs are wont not only to bring injury and contempt upon the chymical art, but also upon metallic nature itself and natural chymistry, while from furnace to metallic vein they propagate their impostures in this manner: silver and gold dissolved in sharp waters they pour into the heaps and veins of minerals. So granulated silver or gold, shot from a bombard into the passages of mineral veins, that it may stick in those places, shows rich veins and opulent mines.

Nay, gold reduced into powder or filings, by whose benefit iron — though it could not transmute iron into gold, not even the half part of a nail — yet I do not doubt that guile underlay that operation. Such a nail I have held in my own hands, which is kept at Florence in the Grand Duke's cabinet with this inscription: that that transmutation was made in the presence of the Pope himself and the Duke of Florence, by a red water, into which an iron nail was dipped. The cause is this: that if such a mutation were made by a perfect tincture, it could in no way be made through the form of water, by which the heated iron is quenched, before the tincture enters and perfects its operation — which operation cannot be made without great ignition of the iron and long detention in the same, until the impure be segregated from the pure. Besides, that nail which I saw differed nothing, or little, in the thickness of the gold from what the iron part had been. Whence the guile is easily gathered. And if that nail were weighed against an iron nail of the same magnitude exactly, then it would be found that that nail is far weightier than the whole iron one, since gold is twice weightier than iron. There are those who extol this nail among chymical miracles; but if that author be considered, and his deeds and the other circumstances, it will be plain to which party he is to be joined. I judge that of these, of whom mention is made here, the captain and standard-bearer was he — unless the common opinion and public fame deceive me — whose... [text defective] The goldsmith's work is necessary: that consolidations of gold and iron must be made... [text defective] not tin and copper, with gold alone... Hence there is no need that they mix lead with Hungarian gold, and thus as it were make a false Rhenish gold — since that which is true, as are the Rhenish florins, by the same cement and reason becomes Hungarian or fine gold.

40. Conrad proceeds: calcined gold mixed with bole or red earth — this is the *terra Adamica* — from which they then make gold.

41. Bricks imbued in sharp water, in which silver or gold has been dissolved, and dried until they grow green, make a golden vein in mines for the finders.

42. Quicksilver (or rather amalgamated with silver or gold) they make easily coagulate silver or gold in the opus.

43. Luna into Sol they also dye with their sophistical impostures in flux. But when there is separation by the cineritium, then Saturn puts off his yellow garment, and in quartation it is seen that that body is only Luna. And so it is only a tincture of color, not of fixity.

44. Gold moreover they exalt by graduation with Venus, so far that it corresponds to Venus in color. They dissolve filings of this in water not too sharp, to the half part gently (and this with them is *to extract the soul of Venus*), and upon this they put silver to be dissolved, from which they then separate the gold. Afterwards they say that they can, through the soul of Venus, make gold from silver. Of such sophistication we have already made mention above under 2. For this medium that grassy grassator(?) used.

45. There is prepared also both silver and gold, so as to be wholly volatile in fire — by which he tries to prove that his powders contain nothing of silver or gold, since in the bottom no corporeal substance remains. Who would not judge such a relation to be most like the truth? Hear what thou shalt do, if any such thing be offered thee: imbue that same with the *soul of Saturn* and mix with *crocus Martis* (of which they are said to have been... [names] Bragadino, Keevenhiller, Iustenhover, and in our time that Scotsman Wyllcby [identified by the series' research as Edward Kelley — see NOTES], who with his projections showed himself boastfully through nearly all Europe), nothing different from the others declared before, very many not good effects and events of his attesting.

46. Conrad among other things also hands down these: to copper they apply less of lead, which is false(?) — and then in the cupel they separate it, until Saturn vanishes, and of Venus something remains. This they call *Venus fixed in Saturn*.

47. There still remains a pseudo-chymical imposture, not of the least moment, though not the greatest: that certain of them (by what means or ways, that is best known to themselves, provided they could at some time render account of them) — of the most perfect and great universal medicine of the philosophers, or of some special tincture of Luna or Sol, which yet they got from others — boast (though most falsely) that they prepared these most praiseworthy medicines of metals from beginning to end, and can still, if only some thousands of thalers be counted out to them, make them anew. By which lies they have conciliated to themselves great rewards and splendid goods, which, that they may keep them, are still to be coaxed with prayers. But when the stone or tincture (which another made, and which they sometimes, even against conscience, obtained in a marvelous manner) has been consumed — nay often (which is to be lamented) most basely lost — then their art of transmutation too has taken an end.

Whatever Conrad has here related is sufficiently known in fact and experience. We could indeed enumerate very many who have made projections of tinctures in divers places in our time — but few who by erudition or probity of life, and by doing their affairs secretly, have left a name. The cause is that the tinctures of all these were either without a fire(?) and extracted from gold, or, if true, yet not made by their possessors. For if they had been made by them, they would have no causes why they should publicly display them to the vulgar. For those who display them desire to gain by them. They are not, therefore, true philosophers, even though possessors of some particular true tincture may exist. This is the chief cause why true craftsmen, and those who have themselves prepared the perfect work, most rarely appear — and those who appear are for the most part not true.

"I have received" (says Hoghelande(?)) "from a certain man of mine of this our age: that an Italian monk, confident in this art, renounced his religion and his order and came into Germany, that he might live more freely — having detested the cowl and the asperities of monasteries. But while he bore himself incautiously, two men insinuated themselves into his closest familiarity, by whom afterwards in a certain forest, on a journey, with the greatest wickedness he was cruelly murdered. Of whom the one showed the powder found upon the monk, as his own, to divers princes and magnates, and with a thousand impostures under the false name of science deluded men. What sort of man he was became known to many — namely he whose half-golden nail is shown elsewhere, as we mentioned above under no. 18."

After these things Conrad tells a marvelous fable of the pseudo-chymists, which is thus:

50. If thou hast suffered thyself to be persuaded by their boastings, that from the urine of a boy and a girl of 7, 8, 9, or 10 years (who must be nourished at least with bread and wine all that time of collection) and from the best white wine thou wouldst and shouldst make the *illuminatum(?) Philosophorum* (which, according to their narration, must be cocted to the utmost...) — who with the Arcanum of human blood, seasoned with rose water and the best wine in a silver spoon, as they say, must be fed artfully; and when in his time he utters a voice, he must be subtly drawn out from the nitre(?) lest he fall back again into the depth and perish — I say, if thou hast been brought to believe these things, that they bring forth in glass, as if made human, little bones of ivory, and persuade thee, O fool, that a homunculus is present — though indeed through some negligence the little bones alone remain, which attest his presence — it is ridiculous what they tell: that he is a contracted man of short stature, of a palm's height, clad in a purple ankle-length toga of velvet, sleeping in a tiny closed litter, sitting at table at meals, eating from red and silver vessels, speaking most wisely of all things, of subtle discourse — and in fact proving that he is the son of the philosophers. And what end is there of such triflers' trifles, which were handled as jokes a few years ago? So much Conrad.

It is no marvel that these fables and innumerable things of this sort have been devised and believed by brutish men, since very often in the books of the philosophers mention is made of conception, of the embryo, of nourishing with one's own blood, of generation, nativity, and education until it arrives at mature age — if the letter alone is respected. But whoever understands such things thus manifestly shows that he understands nothing of the matter. For scarcely anything of human or natural or artificial action can be devised to which the philosophers' work is not likened, in part or in whole — and therefore it is not to be imagined either that it is nothing, or that all such things are as they are said to be. Those are most arcane things, reserved for the sons of the doctrine for discerning; but for the rest, such as the pseudo-chymists are, wrapped up, so that they can scarcely extricate themselves from them.

With the aforesaid fable this one agrees, which lately a most pestilent deceiver and pseudo-chymist persuaded certain simple folk of the vulgar: namely, that he knew how to be in the *Mons Veneris* (Mountain of Venus), in imitation of that Tannhäuser, and had there learned many arcana which he knew in chymics, and had seen very many marvels; among the rest a certain old companion, comrade of his bed(?), to whom he had so long stuck, glued limb to limb(?) — from which mountain, by a marvelous art, abstaining from food and drink for many days, he at last emerged. Which rogue, after he had flown over a great shore(?) of Germany with his wiles and lies (now and then changing homeland and name), to the loss of many, and circumvented them — were worthy to be dragged down into some subterranean Cyprus(?) — not of Venus, but befitting such a criminal companion — where he might expiate with late and serious repentance all the impostures and frauds he perpetrated, nor hereafter most falsely impose upon others (lying about a noble thing, and a chymical one).

51. Last, Conrad adds concerning deceits about drinkable gold in this manner: "Of the imposture which the pseudo-chymists practice with their false drinkable gold, and of other sophistications, many things were still to be said. But what is to be done? There are so many kinds of nefarious iniquity that it is not possible to know them all, much less to tell them. To what good did that Penotus(?) with his sophistical drinkable gold — from oil of vitriol, seed of cloves, fennel(?) and vitriol — come, a few years ago at Prague, may be seen from his books on chymical medicines. Even at this day certain men should consider their honesty and be silent with their sophistical drinkable gold, if they had not that better thing(?) — while they mix gold leaves with salt of tartar, and having poured spirit of wine over them set it alight several times, and then with spirit of wine extract the redness thence — not (as they falsely opine and assert) from the gold, but from the salt of tartar — and fraudulently proclaim that red extract their drinkable gold. Such men deceit accompanies in every part." So far Conrad.

To which we shall add one and another mode of deception of the pseudo-chymists from other authors.

Anselm Boethius(?) in his book *De Gemmis*, where he treats of the magnet stone...: "Quicksilver," he says, "to draw gold and silver to itself has long been known to the spagyrists and pyrotechnists. Nay, the chymical impostors defraud many by the knowledge of this thing. For while they assert they can transmute part of cinnabar into silver, they burn it with silver leaf, calling it *to cement*. Then the quicksilver, which lurks in the cinnabar, allures to itself some portion of silver, which, the work finished, being returned, they think — as I said — some part of the cinnabar transmuted. But they are deceived, since it has left the silver leaf (the fire consuming something) rather than extracted it." Of this imposture mention is also made by Dionysius Zacharius(?) and Rubeus(?), and many others.

With like guile (says Boethius in the same place) they deceive, while they put prepared mercury upon a golden paten and pour spirit of wine over it. The spirit being kindled and extinguished, first the mercury, somewhat heated, draws to itself not a little gold from the paten, with no visible spot or stain left in the paten; which afterwards, in the same form in which it was before, being heated, returns as much gold as it drew. Which, however, the chymists deny to have drawn from the gold, asserting it generated from some portion of mercury turned into gold. But that is false, since as much left the gold as the mercury drew. So much Boethius.

Likewise Crollius in his *Basilica*, after the description of drinkable gold: "In this place I would candidly warn the candid reader, admonished, concerning certain impostures. That powder of gold could by malicious long-handed(?) agents be imposed in a rare manner upon the simple and incautious: communicated(?) once to a certain bark-philosopher(?) under sincerity and pharisaical piety, most dexterously deceiving, covering a hypocritical and serpentine cunning — who, after he had learned this powder (after the admixture of triturated sulphur, by the admonition of striking(?)), unknown in face, mixed it with his impostures, and with the loss of others and his own gain attempted the multiplication of gold among many. The same, after he had understood the way of *Luna into the vulgar aqua separatoria(?) dissolved, and with vulgar water made salt, to draw down an infusible(?) powder, which, thrice or four times edulcorated, afterwards melts at fire in the crucible, and after fusion returns in the form of horn — this horn-Luna, unknown, easily melting at candle and fire and cuttable with a knife, mixed with other minerals and lead — and by this notable and damnable imposture he afterwards lied, before many unwary, upon examination, a transmutation of Saturn into Luna." So far Crollius.

Not unlike these we add an imposture used by some, from the relation of a certain friend: they take fine Luna (silver), and to it melted they add as much common triturated sulphur as it can take, stirring ever with iron. This silver is like *minera argenti glast(?) or vitrea* — called glassy — or rather leaden, so that it can scarcely be discerned from it. Therefore the pseudo-chymists call it *lead* whose sulphureity has been taken away, and with a certain sophistical powder, instead of a silver-producing tincture, they most easily turn it, sprinkled upon it, into good silver — the fire burning the sulphur.

But to these impostors at last an end will come — as also to their impostures, which we have under this fourth note copiously enough recounted, not from our own observation or wit, but from others', as we have cited the authors themselves.

But someone might interpret "Saturn" and "Venus" thus — as expressing more the loss than the profit of the commonwealth or of chymistry: that anyone who before was ignorant of their use may now first learn and exercise it. But those who so judge are too subtle and acute by their own judgment, and fear what is not to be feared. We have written these things for the caution of good men and lovers of chymia — not for the abuse of evil men and pseudo-chymists, who, if they have otherwise abused them beforehand (as yet I scarcely opine, unless they be by their nature too prone to impostures and exercised in that practice), led rather by the reading of this treatise, will find causes why they should abstain from them, and refer themselves to the order of true chymists rather than — as the greater part(?) ... For neither are vices taxed by theologians viva voce or in writings that they may be taught or inculcated — though some of those who have heard or read them may afterwards perpetrate many things not before attempted. Nor are the actions of crimes and misdeeds handed down by jurists that the wicked may bestow pains upon them. Much less do physicians treat of poisons that anyone may be infected or perish by them — but that the infected may know by what poisons they are afflicted, that, the signs of each being discerned, its own infallible remedy may be at hand for each. So we have collected the wiles, impostures, and deceptions of the pseudo-chymists, and the divers notes of each, that, known by signs, they may be shunned by all good men, and the more easily distinguished by the vulgar from true chymists: lest the best art be confounded with the worst drone and sophistication, and the most learned men, excelling in honesty, with nefarious rogues — as we warned before — but to each his own praise or blame, his own reward or punishment, his honor and disgrace, be in the open. He too will do wrong to us and to his own judgment who ascribes the same to him who taxes vices and confounds others' falsehoods and refutes them: for then prophets and apostles, preachers and all judges, would be obnoxious to the same crimes and impostures which they tax — which, as it is unworthy of them, so is it far alien from the discourse of a rational man.
