DEDICATION

To Don Pietro Carrera, priest of Militello in Sicily, 1571-1647.

He was the parish priest of a small Sicilian hill town, and in 1617 he did
two things no one in Militello had done before: he had a book printed there —
the first book ever printed in Militello — and in it he set down everything
known about the game of chess, from its invention to its finest endings. He
died in 1647, thirty years after his book appeared. (Beyond these bare facts
his life is not documented in the materials used for this edition, and none
is invented here.)

His work is vital, and the proof is not praise but arithmetic. In 1822 the
English chess master William Lewis translated Carrera into English — but in
his preface Lewis wrote plainly that he had "not thought fit to translate
the whole of the original," and named what he threw away: "the chapter on
the origin of the game; an account of a new game on a board of ninety-six
squares, played with the chess-men and two additional pieces." What the
practical player of 1822 had no use for was exactly this: Carrera's proof
that chess was invented by Palamedes at the siege of Troy, and Carrera's own
invention — two new pieces, the Champion and the Centaur, on a board enlarged
to eighty squares — three hundred years before Capablanca dreamed of adding
pieces to chess. These chapters have waited more than two centuries for an
English voice. This edition gives them one, translated fresh from Carrera's
Italian, never borrowing a line from Lewis.

Where to go next: the whole 1617 Italian volume stands free on the Internet
Archive (identifier bub_gb_-fRYAAAAYAAJ); Lewis's 1822 Chess for Beginners
covers the practical chapters — openings, endings, and checkmates — that he
did translate.

BOOK I, CHAPTER I
Of the Inventor of the Game of Chess

[The printed Argument of the chapter, by Dr. Mario Tortelli:]
It is proved that Palamedes was the inventor of the game of chess. The names
of the game are declared, their derivation, and many significations drawn
from it. Its excellence is discussed, and the diverse ways of playing it. It
is examined whether knowledge of the game is bounded. The customs of players
in the game are treated. The combinations of the chessmen among themselves,
and among the squares of the board, are explained. The ancient players and
the most celebrated modern ones are reported, and those who have written of
the game.

The antiquity of time, and the small care men have taken, have caused the
origins of things — though they had a certain beginning — to come down to us
uncertain and doubtful. Hence we do not know whom to believe among those who
write of diverse things in different ways. That same uncertainty which has
befallen many discoveries in the sciences, the arts, and other matters, we see
prevailing today, and in the highest degree, around the invention of the game
of chess.

Ruy Lopez, in chapter 3 of his book on chess, gives it to be understood that
he could prove the game came out of Sicily — yet he does not prove it,
because he cannot, that opinion being new and his alone, born only of his
excessive love for his homeland. He likewise rejects the view of those who
would have it invented by the Moors; the reason he gives is that the game is
older than the Moors — a saying that does not please studious minds, for the
Moors are most ancient, as is read in diverse authors. Indeed I will add that
they drew that very name from their mothers' wombs, for "Moor" means black,
and there is no doubting the word is corrupted from the Latin. Yet I do not
grant that they were the inventors, for I see no one who takes up and defends
their cause; one hears only a false outcry resting on the uncertainty of the
vulgar.

Damiano, the Spanish writer, in the work he composed on chess, reports that
some make King Xerxes its inventor; and Polydore Vergil — followed by Giacomo
da Cevoli and Gregorio Ducchi, in book 2, chapter 13 of *De inventoribus
rerum* — with no authority behind him attributes the invention to Xerxes, a
philologist also called by others Philometor, for the sake (as he says) of
correcting a cruel king, whom the said Giacomo takes to be Evilmerodach, king
of Babylon, son of Nebuchadnezzar. In support of these views some Spaniards
adduce that *axedrez*, the Spanish word meaning the chessboard, is derived,
though corruptly, from the name of Xerxes. Neither opinion is embraced, for
in Homer we have the game of chess played by the suitors of Penelope — and
from that time to King Evilmerodach ran some hundreds of years after, and
still more to King Xerxes, as is easily gathered from the writers.

Others, together with Damiano, would have it that Lydus and Tyrrhenus,
brothers of the Greek nation, pressed by famine to pass the time, devised the
invention of the game — a sentence I have never seen written nor approved by
any worthy author. I take it to be founded, though weakly, on that passage of
Herodotus in his first book, where he tells how in the time of Atys, son of
King Manes, Lydia, afflicted by famine for lack of food, gave itself to
inventing new things; for the Lydians were then the inventors of dice, of
ball, and of other games. Indeed, according to Hesychius, the Romans took
that name LUDI from the Lydians, changing the *y* to *u*, as the Latins do
with Greek names. Yet Lydus and Tyrrhenus were sons of Atys — though
Herodotus has no contradictors in this, as he has in some things — and still
it would not follow that the Lydians were the inventors of chess. Besides,
by the reckoners of time, the brothers Lydus and Tyrrhenus lived many years
after the Trojan war, from which we draw the origin of the game.

Those who make the Chaldeans the inventors — among them Diomedes, after
Giacomo da Cevoli — are not followed; indeed I find no author who leans on
it. Others affirm the game was invented by the Egyptians, among them Jodocus
Damhouder, as he says in his *Parenesi Cristiane* in the admonition he makes
to soldiers; yet this opinion does not stand, and it is rejected by Celio
Rodicino in book 10, chapter 14 of the *Antiche lettioni*.

Two other opinions remain. One is that its inventor was Palamedes the Greek,
at the beginning of the Trojan war; the other, that it was invented by those
rival princes in the love of Penelope, some years after the said war. These
two sentences, being joined the one with the other, I will describe and
expound together, as far as my weak strength and the reasons that occur to me
allow.

Homer in the first book of the *Odyssey* says thus:

   He found the proud suitors, who then
   Before the doorway, seated at their ease,
   Were delighting their spirit at chess.

The Latin version of Giovanni Spondano has it likewise:

   *Invenit autem Procos superbos, qui quidem tum
   Calculis ante ianuam animum oblectabant.*

Athenaeus, in the first book of the *Deipnosophists*, discoursing on this
passage of Homer, says that Apion the Alexandrian told how he had heard from
Cteson of the isle of Ithaca what game of chess it was that Penelope's
suitors played, affirming it to be this. They had taken a hundred and eight
chessmen — for that was the number of the suitors — and made of them two
opposing and equal squadrons, so that each completed the number of
fifty-four; in the middle of the board was seen a certain space of empty
squares, in which they placed one chessman called by them by the name
Penelope. This was the target of the chessmen of the one and of the other
squadron. He whose turn it was to play directed one of his chessmen toward
Penelope; and if by chance he struck her and moved her forward from her
place, he set that same chessman which had done the deed in Penelope's
square. Then Penelope's chessman was put back anew in its own lodging, where
it sat before, and the same player moved another chessman against Penelope;
and if he struck her without touching any of the other chessmen, he was
declared victor, and had excellent hope in the marriage of Penelope. At this
game Eurymachus won many times, and so with great hope he promised himself
her nuptials.

All that Athenaeus reports of that manner of game — with his good leave —
may be doubted. For though the reader gives faith, as is due, to the words
of so grave and worthy a writer, he still hangs ambiguous over the report of
that Apion the Alexandrian, founded on the other report of Cteson the
Ithacan — men known by name alone. Experience shows us that in our own day
many tales come even from the mouths of grave men, depending yet on others'
sayings, which are manifest lies. But granting the report most true that the
suitors were the inventors of the game, we argue instead that they took their
game from the game of chess invented earlier by Palamedes, as we shall say at
Aulis — for in it one sees the imitation of check, which is that assault
given to Penelope's chessman; one sees the halting in the place of the enemy
chessman, as is seen in removing Penelope's chessman and setting the other
elsewhere in her square; there is also the representation of the Queen in
Penelope's chessman, though on a different principle.

It is plain that witty men, after the first invention of a thing, adding or
changing on that same foundation, invent other inventions — as is known to
have happened with the invention of the game of Goose in our fathers' time.
That game, having been invented in Florence and mightily pleasing Francesco
de' Medici, then Grand Duke of Tuscany, he sent it to the Majesty of King
Philip II in Spain, where, once published, it gave good wits matter to invent
others little different from the first, among them the game called *Courted
Philosophy*, invented by the Spaniard Alonso de Barros. It need not seem
difficult that in the course of twenty years — which is what runs from the
beginning of the Greeks' enterprise to the suitors — knowledge of the game
should have spread through those lands, there being no great distance
between Ithaca and Aulis.

If it is objected to me that the suitors would have exposed themselves to
that same enterprise in the game of chess, had chess existed before, I answer
that the suit, which was between a hundred and eight, could not be decided
with the game of chess; and perhaps not all knew the game, or not all
delighted in it, so they formed one after their own fashion which would
satisfy each. I will say too that they did it to bend Penelope herself, for
women take great pleasure in inventions devised for love of them — for which
reasons the game of chess did not serve the turn.

There remains for me to answer another objection that may be drawn from
Athenaeus's words in that same passage cited above, where he says the suitors
learned this manner of game neither from Diodorus the Megalopolitan, nor
from Theostenes, nor even from Leo — who, as Phanias writes, was in that game
truly invincible — hinting that these players lived in the suitors' time. We
grant it all, for it does not destroy Palamedes' invention but approves it:
we shall say the game he invented pleased so well that at once there arose
from it most perfect players. This is confirmed by the authority of Pausanias
in book 10, who tells how one of the two Ajaxes watched Palamedes playing
chess with Thersites; and Euripides too adduces it in the tragedy *Iphigenia
in Aulis*, saying that Ajax and Protesilaus played at chess, and with them
were the other Ajax, Palamedes, and Diomedes, while round about stood
Meriones, Ulysses, and Nireus. Hence we must affirm that the game, from its
birth, was taken into use with the greatest profit of the players.

Palamedes, then, according to the common opinion which I follow, finding
himself at Aulis — a city and port of Boeotia — with the army of the Greeks,
which was detained there awaiting fair weather to sail to the harm of the
Trojans, invented the game of chess. Of this Philostratus bears witness in
the *Heroica*, on Palamedes, reasoning of him in these words:

   "For he — I speak of Palamedes — I will say it,
   Not without God's help,
   Drove far off hunger,
   And those who sat in dry encampment,
   Where the sea murmurs, to flee idleness
   He induced, and showed them
   The game of chess,
   Which he had invented,
   For the merry pleasure of the soul."

Pausanias in book 10: thence the other Ajax stands watching Palamedes and
Thersites, who were playing at chess — of which same game Palamedes was the
inventor. He says the same again in book 1: that Palamedes dedicated the
chess he had invented in the temple of Fortune. These authorities of the
Greek writers — as I have interpreted them, so too Andrea Tiraquello the
jurist, a man adorned with all letters, understands them in the commentary
he makes on Alexander of Alexandria's *Genial Days*, book 3, chapter 11 — and
other writers likewise.

The most learned Matthew Rader, in his commentaries on Martial, book 14,
with others together, interprets Philostratus's authority differently: he
would have it that in that place he says Palamedes invented the game of
*Tali* [knucklebones]; indeed from that same passage of Sophocles he declares
the same, and by Pausanias he understands the invention of the Tali and of
dice. I take it he never condescended to weigh Philostratus's words, which
in Federico Morello's translation sound thus: *Ceterum in Aulide
existentes...* — he speaks of Palamedes — *...ludum non modo non ibidem,
verum solertem ac studio dignum* — "a game not only not [vulgar] there, but
skillful and worthy of study." To the game of Tali and dice those words —
*a game skillful and worthy of study* — do not suit; they suit the game of
chess at full. And Philostratus himself decides the question. Pausanias's
authority in book 10, by which Palamedes and Thersites are adduced, watched
at play by Ajax, gives us no light of proof in favour of dice, because the
use of the dice game ordinarily admits a greater number of persons than two
— so that it may be set down as a maxim that where there is dicing, there is
a multitude of players. The other authority of the same writer in book 1,
where we say Palamedes dedicated the chess in the temple of Fortune, is not
without purpose: for the inventor, wishing to signify that chess was not
subject to fortune, chose to set it as if for ensigns in her house — after
the likeness of those captains who, not content with having beaten the enemy
in his own country, in sign of victory and for the eternal memory of
posterity raise a pyramid there.

The last verse of Sophocles' authority, which is much like Homer's third
cited above, answers rather to the sense of chess than of dice: for the
gladness and pleasure found in the game of chess are not known in that of
dice, wherein displeasure and anguish reign.

Leaving these considerations, let us recur to the divine Plato, who in the
*Phaedrus*, adducing Theuth the sage of Egypt as inventor of the game of Tali
and dice, reasons thus: "Theuth's name is Theuth; he first of all invented
number, the art of reckoning, geometry, astronomy, and the games of Tali and
dice, and letters besides." Herodotus too in his first book says the Lydians
were the first to invent the game of dice, and other games — but not the
game of Tali. Hence from these two most weighty authors, though they differ
between themselves, we have it that another, and not Palamedes, was the
inventor of the Tali and of dice; so the sayings of Philostratus, of
Sophocles, of Pausanias fit the understanding of the game of chess. And since
we have chanced on mention of the game of Tali, for the greater clearness of
this discourse it has seemed good to speak of it briefly.

The game of Tali, which today among us is lost, was used in antiquity with
four dice, each of which had four faces full of number and two empty, for the
Two and the Five were lacking. Their shape was not wholly round, but much
partaking of roundness; nor was it seen completely square like that of dice,
but such as we know to be the shape of the knuckle — the joint of the hinder
feet of certain animals, as the lion — for the game of Tali took its name
from the shape of this knuckle, which is called *talus*. It befell the
players at times, though rarely, that the talus settled on one of the two
empty faces, and those were called by the Greeks *antemne*. Then, to escape
the frauds of those who doctored the talus to their liking, the game was
played by putting the tali in a small vessel of wood or other matter, called
by the Latins *turricula* and by the Greeks *pyrgus*, and casting them on the
game table — which we see observed by many even in our times in the game of
dice, for the sake of that same cheating which is vulgarly called *sficcare*
[loading the cast]. Of the manner of the game of Tali, and of all that
belongs to winning, it is not my thought to reason.

I take it we have proved enough that the origin of the game of chess depends
on Palamedes; yet for greater support we will adduce the authorities of
celebrated men who understood the same as we do concerning the invention of
the game. Saint Gregory Nazianzen in his third oration attributes the
invention of chess to Palamedes; Aurelius Cassiodorus, called the Great, in
book 8, epistle 31, hints the same; Celio Rodicino in book 10, chapter 14 of
the *Ancient Lessons* affirms the same, as does Alexander of Alexandria in
book 3, chapter 11 of the *Genial Days*, and together with him Andrea
Tiraquello commenting on that same place; Servius on the second book of
Virgil's *Aeneid* at that place "...Palamedis..."; Torquato Tasso in the
*Romeo*; Theodor Zwinger in the *Theatre of Human Life*, volume 18; Johann
Fungerius in his *Dictionary of Three Languages*; Ambrogio Calepino in the
*Dictionarium* — "Palamedes" — of his vocabulary; Cesare Rao in his twentieth
invective; Innocenzo Ringuieri in book 10 of games, chapter 98; Bartolomeo
Arnigio in the *Veglia sesta*; Caccialupo, and Paride del Pozzo, as Giovanni
Parladorus reports in book 11, chapter 7; and Giovan Battista Baiardo on
Giulio Claro in book 5 of the *Criminal Practice*, section *Ludus*.

And because, as I have said, the game was invented at Aulis before the Trojan
war, we do not receive those reasons others adduce — that Palamedes by the
Queen of the game meant Penthesilea, by the black squadron the camp of
Memnon, by the chess Knight the Trojan horse, and many other considerations
founded on the progress and completion of the Trojan war — for they repugn
the truth of history.

There rises another opposition, held by Celio Calcagnino, one of the greatest
scholars of his time, in the discourse he makes on the game. It is this: that
the game of chess was not known to the ancients — an opinion followed by most
learned men, alleging that those memories of chess found in Martial, in
Seneca, and in others are to be understood of the chess of the *Riga*
[draughts-board], and not of the chess of our game of which we reason. That
this is not true I shall prove with diverse and well-grounded reasons, and
also with the authority of most weighty men.

Calcagnino — because he lacked the full knowledge of the game, without which
he could not observe and examine at full its properties, which lie scattered
through the writers — [went astray]. First it will be needful to unfold the
knowledge of the games of the Riga, that all may remain made clear.

The Riga among the ancients was of three kinds. One, as Celio Rodicino
affirms in book 10, chapter 27 of the *Ancient Lessons*, on the authority of
Eustathius, contained sixty chessmen, part white and part black — and this
was called *Grammismus*, *Diagrammismus*, and *Gramma*, which are the same as
*linea* [line], though *gramma* signified every riga. Julius Pollux confirms
this in his *Dictionary*, reasoning thus: "You say white stones, and as many
black." Cicero in the *Hortensius*: *Itane ubi concedo, quod in duodecim
scrupis nolim, ut calculum reducas, si te alicuius dati poeniteat* ["Do I
grant, then, that on the twelve lines I would not have you take back a man,
if you repent of some move?"]. Quintilian in book 10, chapter 10: *An vero
scenda in lusu duodecim scriptorum cum prior calculum promovisset* ["Or
indeed, on the board of the twelve lines, when the first had advanced his
man..."].

This riga, as the figure that follows shows, is divided into five lines cut
lengthwise, and as many across, with two other lines crossing it and ending
in the corners. In it are seen four other small lines, which cut across the
four squares of the riga, and each of them embraces three houses; each of the
greater lines, both straight and oblique, has five, and on these sit the
stones of the game — except the middle line, which has four places filled
with stones, because the house in the middle of it remains empty. This middle
line was called by the ancients *Sacra* [the Sacred], for it was not lawful
without great necessity to move the stones from it — whence was born the
proverb *Sacram movere lapidem* ["to move the sacred stone"], which means to
compel a man to an act of extreme necessity. Theocritus in the 6th Idyll: "And
from the line he takes back the stone."

The stones which reach the last line, according to Isidore as Giuseppe
Scaligero reports on the 6th book of Varro, were called *Inciti*. Here it
seems needful to me to warn that this riga is different from that game the
Sicilians call *Marrella* and the Spaniards *Juego de las damas* [draughts],
though the two games have some things in common; of it I see no author makes
mention. The third kind of riga, as appears by the figure here set down, was
the same [game] still played by low men with the same manner of play, called
the Riga of Three. Ovid in book 3, *De tristibus*:

   *Parva sedet ternis instructa tabella lapillis,
   qua vicisse est continuasse suos.*

And in book 3, *De arte amandi*:

   *Talia tabella capit ternos utrinque lapillos,
   qua vicisse est continuasse suos.*

From these species of rigae is perhaps derived the Riga of Nine, which today
is more in use than the others, and it is wont to go on the reverse of the
chessboard; of it, for all I have seen, I find no memory among the ancients.

Because these three kinds of riga were played with small stones, the Latins
called their chessmen *calculi*, *scrupi*, *lapilli* — which are of one
signification. The ancients, reasoning of these games, always described them
with the bare names of stones; to which plainly do not suit names of
soldiers, and descriptions of battles — which is proper to the game of chess.
Therefore those writers who, treating of the game, liken it to a war, and
the chessmen to fighters, meant our game of chess, and none of those of the
riga.

Ovid in book 3 of the *Art of Love* makes the game of chess different from
that of the Riga of Twelve, and treats of them separately, in these verses:

   *Quantaque non solitis latronum praelia ludat,
   unus cum gemino calculus hoste perit.*

["And how he plays the soldiers' battles in unwonted wise; one man perishes
with a double foe."] And the warrior, caught by his own [man] without a
comrade in arms, seldom runs his begun work back again. The properties of the
chessmen are plainly expounded. Then follows, for the Riga of Twelve, this
distich:

   *Est genus in totidem tenui ratione redactum
   spicula, quot menses lubricus annus habet.*

["There is a kind reduced by slender reckoning to as many points as the
slippery year has months."]

Julius Pollux in his *Dictionary*, in the treatise of games, says thus:
"That game which is called of many chessmen is a game of *petteia* [draughts];
it embraces the houses sitting in the lines." That this sense belongs more to
the game of chess than to the riga, Pollux himself shows with these words
that follow: "Next to this game — that is, of chess — comes the *grammismos*
and *diagrammismos*, which game is called by some 'the lines.'" The game to
which the grammismos comes next cannot be understood as the Riga of Twelve,
nor yet the Riga of Three: for if it were so, Pollux would have said that
game came next to the grammismos. So, there being no other rigae, we must
understand the game of chess, to which the grammismos draws near. Nor does it
contradict this that the grammismos has the greater number of chessmen: for
as a riga-game it lacked those subtleties which are in the game of chess,
and it might well have some qualities partaking of the game of chess —
seeing that to this hour I have found in no one the manner of playing the
grammismos.

And because the ancient writers called the game of chess *ludus
latrunculorum*, or *latronum*, and the chessmen *latrunculi* and *latrones* —
by which some Latins, ill-led, have called the chessmen *furunculi*,
judging *furunculi* to import the same as *latrunculi*, that is, little
thieves — I say that those soldiers who served in war for pay were called
*latrones*; hence, according to Varro in the sixth book of the Latin tongue,
they were so named as *latrones a latere*, because they stood at the king's
side — indeed *laterones* and *laterunculi*, ancient voices, are the same as
*latrones* and *latrunculi*. Plautus in the comedy of the Braggart Soldier:
*Nam rex Seleucus me opere oravit maximo, ut sibi latrones cogerem et
conscriberem* ["For King Seleucus begged me with the greatest earnestness to
levy and enlist soldiers for him"]. Ennius: *Fortunamque, quas ceperunt
latrones, inter se memorare* ["and to tell among themselves the fortune
which the soldiers took"]. Hence by *ludus latrunculorum* and *latronum* is
not meant a game of robbers, but a game of soldiers — which name suits no
game of the riga.

Not content with the above reasons, I will add the opinions of many who by
*ludus latrunculorum* or *latronum* understood the game of chess. Domizio
Brusonio, in his examples on certain places of Martial and Seneca, understands
chess. Raffael Volterrano in the twenty-ninth book of the *Commentarii urbani*
on that verse of Martial, *Insidiosorum si ludis bella latronum*, and also on
Seneca in those words of epistle 106, *Latrunculis ludimus*. Giuseppe Passi
in his *Mostruosa fucina* agrees with the same opinion, on the said verse of
Martial, and also on Ovid. Giovan Maria Cattaneo, commentator of Pliny the
younger, in book 7 of the epistles, in the epistle to Geminius, and on that
verse of Ovid in the second *De arte amandi*, *Sive latrocinii sub imagine
calculus latet*, and on the said place of Martial. Pietro Galesini in his
*Tesoro* on Martial and Ovid. Giovan Ravilio in the *Officina* on Martial.
Bartolomeo Merula, commentator of Ovid in the *Art of Love*, on the same in
book 3. Theodosio Trebellio on Lucan's verses to Piso, which he attributes to
Ovid: *Callidiore modo tabula variatur aperta*. Niccolò Perotto on Martial.
The *Tesoro* of the Latin tongue on Seneca, Martial, and Ovid. Andrea
Tiraquello on Martial, and on that verse of Ovid in book 3 *De arte amandi*,
*Quantaque non solitis latronum praelia ludat*. Alessandro d'Alessandro in
book 3, chapter 11 of the *Genial Days* approves it among the ancients with
these words: *Ludus calculorum veteribus praecipuus fuisse traditur, qui et
latrones dicti* — and he is followed by Cesare Rao in his twentieth
invective: "Noble and ingenious is the game of chess, and by men and by women
in ancient times was it esteemed." Marcello Donato, Count of Ponzano, in the
elucidations he makes on Flavio Vopisco, in the *Probus*, on those words,
*Nam cum in quodam convivio tuo ad latrunculos luderetur, ausus ipse deinde
Imperator exisset*, brings back the game of chess to the ancients, chasing
away the contrary opinions. I could have many more of the same sentence,
whom I pass over to make passage to greater proofs.

The Riga of Twelve is not only of base consideration, as Ovid tells in the
3rd *De arte amandi* — *Est genus in totidem tenui ratione redactum spicula,
quot menses lubricus annus habet* — but is practised for the most part by
peasants, on stones in the middle of the streets. Hence it is not to be
believed that famous writers would give the praise of an unworthy and abject
game to men illustrious for nobility, for arms, and for letters. The game of
chess is far from this baseness; therefore the noblest cavaliers who profess
the game have held it their glory — I speak of those eminent in knowledge of
the game. Read Lucan's *Panegyric to Piso*, how he celebrates it. Petronius
Arbiter, who lived in Nero's empire, would not have called the riga a noble
game against Ovid, who said it of base consideration — so that in that verse
of his in the *Satyricon*, *Cale... in tabula nobile avat opus* ["...on the
noble board..."], the game of chess will without difficulty be understood.
Seneca in book 18, epistle 106, by those words *Latrunculis ludimus, in
supervacuis subtilitas teritur* ["We play at soldiers; subtlety is worn away
on superfluities"], clearly shows the game of chess, in which there is a
subtlety that is not in the riga.

If [Calcagnino] means the riga and not chess — I will say anew that he
scarcely knew the game of chess, and less that of the riga; for we know that
in the riga no such wariness is seen that from it one could learn any manner
of military discipline, as is amply seen in the game of chess.

Ovid in the 3rd *De tristibus* expounds certain properties of chess which
answer ill to the riga, in these verses: *Ut mage velle sequi sciat, revocare
priorem; si cadat, ut tuto fugiens incomitatus eat* ["That he may know rather
to will to follow, to call back the former; if he fall, that fleeing he may
go safe and unattended"]. Following and fleeing are proper to the game of
chess: for the chessmen of the riga, by its conditions, follow but do not
flee — that is, they cannot draw back — otherwise we should understand that
one of them had arrived at Queen, or that among the ancients the riga-men
could go forward and backward at liberty, as children sometimes play them.
Ovid in the third of the *Art of Love* openly teaches us the effect of the
double check in that verse, *Unus cum gemino calculus hoste perit*; and in
the 3rd *De tristibus* more clearly: *Cum medii gemino calculus hoste perit*.

Moreover a vestige of antiquity hints to me that in the remotest parts of the
East Indies a game much like our game of chess was used by the people of the
country before the Portuguese had thought of making the voyage; and this I
affirm on the authority of Giovan Pietro Maffei, a worthy historian. He,
reasoning of Diego Lopes Sequeira, the King of Portugal's minister in Malacca
— that celebrated city in the Golden Chersonese — for whom Patiac the Indian,
son of Utimute Raja, the mightiest after Mahmud tyrant of Malacca, was laying
an ambush, says thus in book 4 of the *History of the East Indies*: "He, to
recreate his spirit somewhat, as is the custom, had set himself to play at
chess, when the son of Utimute Raja came upon him with some armed soldiers,
under pretext of visiting him. Sequeira, sitting, seeing him come, left off
playing and went to meet him to receive him — indeed, he left off playing,
for among us too it is a kind of pastime for such needs; I shall take it
greatly to heart to yield in whatsoever house I am." So Francesco Serdonati
translates from the Latin. That the author of the work means the game of
chess is plain from his own words, which are these: *Ipse interim
oblectandi animi causa, ut fit, latrunculorum ludo tempus terebat* ["He
meanwhile, to divert his mind, as is done, was passing the time at the game
of soldiers"].

After so many reasons, let us come to the last and mightiest authority,
which is Lucan, a most noted and celebrated poet, and which will cut the suit
short against Calcagnino and his followers. He, in the *Panegyric* he makes
to Calpurnius Piso — which work has by some been falsely attributed to Ovid —
reasons thus:

   *Nec tibi mobilitas minor est, si forte volantem,
   seu vitet geminare pilam, sive revocare cadentem,
   et non sperato fugientem reddere gestu.
   It ret in populus spectacula; totaque ludos
   turba repente suos tam laetabunda reliquit.
   Te si forte iuvat studiorum pondere pressum
   non languere tamen, lususque movere per artem,
   callidiore modo tabula variatur aperta
   calculus, et vitreo peraguntur milite bella,
   ut maneat nigris, nunc par niger alliget albo.
   Sed ubi quis non terga dedit, quis te duce cessit
   calcanti saepe expugnaturus paene et hostem?
   Mille ille modis ac tua dimicat, ille petentem
   dum fugit, ipse rapit, longo venit ille recessu.
   Qui stat in speculis, hic se committere rixae
   audet, et in praedam venientem decipit hostem;
   incipit subit ille moras, stimulisque ligato
   obligat ipse duos, hic ad maiora movetur,
   ut citus, et fracta prorumpat in agmina mandra.
   Clausaque deiecto populetur moenia vallo.
   Inter ea fiet quamvis acerrima surgant
   praelia militibus, plena tamen ipse phalange,
   aut etiam pauco spoliata milite vincis,
   et tibi capta resonat manus atraque turba.*

The poet, after praising Piso as a player of ball, entering to celebrate him
as a chess player, says *callidiore modo* ["in more cunning wise"], because a
shrewder reckoning and a greater art are required for the game of chess than
for ball — which, I think, cannot be said of the riga in comparison with
ball, for its moves are few, brief, and straitened; indeed such that any man
in the briefest time may equal himself in the game of chess [?]. But the many
and diverse journeys of the chessmen are followed by a thousand tangles in
the game; hence he says, *Mille modis ac tua dimicat* ["He fights thy battle
in a thousand ways"]. That variety of moves is not seen in the riga. *Me
petentem dum fugit, ipse rapit* — behold the move: he draws the piece back to
make prey of the enemy; I am of the opinion the author means the Rook. *Longo
venit ille recessu* shows me the Queen, coming from afar; such distance of
journey is not in the riga-men, for they all leap from nearby. *Qui stat in
speculis* describes the Bishop, standing on the watch like a spy. *Incipit
subit ille moras, stimulisque ligato obligat ipse duos* reasons of the pawn,
which moves tardily and dwells in the houses more than the pieces; its move
is for the most part to thrust itself between two enemies, killing one of
them of necessity. He likens it to one bound, for its slow going and its
little worth, being the least among the chessmen. *Hic ad maiora movetur* —
this is the Knight, which must thrust forward to break the battle; hence the
poet sings, *Ut citus, et fracta prorumpat in agmina mandra* ["that swift, the
fold broken, he may burst upon the troops"]. It is the Knight's property —
for by *mandra* he denotes the closing of the game, which the Knight is wont
to break. *Clausaque deiecto populetur moenia vallo* — the game broken, the
enemy's camp is sacked. *Plena tamen ipse phalange, aut etiam pauco spoliata
milite vincis* — this place alone, from which we draw the greatest clearness
of chess beyond any other, suffices to convince the contrary opinion: for in
it is declared the victory of the game, which is checkmate, which often
befalls with all the mated man's pieces and pawns whole, or with few lost —
which in no way can suit the riga, whose victory always ends with the taking
of all the chessmen.

The followers of the contrary view might object to me that, if the game of
chess had been known to the ancients, mention would have been made among them
of the King, the Queen, and the other pieces and pawns, whose names are read
in no writer. I answer it is possible the game was then in use without this
declaration of names; it suffices me that the qualities of the pieces and
pawns be read, as I have expounded above in Lucan's verses. Indeed, in that
verse of Ovid in the third of the *Art of Love* — *Bellatorque suo prensus
sine compare bellat* ["And the warrior, caught, wars without his comrade by
his own strength"] — Bartolomeo Merula the commentator by the voice
*bellator* understands the King, and says others understand the Knight; and
by the voice *compare* he understands the Queen, and others understand the
adversary's Knight. And I add that *bellator*, by the word *prensus*, shows
me it is the King, who though taken — that is, shut in, or checked — or
though one of his men be taken or shut off, is not dismayed but fights
intrepidly. I am also of the opinion that the diction *compare* signifies the
Queen.

It only remains for me to say, for a last and no light reason, that granting
the game of chess was not in the time of Ovid and Martial, we must affirm it
was invented after — which, if it were so, the inventor would certainly be
known. For from the coming of Christ till now there is no happening, no
origin of a thing invented, that has been hidden: the authors, making mention
of the minutest things a thousand and two thousand years back, it is not
likely they would have left unrecorded the invention of chess, a game which
surpasses all others in dignity. Of many authors I have read, I find none who
says the game of chess is new — that is, invented after Martial's age; every
one remits it to antiquity. Hence, without wrong to the inventor, it may be
believed the game was not invented at first as perfect as it is seen today.
Art is the imitator of nature, which we see produces everything imperfect
from birth, and ripens it afterward, bringing it to perfection with time. The
crown of perfect things is known in God alone, from whom, as from a most
ample fountain, graces diffuse themselves to mortals. Wherefore, embracing
what has been said, let us end the discourse with an epigram of our own on
chess:

   *Si quaeris, tanti quis ludi invenerit artem,
   praedicat incertos infitiata fides...*
   [If you ask who invented the art of so great a game, a denied faith
   proclaims uncertain things...]
   *Te tamen... Palamedes...*
   [To thee, Palamedes... be the praise, won with noble blood and wit.
   But that Jove invented it — if I may speak the truth — is certain: when he
   bore arms against his guiltless patron, the lots of Pytho were cast, by
   which the fight was raised to heaven... The exiled Apollo revealed the
   game to us, when, wretched, he was cast down from the ethereal citadel.
   Then, a new herdsman feeding Admetus's flocks, he taught the shepherds and
   the country goddesses.]

[Here ends Chapter I of Book I.]

BOOK VIII, CHAPTER II
Of the New Game

The game of chess, as is plain, refers its invention to the art of war, to
which correspond the goings and the government of the pieces and the pawns.
The King, being the greatest personage, must reside in the safest place,
surrounded and defended by his men; he must not thrust himself into dangers,
but go deliberately — as his grave and tardy journey shows. The Queen, as
she who represents the captain-general, the second person after the King,
must command freely with supreme power, which her ample course in the
straight line and the oblique — granted to her alone and to none other —
hints. The Rooks, the Knights, and the Bishops denote the other officers,
inferior to the Queen but different among themselves, one superior to the
other on diverse reckonings — and all this is known by their goings and
their importance. The commonalty of the army, the lowest, are signified to
us by the pawns. So if we weigh with a just balance the reasons of each, we
shall find the due correspondence throughout — save only in the leap of the
Knight.

The King, in his journey from house to house, contains in himself the road
of the Queen, of the Rook, of the Bishop, and even of the pawn. The Rook has
his course in common with that of the pawns; in the Bishop's going is
comprised that of the pawns when they take any of the enemy; the Queen
embraces the movement of the Rook, of the Bishop, and of the pawn. Only the
Knight stands free of this community. Nor does it seem likely that, besides
the General, there should be none other with divided authority. And though
it is known to be needful for the good rule of soldiers, yet it is reasonable
to substitute a captain — whom the Knight, or the squadron of knights,
represents — who shall be almost equal to the General; and after him to
assign another, somewhat inferior, that all the offices of war may be
perfectly fulfilled.

Then let it not be unseemly that in the game there be given a piece superior
to the rank of Knights, differing little from the Queen, and another given
lesser than it. The greater of these might be called Rook-knight, for he
embraces the movement of the Rook and of the Knight together — but it has
pleased me to name him the CHAMPION. The other I might name Bishop-knight,
for the course of the Bishop and of the Knight is in him — but I name him
the CENTAUR.

If any oppose to me that it is unfitting to set the Knight's journey in
another piece, I shall answer that, there being also the Rook's journey and
the Bishop's, it will be no excess that they have been doubled in the Queen.
And lest any believe these pieces to be equal in value to the Queen — which
would utterly destroy the invention — I say that each of them is reckoned
inferior to the Queen: for the King may not draw within one square of the
enemy Queen, yet he may draw near to the Champion on the oblique line, as he
may also draw near to the Centaur on the straight line. Other inequalities
may be known from the power and the journeying of each. We find openly,
moreover, that between the Champion and the Centaur there is no small
difference. Who will deny that the Rook is greater than the Bishop? So
likewise the Knight, joined to the Rook, makes the Champion of greater price
than when joined to the Bishop [makes the Centaur]. Nor would I have any man
think that between the Champion and the Centaur there is that same
difference which is reckoned between the Rook and the Bishop — for it is
somewhat greater, and the union of force is the cause of it. I shall explain
it with a lowly example. One small ant will easily drag one grain of wheat;
ten thousand ants, though each bigger than that one, will not be able to move
a piece of iron weighing what ten thousand grains of wheat weigh.

From what is said above the order of the game is seen at full. After the
King, the worthiest soldier is the Queen; third to her in excellence the
Champion; next to him we number the Centaur; in the fourth place we set the
Rook; then equally the Knight and the Bishop; and in the last, the pawns.

This explained, there remains to set the said pieces in their places. And
because they cannot be contained in the shape of the common board, the board
must be given another fashion: it shall be, if the board be increased with
squares. There shall be constituted eight lines across and ten lengthwise,
which all contain the number of eighty squares — so that the board comes to
be increased beyond the ordinary by sixteen squares. And this invention of
squares is well founded in reason: for the proportion of the pieces and of
the chessboard requires that the Champion be given his pawn, and likewise
that his pawn be given to the Centaur. Hence from the increase of the four
pieces — two for each side — and from the increase of the four pawns, two
likewise for each squadron, we increase sixteen squares, of which eight are
empty, to give field to the said four pieces and to the four pawns.

Ponder and balance this same reckoning in the pieces, the pawns, and the
squares of the common chessboard, and it will be known to correspond to the
same number: for thirty-two chessmen possess the sixty-four squares, and
eight chessmen increased beyond possess sixteen squares more. If it be
opposed to me that in this manner the shape of the board does not come out
in perfect square, I shall say this is not necessary — but necessary is the
proportion of the squares, to which I have had full regard. Indeed, let us
affirm that the board of the new game is no less perfect than the board of
the common game: for if we look to the numbers we shall find a most complete
perfection, which is known in the Eight, in the Ten, and in the Eighty —
which numbers embrace the whole board.

What places the Champion and the Centaur must have in the chessboard will
not be hard to find. The King and the Queen shall stand in the same proper
places in which they are on the common board, with the same laws. At the
King's flank follows the Bishop, as the other Bishop follows at the Queen's
flank; next the Bishops in the same order follow the Knights. After the
Knight on the King's side set the Champion, and after the Knight on the
Queen's side set the Centaur. In the second rank, likewise across, shall be
seen ten pawns; the same order shall be seen in the other adverse squadron.
For the rest — whether it belong to the rule of the pieces or of the pawns —
observe the laws of the ordinary game.

For the greater clearness of what has been said, we set here the shape of
the chessboard, as is seen hereafter, together with its pieces.

[Here the 1617 edition prints a diagram of the board: eight squares wide and
ten squares long, eighty squares in all. The back rank, from the King's flank
to the Queen's, runs: Rook, Knight, Bishop, Queen, King, Bishop, Knight,
Champion — and on the Queen's wing, after her Knight, the Centaur; the second
rank holds ten pawns a side. The diagram's lettering is lost in the scan and
is here described in words from Carrera's own directions above.]

Let us come now to the government of the new pieces. The Champion will enter
the field [come into play] when some exchange of pawns, or of some of the
pieces, has been made — with the [advancement?] of the Queen, of which we
have spoken elsewhere. The Centaur may come out somewhat earlier, but not
before the others, for the beginning of the battle is made by the pawns, the
rooks, and the bishops.

[Here a passage of the 1617 page is too broken in the scan to be read with
certainty; the sense that can be recovered is that the two new pieces are to
appear in company with the rest of the army, and not to stand idle.]

The Champion, being nothing else but a Rook and a Knight joined, may be
fashioned so that in its upper part it shows the sign of the Rook and of the
Knight; and we say the same of the Centaur, for being composed of Bishop and
Knight it may represent the image of the one and of the other.

Let him who reads know that it is not my design to adduce this invention to
set it above the ordinary game, or to show that game imperfect — for I am
far from such rashness. Rather I affirm, and so I mean, that the common game
is most perfect. This new one which I propose will serve to feed the minds
of studious lovers of new things, since it will be seen more copious than
the old in breadth of moves, of happenings, and of victories, as trial may be
made of it. Hence I shall believe that no man of right judgment will blame
me — though at present, or in time to come, there should be found no one to
embrace or praise such an invention. For the inventors of things, be they
good or bad, deserve to be commended — if not in the thing, at least in the
thought.

[Here ends the new-game chapter. In the 1617 edition no Chapter III of Book
VIII is numbered; the text passes from this chapter to Chapter IV, on cases
of conscience in play.]

PRIMACY NOTE

None found after research — for this slice. No English rendering of Carrera's
Book I, Chapter I ("Of the Inventor of the Game") nor of his Book VIII new-game
chapter ("Of the New Game," with the Champion and the Centaur) has been
found. William Lewis's Chess for Beginners (London, 1822) translated the
practical body of Carrera's work — the openings, endings, and mates — and
states in his own preface that he deliberately left out "the chapter on the
origin of the game" and "an account of a new game... with the chess-men and
two additional pieces." Lewis's 1822 book is therefore check-only for this
edition: consulted to define the boundary, never used for a line of wording.

Caveats, stated plainly:
- Lewis's preface describes the new game as played "on a board of
  ninety-six squares." Carrera's own text, translated here, describes eight
  lines across by ten lengthwise — eighty squares, sixteen more than the
  ordinary sixty-four. The translation follows Carrera, not Lewis's memory.
- Book VIII, Chapter I of the 1617 edition ("Of the game of memory,"
  blindfold play) stands between the book's endgames and the new-game
  chapter; it is outside this slice and is not translated here.
- No numbered Chapter III of Book VIII appears in the 1617 printing used;
  the text passes from the new-game chapter to Chapter IV.
