THE TWO DILEMMAS RHYS DAVIDS LEFT OUT

The Paradox of Conception and The Modesty of the Buddha, from Book IV of the Milindapañha

Englished from Trenckner's Pali (1880)

* * *

Completing the classic Sacred Books of the East translation of T. W. Rhys Davids (1843–1922), The Questions of King Milinda, with its two omitted dilemmas, here Englished from Trenckner's Pali.

Basis: Fresh English translation of Dilemma 6 (Trenckner pp. 123–130) and Dilemma 21 (Trenckner pp. 168–170) of the Milindapañha, from V. Trenckner's edition of the Pali text (London, 1880; Internet Archive milindapahobeing00tren) — the very edition Rhys Davids translated from. The passages are printed whole, as the dilemmas stand in the Pali, with the king's objections and Nāgasena's resolutions entire. Editorial matter (the introduction and the numbered notes following the text) is the editor's.

What is absent from the classics: In his two volumes Rhys Davids translated forty of the forty-two dilemmas of Book IV and passed over two in silence, directing his readers to the Pali. This pamphlet supplies those two. It is, to the editor's knowledge, the first free, public-domain-complete rendering of the Rhys Davids Milinda: complete modern versions exist, I. B. Horner's Milinda's Questions (1963–64) and Maria Heim's (2025), but those are in copyright and have not been used as a crib here; this translation is independent, from the Pali.

INTRODUCTION: TWO GAPS IN A GREAT BOOK

T. W. Rhys Davids could translate anything the Victorians allowed into print, and almost everything they did not: he carried an entire canon across into English. But twice in the Mendakapañha, the Book of Dilemmas, his pen stops. King Milinda asks Nāgasena how, if three things must meet for conception, Sāma could be conceived by a thumb's touch on a navel — and Rhys Davids goes on to the next dilemma as if the question had never been put. Twenty dilemmas later the king asks how the Buddha, who preached restraint of the body, could show the Brahman Sela "the organ concealed in its sheath" — and again the English falls silent.

The omissions were not corrections, and no erratum confesses them. They live where a Victorian translator would bury them: in the two places the matter runs to what his age called indelicate — conception without intercourse, and a Buddha's anatomy. What he wanted his readers to have of those passages, he wanted them to have in a language most of them could not read.

The pity is that the dilemmas are good ones, argued in the book's own manner: a quotation from the Buddha that seems to condemn the Buddha, a distinction that splits the horns of the dilemma, and a chain of homely similes that wins the king. Nāgasena's conception-by-touch ends with a taxonomy of embryo-descent (by karma, by mode of birth, by family, by invitation) that no summary does justice to; his answer on Sela turns on the shadow of a thing, shown by supernormal power to a single doubter — and on the claim that a teacher's skill of means is measured by what awakens the pupil, not by what would be seemly in the abstract. Restored to their places (after Dilemma 5 and Dilemma 20, between the neighbours Rhys Davids did translate), the two pieces read as what they are: Milinda thinking against his own proof-texts, and winning.

These two are printed here from Trenckner's Pali — the edition of 1880 that stands behind the Sacred Books of the East version itself. Quotations turned to verse are set as verse; the notes at the end identify the stories and passages the dialogue takes for granted. Nothing has been softened and nothing added to the sense; where the Pali is blunt, the English is blunt.

6. THE PARADOX OF CONCEPTION

"Venerable Nāgasena, it has been said by the Blessed One: 'When three things come together, monks, there is the descent of an embryo: here, mother and father come together, the mother is in her season, and a being to be reborn is present — with the meeting of these three there is the descent of an embryo.'" [1]

"That was no reserved word, no partial word, no word said in a figure — it was spoken out plainly, seated in the midst of gods and men. And yet a descent of an embryo with only two things meeting is also seen. By the ascetic Dukūla was the female ascetic Pārikā, in her season, touched on the navel with his right thumb; by that touch on the navel the child Sāma was born. And by the seer Mataṅga was a Brahman maiden, in her season, touched on the navel with his right thumb; by that touch the youth Māṇḍabya was born. [2]

"Now if, venerable Nāgasena, it was said by the Blessed One, 'When three things meet there is the descent of an embryo,' then the saying that Sāma the child and Māṇḍabya the youth were both born by that touch on the navel must be false. And if it was said by the Tathāgata that Sāma the child and Māṇḍabya the youth were born by the touch on the navel, then the saying 'When three things meet there is the descent of an embryo' must also be false. This too is a two-horned question, deep and subtle, a matter for the wise, and it has come to you: cut through the thicket of doubt, hold up the bright torch of knowledge."

"Great king, both things were said by the Blessed One: 'When the three meet there is the descent of an embryo: mother and father come together, the mother is in her season, and a being to be reborn is present,' and 'Sāma the child was born, and Māṇḍabya the youth, by the touch on the navel.'"

"Then, venerable Nāgasena, show me by what reasoning the question can be rightly settled, and so let me be convinced."

"Have you heard, great king, by what means Saṅkicca the child, and Isisiṅga the ascetic, and the elder Kumārakassapa were born?" [3]

"Yes, venerable sir, it is told; the report of their birth is well known. Two does, in their season, came to the place where two ascetics had passed water, and drank the urine, which bore the seed of life; by that urine-born seed Saṅkicca the child and Isisiṅga the ascetic were born. And when the elder Udāyi had gone to a nuns' dwelling-place and, with a mind stirred by passion, was gazing at a nun's body, seed was shed upon his robe. Then the venerable Udāyi said to that nun, 'Go, sister, bring water; I will wash my under-robe.' 'Nay, reverend, I will wash it myself,' she said. Then that nun, being in her season, took part of that seed with her mouth and put part of it where her own seed was, and by that the elder Kumārakassapa was born — so people say. Do you believe that account, great king?"

"Yes, venerable sir; there I find a powerful reason to believe it, and that is the very ground of my belief: by this means were they born."

"And what is the reason, great king?"

"Well, venerable Nāgasena — if seed falls into a properly prepared embryo-stock, it very quickly grows."

"Just so, great king."

"Even so, venerable sir: that nun, being in her season, while the embryo-stock stood formed and the flow of blood checked and the element at rest, took that seed and placed it in that embryo-stock; by that a conception was established for her. This we perceive as the reason for their birth."

"It is so indeed, great king; in just this way I accept it: the embryo comes to be by entrance through the passage. And do you, great king, accept the descent of the embryo [that resulted] for Kumārakassapa?"

"Yes, venerable sir."

"Well done, great king! You have come over to my side; and if you speak of the descent of an embryo even in a single manner, you make me your ally. [4] Now then, those two does that drank urine and conceived: do you believe in the descent of an embryo for them?"

"Yes, venerable sir. Whatever is eaten, drunk, chewed, or tasted, all of it passes down into the embryo-stock and, arriving there, ripens into growth. Just as all rivers, venerable Nāgasena, flow down into the great ocean and, arriving there, swell and grow: so too whatever is eaten, drunk, chewed, or tasted all passes down into the embryo-stock and, arriving there, ripens into growth. For this reason I believe: there can be a descent of an embryo even by way of the mouth."

"Well done, great king! You have come over to my side still more strongly if even by drinking at the mouth there is a meeting of two of the things; and you accept the descent of the embryos of Saṅkicca the child, of Isisiṅga the ascetic, and of the elder Kumārakassapa."

"Yes, venerable sir; the meeting passes down."

"Now, great king, Sāma the child and Māṇḍabya the youth fall within the threefold meeting too, and are at one with the former cases; here is the reason for it, which I will explain.

"Dukūla the ascetic and Pārikā the female ascetic were both of them living in the forest, intent on seclusion, seeking the highest good; and by the heat of their austerity they shook even as far as the Brahmā world. [5] Then Sakka, king of the gods, who waited on them in person, saw in his regard for their worth that in the time to come the eyes of both of them would fail, and seeing this he said to them: 'Do me this one favour, good sirs; please, beget one son; he will be a support and stay for you both.' 'Enough, Kosiya! Do not say so,' they said, and did not accept his word. A second and a third time, full of kindness and desiring their welfare, Sakka, king of the gods, said to them: 'Do me this one favour, good sirs; please, beget one son; he will be a support and stay for you both.' And a third time they answered him: 'Enough, Kosiya! Do not join us to what is not good for us. Never, not if this body breaks — and it is of a nature to break — not if the mountain-sides fall, not if the peaks fall in, not if the sky splits open, not if moon and sun come down, shall we mix ourselves with the things of the world. Do not come before our face again; and if you come, this is the trust you get from us: we shall hold you a doer of harm.'

"Then Sakka, king of the gods, not winning their consent, went back much subdued and, with folded hands, begged them again: 'If you will not undertake to do my word, then, venerable sir, when the female ascetic is in her season, in her flowering time, you shall touch her navel with your right thumb; by that she will conceive: even that is a meeting, for the descent of an embryo.' 'I can do that word of yours, Kosiya; even by that measure our austerity is not broken.' So they consented.

"And at that time there was in the world of the gods a god, rich in well-laid roots of merit, whose life was spent and who had reached the end of his span; he was able to descend at his own pleasure, even into the family of a universal monarch. Then Sakka, king of the gods, went to that god and said to him: 'Come now, friend; fair dawned the day for you; the winning of your wish has drawn near. I waited on you for this: you will have your dwelling in a delightsome place; you will be born into a fitting family; you will be raised by a good father and mother. Come; do my bidding,' and he begged him a second and a third time, with folded hands at his head. Then that god said: 'What is this family you keep on mentioning to me again and again?' 'Dukūla the ascetic and Pārikā the female ascetic.' The god, having heard that word of his, was pleased and consented: 'Well, friend; as you wish, so be it. But longing though I am, friend, to be born in the family I have wished for — in which family shall I be born: among those born from an egg, or from a womb, or from moisture, or suddenly?' 'You shall be born, friend, of a womb-born mother.' Then Sakka, king of the gods, having reckoned the day of his descent, informed Dukūla the ascetic: 'On such-and-such a day, venerable sir, the female ascetic will be in her season, in her flowering time; on that day, sir, you shall touch her navel with your right thumb.'

"And on that day, great king, the female ascetic was in her season and in flower; the god had arrived and taken his place; and the ascetic touched the female ascetic's navel with his right thumb. Thus those three meetings came to be. By that touch on the navel, desire arose in the female ascetic; but her desire arose in dependence on that touch on the navel, not because the meeting went beyond its bounds. Talking together too is a meeting; speaking too is a meeting; gazing too is a meeting; and in the early stage of desire having arisen, touch is a meeting, and by the meeting descent comes to be. And so, great king, even without impropriety, by touch alone there is the descent of an embryo — just as, great king, a blazing fire even without touching makes ice that comes near it melt away: so too, great king, even without impropriety, by touch alone there is the descent of an embryo. [6]

"In four ways, great king, comes about the descent of an embryo for beings: by their karma, by their mode of birth, by their family, and by invitation. And yet all these beings are born of karma, and have their arising from karma.

"How is the descent of an embryo by karma? Beings rich in well-laid roots of merit, great king, are born at their own pleasure: in the family of rich nobles, or of rich Brahmans, or of rich householders, or among the gods; or of an egg-born mother, or born from a womb, or born from moisture, or born suddenly. Just as a man, great king — wealthy, rich, with great possessions, with much gold and silver, with much property and equipment, with much wealth and grain, with many connections and followers — buys with pleasure whatever he wishes in his heart, even at twice or three times the price, whether a bondwoman or a bondsman, a field or a site, a village or a market town or a district: so too beings rich in well-laid roots of merit are born at their own pleasure — in the family of rich nobles, or of rich Brahmans, or of rich householders, or among the gods; of an egg-born mother, or born from a womb, or born from moisture, or born suddenly. In this way is the descent of an embryo by karma.

"How is the descent of an embryo by mode of birth? Hens, great king, conceive by the wind; cranes by the sound of thunder; [7] and all the gods are beings who do not lie in a womb: for them the descent of an embryo comes about in various ways. As men, great king, walk the world in various ways — some cover themselves in front, some behind, some go naked, some shave their heads and wear white cloth, some wear their hair in a topknot, some shave their heads and wear the saffron robe, some wear the saffron robe and the topknot too, some are naked ascetics wearing bark cloth, some wear hides, some wear ropes — all these men walk the world in various ways: so too, great king, though they are all beings, the descent of an embryo comes about for them in various ways. In this way is the descent of an embryo by mode of birth.

"How is the descent of an embryo by family? There are four kinds of family: born from an egg, born from a womb, born from moisture, born suddenly. If a spirit to be reborn, [8] coming from anywhere, is born in an egg-born family, there he is egg-born — and likewise in a womb-born family, in a moisture-born family, in a suddenly-born family: creatures arise in each family as befits that family. As, great king, the wild animals and birds that come to Mount Neru on the Himālayas all leave off their own colour and take on golden colour: so too, great king, any such spirit, coming from anywhere, reaches an egg-born mother, leaves off its own nature and becomes egg-born — and likewise with a womb-born, moisture-born, or suddenly-born mother. In this way is the descent of an embryo by family.

"How is the descent of an embryo by invitation? Here, great king, there is a family without a son, rich in possessions, faithful, believing, well-conducted, of good character, resting upon austerity; and there is a god, rich in well-laid roots of merit, who is of a nature to fall from that world. Then Sakka, king of the gods, out of compassion for that family, invites that god: 'Direct your mind, friend, to the womb of the chief wife of such-and-such a family.' And he, by reason of that invitation, directs his mind to that family. Just as, great king, men who desire merit invite a meditative monk, a man delightful to the mind, and bring him into their house, thinking, 'This one, when he has come, will be a bringer of happiness to the whole family': so too, great king, Sakka, king of the gods, invites that god and brings him into that family. In this way is the descent of an embryo by invitation.

"Sāma the child, great king, was invited by Sakka, king of gods, and descended into the womb of Pārikā the female ascetic. Sāma the child, great king, had done merit; his parents were well-conducted and of good character; the inviter was able; and Sāma the child was born by the directed thought of those three. Here, great king: if a skilled farmer set seed in good soil in a good field, and no harm befell that seed as it sprouted, would any harm come to its growth?"

"No, venerable sir; free of harm, venerable sir, the seed would sprout quickly."

"Even so, great king, Sāma the child, freed from the obstacles that stand against being born, was born by the directed thought of those three. And have you heard, great king, that by the anger of seers a rich and flourishing district full of people has been cut off and laid waste?"

"Yes, venerable sir, it is told upon earth: the Dandaka forest, the Mejjha forest, the Kaliṅga forest, the Mātaṅga forest — all of that became forest, and all those districts came to ruin by the anger of seers."

"If, great king, by their anger rich and flourishing districts are destroyed, would anything be born by their favour?"

"Yes, venerable sir."

"Then so it is, great king: Sāma the child was born by the powerful directed thought of three — as one made by a seer, made by a god, made by merit: hold it so, great king. These three, great king, are gods born into a family at Sakka's invitation: Sāma the child, Mahāpanāda, and King Kusa — all three of them Bodhisattas. [9]"

"Well shown, venerable Nāgasena, is the descent of the embryo; well spoken the reason; darkness has been made light; the tangles are untangled; the talk of the other side has been swept away. It is so; I accept it as you say."

21. THE MODESTY OF THE BUDDHA

"Venerable Nāgasena, this too was said by the Tathāgata:

"'Good is restraint in the body; good is restraint in speech; good is restraint in the mind; good is restraint everywhere.' [10]

"And again: seated amid the four assemblies, before gods and men, the Tathāgata showed to Sela the Brahman the male organ concealed in its sheath. [11] Now if, venerable Nāgasena, it was said by the Blessed One, 'Good is restraint in the body,' then the saying that he showed Sela the Brahman the organ concealed in a sheath must be false. If he showed Sela the Brahman the organ concealed in a sheath, then the saying 'Good is restraint in the body' must also be false. This too is a two-horned question, and it has come to you; it is to be solved by you."

"Great king, both were as you say: it was said by the Blessed One, 'Good is restraint in the body'; and to Sela the Brahman the organ concealed in a sheath was shown. But to a man in whom doubt about the Tathāgata has arisen, for the sake of awakening him, the Blessed One by supernormal power shows a body like the thing itself, and he alone sees that marvel."

"But who, venerable Nāgasena, will believe this, that a man gone into an assembly is the only one who sees that hidden thing, while the rest, though sitting in the very same place, do not see it? So then, tell me the reason in this matter; convince me by a reason."

"Have you ever seen, great king, a sick man surrounded by his kinsfolk and friends?"

"Yes, venerable sir."

"Does the assembly see the pain by which that man is suffering pain?"

"No, venerable sir; that man alone, venerable sir, suffers it."

"Even so, great king: on whomsoever doubt about the Tathāgata has arisen, to him alone does the Tathāgata, for his awakening, show by supernormal power a body like the thing itself, and he alone sees that marvel. Or again, great king: if a spirit entered into some man, would the assembly see that spirit's coming?"

"No, venerable sir; the sick man alone sees the coming of that spirit."

"Even so, great king: on whomsoever doubt about the Tathāgata has arisen, he alone sees that marvel."

"It is a hard thing, venerable Nāgasena, that the Blessed One has done, in showing what should not be shown even to one man."

"Great king, the Blessed One did not show the hidden thing; by supernormal power he showed its shadow. [12]"

"Even, venerable sir, when its shadow has been seen, the hidden thing itself is seen — the very thing on seeing which one crosses over."

"And yet, great king, the Tathāgata does even a hard thing in order to awaken the beings who are to be awakened. If, great king, the Tathāgata were to let slip, one by one, the means of awakening, beings to be awakened would not awaken. But because, great king, the Tathāgata knows the means by which those to be awakened are awakened, by that very means does he awaken them. Just as, great king, a physician, a worker with the knife, comes to a sick man with whatever medicine makes him well: what needs emptying out he empties; what needs purging he purges; what needs anointing he anoints; what needs an enema he gives an enema: even so, great king, the Tathāgata, by the very means by which those to be awakened are awakened, awakens them. And as, great king, a woman big with child shows her hidden part, the part that should not be shown, to the doctor: even so, great king, for the sake of awakening those to be awakened, the Tathāgata showed the shadow of the unshowable hidden thing by supernormal power. There is no place, great king, that is unshowable, when it is a question of the person. If, great king, a man would awaken on seeing the Blessed One's very heart, even his heart would the Blessed One show him, by skill of means. Skilled in means, great king, is the Tathāgata, and clever in teaching."

"Did not the Tathāgata, great king, knowing the bent of the elder Nanda's mind, take him to the world of the gods and show him the celestial maidens, thinking, 'By this will this son of good family awaken'? [13] And by that, that son of good family awoke. Thus is the Tathāgata, great king, skilled in means, clever in teaching, though in many a way he spurned and blamed and loathed the sign of beauty: yet for the sake of awakening that monk he showed him the heavenly maidens with their dove-like gait. Thus too is the Tathāgata skilled in means, and clever in teaching. And again further, great king: the Tathāgata went to the brother of the elder Cūḷapanthaka, who had been driven out and was grieving and downcast, and gave him a fine piece of cloth, thinking, 'By this will this son of good family awaken'; and by that reason that son of good family won mastery in the Conqueror's dispensation. [14] Thus too is the Tathāgata skilled in means, and clever in teaching. And again further, great king: the Tathāgata, thrice asked a question by the Brahman Mogharāja, did not answer him, thinking, 'So will this son of good family's mind be calmed; by the calming of his pride there will be understanding'; and by that, that son of good family's mind was calmed, his pride calmed, and that Brahman won mastery in the six knowledges. [15] Thus too is the Tathāgata skilled in means, and clever in teaching."

"Well solved, venerable Nāgasena, is the question, in many kinds of reasons: what was hidden has been laid open; darkness has been made light; the knot has been cut; the talk of the other side has been crushed; you have given eyes to the sons of the Conqueror; the rival teachers stand without answer; and you have reached the chief of the elephant herd." [16]

NOTES

[1] The king quotes the Blessed One's saying (the descent of the embryo: Mahātaṇhāsaṅkhaya Sutta, Majjhima Nikāya 38, and elsewhere): three things must coincide — the union of the parents, the mother's season (utu), and the presence of the gandhabba, the being awaiting rebirth. Dilemma 6 (Trenckner pp. 123–130).

[2] Sāma (the hero of the Sāma Jātaka, no. 540): son of the blind ascetics Dukūla and Pārikā, begotten without intercourse, by the mere touch of the father's thumb on the mother's navel. Māṇḍabya appears here as a second case of the same kind, at the hands of the seer Mataṅga — keep watch on the names: the scan writes "Dukila/Dukula," "Mandabya," "Matanga"; Trenckner's own spelling has been followed. Kumārakassapa (below) is the famous preacher of the Pāyāsi Sutta.

[3] The elder Udāyi here is Kāludāyi. The nun's act with the seed is reported plainly, as the text reports it — Rhys Davids would have had this passage before his eyes too: it sits inside the very dilemma he left out.

[4] "You make me your ally": Nāgasena's jujitsu is argumentative courtesy — every fresh conception the king concedes is counted a fresh "meeting" won.

[5] The merit of both ascetics "shook even as far as the Brahmā world" (tāpatejena yāva brahmalokā): the stock phrase for austerity so intense it echoes in the highest heavens, expressed here as a warming ("tapa," heat, is cognate with "austerity").

[6] Similes carry the proof here as always in Book IV: ice un-touched by the fire still melts when it comes near (the fire is heat acting at a distance; the embryo descends without the act). The king's own argument is preserved intact: seed placed in a prepared field (kalala, the first stage of the embryo) is the ground of his assent in the Kumārakassapa and deer cases, and Nāgasena adopts it word for word.

[7] "Hens conceive by the wind; cranes by the sound of thunder": popular natural history in the Pali books; geese in some readings. The point is the taxonomy, not the zoology.

[8] Gandhabba (Skt. gandharva): the being-to-be-reborn, whose "presence" is the third condition. In the "family" (kula) paragraphs that being takes the kind of whichever of the four families of birth — egg-born, womb-born, moisture-born, apparitional — it enters, as birds that reach Mount Neru (Meru) take on the mountain's golden colour.

[9] Mahāpanāda and Kusa (Kusa Jātaka, no. 531) are, like Sāma, reckoned births of the Bodhisatta (the future Buddha) — so the "invited" births honour both the ascetics' chastity and the pedigree of the child.

[10] The verse is Dhammapada 361, quoted by the king as canonical proof-text.

[11] "The organ concealed in a sheath" (kosohita vatthaguyha): the thirty-second of the thirty-two marks of a Great Man, the withdrawn male organ, which the Brahman Sela wished to verify (Sela Sutta, Majjhima Nikāya 92). The dilemma is Dilemma 21 (Trenckner pp. 168–170).

[12] "Its shadow" (chāyā): Nāgasena's whole answer hangs on the distinction — the Buddha produced by iddhi (supernormal power) a counterfeit (paṭibhāga) body that only the doubting Sela saw; nothing hidden was literally exposed. The king's objection (that a seen shadow is as good as the thing seen) is then met not by logic but by purpose: awakening.

[13] Nanda, the Buddha's half-brother, was lured from his wedding by a tour of heaven and its apsaras (Udāna 3.2 and the commentaries): the "sign of beauty" the Master elsewhere condemned, used deliberately as bait for a mind that only beauty could move.

[14] Cūḷapanthaka, the dull brother driven from the Order by Mahāpanthaka, was set to rub a clean white cloth until it soiled — and by watching it soil learned impermanence and became an arahant.

[15] Mogharāja, one of the sixteen students of Bāvarī (Sutta Nipāta, Pārāyanavagga), was put off three times before his question was answered, so that pride might cool before insight.

[16] "Reached the chief of the elephant-herd" (gaṇīvarapavaram āsajja): the lion among disputants has met his match — Nāgasena is ranked with the head of the teaching lineage.

WHAT THIS PAMPHLET IS

T. W. Rhys Davids (1843–1922) translated The Questions of King Milinda into the Sacred Books of the East (vol. XXXV, 1890; vol. XXXVI, 1894). It remains the classic English version, and it is not quite complete. In Book IV, the Mendakapañha — the "Solving of Dilemmas" between King Milinda and the monk Nāgasena — he passed over two of the forty-two dilemmas in silence, leaving his readers to find them in Trenckner's Pali if they could: the sixth, "The Paradox of Conception" (Trenckner pp. 123–130), and the twenty-first, "The Modesty of the Buddha" (Trenckner pp. 168–170).

He gave his reasons nowhere that we can find; the motive commonly guessed is Victorian prudery, and the matter of the two dilemmas — how beings are conceived outside intercourse, and the Buddha's private anatomy — gives that guess its weight. Whatever the reason, the omissions are a matter of record, not of interpretation: each dilemma can be checked in place, before and behind its neighbours, in Trenckner's edition, and their absence from Rhys Davids' two volumes can be checked by anyone with his Contents and his text.

This pamphlet prints the two dilemmas Englished fresh from Trenckner's Pali (the 1880 edition Rhys Davids himself translated from), so that the classic Sacred Books of the East version can stand, at last, whole. It does not pretend to be the first complete English Milinda: complete modern versions exist — I. B. Horner's Milinda's Questions (Pali Text Society, 1963–64) and Maria Heim's (2025) — both in copyright. The claim here is narrower and true: this is the first free, public-domain-complete Rhys Davids.

Rhys Davids (tr.), The Questions of King Milinda, Part I (Sacred Books of the East XXXV, Oxford, 1890) and Part II (vol. XXXVI, 1894) — left standing untouched for the forty dilemmas he translated; the two printed here are placed to drop into his Book IV in the order of Trenckner's text. - Bhikkhu Pesala's abridged Debate of King Milinda was consulted only to check the numbering and titles of the two dilemmas (his Dilemma 6, "The Paradox of Conception," and Dilemma 21, "The Modesty of the Buddha," in the sequence of Mendakapañha). His wording has not been followed; the rendering here is new and independent from the Pali. - Trenckner (1880), Rhys Davids (1890, 1894), and the Pali text itself are public domain.
