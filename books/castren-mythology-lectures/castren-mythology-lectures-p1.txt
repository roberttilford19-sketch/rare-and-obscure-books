ETHNOLOGICAL LECTURES ON THE ALTAIC PEOPLES

with the Samoyed Tales and Tatar Heroic Sagas

Matthias Alexander Castrén (1857; ed. Anton Schiefner)

Fresh English translation from the 1857 St. Petersburg edition (ed. Anton Schiefner).

> In Memoriam > > Matthias Alexander Castrén (1813–1852) > > of Tervola in Finnish Lapland; traveller in Karelia, Lapland, Arctic Russia and Siberia; first professor of the Finnish language at the Alexander University in Helsingfors; founder of the scientific study of the Uralic peoples.

Dedicated and given to English readers from the labors of Matthias Alexander Castrén (1813–1852), who crossed Siberia on foot, sledge and river-boat to write down the languages and beliefs of peoples no scholar had ever recorded, and who was lecturing these very pages to his first students in the spring of 1851, eleven months before tuberculosis closed his mouth at the age of thirty-eight. The lectures were never fully worked out — his own editor says so, in the preface translated in this volume — and no English version of them has ever been found. His name stands at the head of this book, where it always belonged.

Matthias Alexander Castrén was born on 2 December 1813 at Tervola, in the parish of Kemi in northern Finland, the son of Christian Castrén, parish minister at Rovaniemi. His father died in 1825, and the boy was raised under the care of his uncle at Kemi, sent to school at Uleåborg (Oulu), where he helped keep himself by teaching younger children, and entered the Alexander University at Helsingfors in 1830. He began in Greek and Hebrew, meaning to enter the church; the language of his own country took hold of him instead, and before his course was finished he had already begun laying foundations for a work on Finnish mythology. The need to see the unwritten languages of the kindred peoples with his own eyes drove everything after. In 1838 he travelled through Lapland with the physician Dr. Ehrström; in 1839 he went into Russian Karelia at the expense of the Literary Society of Finland; in 1841–1844 he journeyed with Dr. Elias Lönnrot — the compiler of the Kalevala, whose epic Castrén had lately turned into Swedish — across Finnish and Russian Lapland and beyond the Urals to Obdorsk. Out of that journey came his grammars of Komi (Zyryan) and Mari (Cheremis), printed in 1844 and 1845. Scarcely recovered from the illness that journey cost him, he set out again in 1845, under the Imperial Academy of St. Petersburg and the Alexander University, for the great Siberian expedition of 1845–1849 — the whole of Siberia, people by people, language by language. It made his name and ruined his body. In 1850 he published his treatise On the Personal Affixes of the Altaic Languages and was appointed to the new chair of Finnish language and literature at Helsingfors, the first man to hold it. In 1851, on the fourteenth of March, the Emperor-Chancellor handed him his diploma with his own hand; on the sixth of May he opened the lecture course of which this book is the record. He died at Helsingfors on 7 May 1852, aged thirty-eight, busily at work on what he considered his principal labour, a grammar of the Samoyedic languages — a work he did not live to finish, and which Schiefner published from his papers in 1854.

The circumstances of the tragedy are written plain in the preface to this volume, and readers may judge them in Schiefner's own words below. The lectures were composed almost without preparation, in the crush of new official duties, four times a week through the May of 1851, interrupted again and again by sickness; the chapter on the Voguls had to be worked up by another hand from a draft, because the full manuscript was not found among his papers; the Finnic peoples, his own people, the subject nearest his heart, could be given only the last half-hour of his twelve lectures. "That a revision of the lectures by Castrén himself would have shaped much very differently," Schiefner writes, "need not even be said." What stands in this volume is therefore not the finished monument Castrén intended. It is the scaffolding of a life's synthesis, raised by a dying man at full speed, and left standing when he fell. And yet it stands still.

Why does this book matter — why vindicate his logic? Because Castrén stands at the fountainhead of an entire world of study. He is regarded today as the founder not only of Uralic linguistics but of Altaic and Palaeosiberian studies beside it; in his lifetime men said he had "written the grammars of fourteen languages," and the separate idioms he in fact documented come close to thirty, from the Sámi of Lapland to the Samoyedic, Turkic, Mongolic, Tungusic and Yeniseic peoples of Siberia — with most of them, he was the first human being to collect any coherent grammatical record at all. These lectures are the one place where he stepped back from the grammars and drew the whole map: the Finns, Samoyeds, Turks, Mongols and Tungus of his "Altaic" family, their migrations, their histories as the Chinese annals and the native traditions preserve them, their customs, their gods. Interleaved with the lectures stand the five Samoyed tales and the seven Tatar heroic sagas that close this volume — stories taken down from living tellers in the 1840s, preserved in the form in which he heard them, and now carried into English with them. Modern scholarship no longer follows his "Altaic" frame, and no one should pretend otherwise; but the frame was the instrument that got the recording done, and the record outlived the frame. That is the logic of his labour, and a hundred and seventy years of scholarship since have proved it sound: the peoples he wrote down are studied from his pages to this day.

And why this man, and why now? This series exists for a simple reason: that works which waited, unfinished or unread, for want of one living reader to carry them over, should be carried over — with the dead man's name held up front, his story told true, and the book given away free to anyone who wants it. Castrén's lectures have waited a hundred and sixty-nine years for an English reader. They were left, by his own editor's confession, unrevised; his great Samoyedic grammar was left unfinished at his death; the greater part of his field collections was still unpublished when the fever of his ruined health took him at thirty-eight. If ever a scholar earned the right to be met by a stranger coming the other way — to have his work picked up where his hands let go of it, and held up where the world can see it — it is this Finnish schoolmaster's son who walked to Obdorsk and back and gave his lungs to Siberia. This English edition is offered in that spirit: not a replacement for the Finnish, Swedish and German scholarship that guards his legacy, but a door, opened at last, for the English reader who could not get in.



Editor's Introduction

The book here translated is Ethnologische Vorlesungen über die altaische Völker nebst samojedischen Märchen und tatarischen Heldensagen — Ethnological Lectures on the Altaic Peoples, together with Samoyed Tales and Tatar Heroic Sagas — printed at St. Petersburg in 1857 by the press of the Imperial Academy of Sciences, as part of the German series of Castrén's Northern Journeys and Researches which Anton Schiefner edited from the Swedish collected edition (Helsingfors, 1852–1858). Castrén had delivered the substance of the lectures at Helsingfors in May and June of 1851, in the first weeks of his professorship. Schiefner's preface, translated in full below, tells how hastily they were written, how ill their author already was, and what help other hands gave them after his death; there is no better introduction to the lectures than his, and the editor of this English edition will not compete with it.

The plan of the volume is Castrén's own. An introduction sets out his subject and method: why the Finnish language cannot be studied alone, why the compared languages and compared customs of the kindred peoples are the true instruments for lighting up the Finns' own antiquity, and why — against the craniologists of his day — language, not skulls, is the trustworthy witness of kinship. There follow the peoples in turn, from east to west: the Tungus of Manchuria; the Mongols, with the legendary history of Genghis Khan as the Mongol chronicler Sanang Setsen told it; the Turks, from the Xiongnu of the Chinese annals to the peoples of the steppe who entered Europe; the Samoyeds of the Arctic coast; the small Yenisei Ostyak remnant; and last, longest, and closest to his heart, the Finns — the Ugric peoples of the Ob (with the great ethnographic account of the Obdorsk Ostyaks), the Volga peoples, the Permians, and the Finnic peoples proper, closing with a short concluding word. After the lectures come the five Samoyed tales and the seven Tatar heroic sagas, the harvest of his tale-collecting among the peoples of the Sayan country, printed here in the order of the 1857 edition.

Three honest cautions. First, the "Altaic" theory of this book — the kinship of Finnic, Samoyedic, Turkic, Mongolic and Tungusic in one family — was Castrén's working frame and is stated in these lectures with the confidence of 1851; later scholarship has kept the Uralic family (Finnic and Samoyedic) and dissolved the wider one. The lectures are printed as he gave them, frame and all, because the frame is part of the record and the record is the value. Second, the lectures are unfinished in the small as in the large: they were written at speed, by an ill man, and never revised by him. Where this English seems abrupt, the German was abrupt first. Third, the peoples named here — Nenets, Khanty, Mansi, Mari, Komi, Udmurt, and many more, under the names the nineteenth century gave them — are living peoples with living languages, and this book is offered as a historical record of what a great recorder heard among their ancestors, not as a word over them.

No English translation of these lectures has been found after research. They have been read for a hundred and seventy years in Swedish and in Schiefner's German; the Finns have honoured their author with editions, a learned society's Manuscripta Castreniana, and a bust at Kamppi in Helsinki. The English reader has had, until now, only comments at second hand. This edition closes that gap with a fresh translation made directly from the 1857 German edition, given away free, under Castrén's name.



Signposts: Where to Go Deeper

For the reader who wishes to follow Castrén further, the paths are well marked. Begin with Schiefner's German edition itself, of which this book is the translation: Nordische Reisen und Forschungen, the Ethnologische Vorlesungen (St. Petersburg, 1857), and beside it the companion volume of the deathbed lectures, Vorlesungen über die finnische Mythologie (St. Petersburg, 1853), where the mythology of the Finns that this course could not reach is treated in full. His own voice speaks most directly in the travel writings: the Reiseerinnerungen aus den Jahren 1838–1844 and the Reiseberichte und Briefe aus den Jahren 1845–1849, the narratives of the Lapland, Karelian and Siberian journeys, also issued in Schiefner's German series. The linguistic monuments stand in a row: Elementa grammatices Syrjaenae (1844) and Elementa grammatices Tscheremissae (1845); the Versuch einer ostjakischen Sprachlehre (1849); De affixis personalibus linguarum Altaicarum (1850); and the posthumous Grammatik der samojedischen Sprachen (St. Petersburg, 1854), the principal work he died upon. The unpublished remainder — the field notebooks, wordlists and letters of the thirty-two manuscript volumes — rests with the National Library of Finland in Helsinki (the letter collection is catalogued as Manuscript Collection Coll. 539), and the Finno-Ugrian Society's Manuscripta Castreniana project continues to edit it, volume by volume, for the scholars of the next century. The man who wants Castrén will not lack for Castrén; he lacked only an English door. It is open now.

Editor's Preface to the German Edition (1857)

The present volume of the Northern Travels and Researches has been translated from the Swedish edition prepared for the press in the course of this year by Mr. Carl Gustav Borg in Helsingfors. As Mr. Borg rightly remarks in his preface, Castrén by no means left his ethnological lectures fully worked out or ready for printing. Castrén's appointment as professor at the Alexander University came more swiftly than could have been expected, for the high Chancellor of the University, our now reigning Emperor and Lord, deigned on 14 March 1851 to present him, with his own hand, with the diploma of appointment. As a result, Castrén also found himself obliged, without the least delay, to begin his lectures at once. After his inaugural lecture on 6 May, he opened the present course with lectures on the ethnology of the Altaic peoples, which he gave four times a week in the course of the month of May. He wrote them down with almost no preparation at all, amid the pressure of various official duties, and was in addition now and then hindered by ill health. All the more emphatically must we ask the reader to take to heart, in their full extent, Castrén's concluding words, expressed on pp. 153 ff.

Before Castrén decided to give the present lectures in their present scope, he intended, in view of the soon approaching close of the spring semester, to lecture only on the ethnography of the Finnish or Chudic family, and for this purpose wrote down a part which he later used, with a few small changes, for the ethnological lectures, and by which the greater fullness of treatment occurring in this part also finds its explanation. The section concerning the Ostyaks is taken from Castrén's intended, and for the most part worked-out, ethnographic description of this people, which has also already found a place in the Travel Memoirs, pp. 286–308. The chapter on the Voguls has been worked out by Mr. Borg after a draft left behind by Castrén, since the actual manuscript for it was not found among his papers. The Finnish tribal stock proper has been treated with the greatest brevity and only in part, since Castrén could devote to it only the last half hour of his twelve lectures.

That a considerable number of errors, great and small, have crept in during so rapid a preparation of these lectures should surprise no one; several of them, too, have been removed by the editor as far as possible. That a revision of the lectures by Castrén himself would have shaped many things differently need hardly be remarked. In a few cases his otherwise excellent memory deserted him or led him astray. In particular, it must not remain unmentioned that what is said on p. 14 ff. about H. von Baer's article on the Karagass skull requires an essential correction. Even if the Karagass now speak a Turkish (Tatar) dialect, the neighborhood of the Buryats has nevertheless affected them very strongly, and Castrén himself found (Travel Reports and Letters, p. 388 ff.), "even on a superficial observation, in various Karagass such features as bear witness to a Samoyed origin, and discovered in the whole way of life of the Karagass much that is unmistakably an inheritance from the Samoyeds." Just as little is it a mistake on Middendorff's part when he ascribes to the portraits of the Timan Samoyeds in everything the marks of the Mongolian race, while on the other hand the Kanin Samoyeds seem to him to stand very close to the Finnish type; even if both Samoyed tribes are now in the closest relationship to one another in language, manners, and way of life, the power of assimilation, working energetically onward in the course of time, is by no means thereby set aside. Castrén himself indeed states (Travel Reports and Letters, p. 331) that there are Samoyed and Yenisei-Ostyak tribes which were first Tatarized and then Russified. Compare ibid., p. 360.

The second half of the present volume consists of the Samoyed tales and Tatar heroic sagas translated by Castrén into Swedish and then by the undersigned into German. Two other Samoyed tales have already been printed in the Travel Reports and Letters, pp. 175–182, with which compare those included among the language specimens after the Samoyed vocabularies, pp. 311–401. Besides the Tatar heroic sagas communicated here, Castrén collected many others in the Tatar original, one of which will be added as a language specimen to the Koibal and Karagass grammar. Perhaps it may soon be possible for the undersigned to lay before the friends of epic poetry a rhythmic adaptation of several such sagas.

It must not remain unmentioned that we owe the table of contents to the diligence of Mr. C. G. Borg.

A. Schiefner

St. Petersburg, 8 (20) August 1857



Introduction

Gentlemen! As I am now about to enter upon the exercise of my office as a teacher, I hold myself obliged to give a brief account of the subject which I intend henceforth to treat in my lectures. Naturally it is the Finnish language, the study of which it is my duty to promote to the best of my ability; yet instruction in language can, as is well known, be pursued from different points of view. Commonly one looks only to the practical benefit which knowledge of languages brings with it. If one wishes to learn a language, either in order to make its literature one's own or in order to draw advantage from one's knowledge of the language as an official and a man of business, or, in a word, if one wishes to use the language as a means to another end, then it is the practical, useful side of language knowledge that one chiefly keeps in view. The man of science commonly looks down with proud contempt upon this side of ability and knowledge in general, and it must indeed be admitted that this side is not the highest. Knowledge constitutes the foremost prerogative of man; through the faculty of knowing, man occupies the first place in the chain of creatures, and he must strive after insight and knowledge chiefly for the reason that thereby he may attain his human and rational destiny. Children can be brought by the rod or by sugar-work to learn the ABC; savages one would compel by Paradise or by flames of brimstone to read God's word; the educated man strives after knowledge because this is his highest purpose on earth. But although knowledge has its own end, we nevertheless cannot rate too highly those men who, without making any use of their knowledge, put the same under a bushel, as was the case with the scholars of the Middle Ages. Rather do we hold it to be very praiseworthy, and very important for the progress of knowledge, that everyone should make use of his knowledge and apply the same as a means to every permitted and noble purpose. The sun is surely not set in the vault of heaven to dispense fire to mortals; Prometheus was nevertheless venerated by the Greeks as a god, because he procured fire from the sun and taught men its use. May knowledge therefore confer in the practical relations of life all the benefit that it can. This wish we cannot express loudly enough when it concerns knowledge of our mother tongue. But even without this knowledge we can very well attain our human destiny, even though in our practical relations it is indispensably necessary to us. I should doubtless greatly misunderstand my calling if, in instruction in the Finnish language, I had only the interest of science before my eyes and took no account of its practical need. It is true that the practical study of Finnish has hitherto not been my proper object of attention; I nevertheless recognize its great importance and shall also in this respect seek to meet all reasonable wishes.

With practical knowledge of language, philology in its ordinary signification is most closely connected. So-called philology is indeed a science of language, but it has its chief value as an auxiliary science of history. For philology, language is essentially a means by which it seeks to investigate the intellectual life of a people. Considered merely from the linguistic standpoint, this philology is one of the most meagre and empty sciences. It consists, in a very essential part, in a minute criticism of the Greek and Roman writers. This criticism has indeed been very necessary in order to purify the writings of the classics from the additions and falsifications of later times, and from this point of view it has no small significance; it must not, however, be set up as an independent end of science. It is rightly regarded as the duty of every scientifically educated man to study the writings of the ancient classics; investigation of language is here essentially only a secondary matter, a means of apprehending the right sense. — With full recognition of this deep significance of philology, yet only as an auxiliary science, I shall endeavour in my lectures to direct the necessary attention to it also. It is my intention in future to explain our old runes, principally with the intention of making us acquainted with the religion, the customs, the manner of life, in short with the whole intellectual life of our forefathers. Thereby our knowledge of language will indeed also be promoted, but properly only for practical need. The so-called criticism of variants or conjectural criticism, which is so important a constituent part of classical philology, will on the other hand not be a particularly essential object of our investigations.

The study of languages in its highest, scientific signification bears the name of linguistics, and its purpose is language itself as such. While philology finds its nourishment only in languages that have a literature, every language, on the other hand, can become an object of linguistics, and commonly precisely those languages which lack all literature are the most interesting in a linguistic respect, since they have best preserved their original character. The linguist has therefore nothing to do with literature, but his whole endeavour must be directed to investigating the nature and the essence of language itself. Here, however, one circumstance is to be carefully noted, namely: while the philologist can restrict himself to the domain of one language, the linguist must seek to acquire the greatest possible universality. In this respect Schleicher remarks very aptly: "the zoologist must have an exact survey of the whole animal kingdom, even if he should have made only a single family the object of his studies; just so the linguist cannot dispense with the most extended knowledge of languages possible; he too needs a survey of the whole domain of language, even in order to investigate only one language as the standpoint of his science demands. Just as the beings of nature, so too languages form a graduated series; every higher stage includes all the lower in itself, contains them as sublated moments; how is it then possible to assign a place to a language and to comprehend its essence rightly without having the whole soul of linguistic development before one's eyes? Not so the philologist: complete familiarity with few, indeed with a single language suffices for him, whereas the consideration of several languages is so much a requirement of linguistics that one is accustomed, not unjustly, to regard it as an outward mark of this discipline and to use language comparison as a synonym of linguistics." "The philologist," Schleicher continues, "resembles the farmer who tills a fruitful and rich field with a pair of horses; it suffices for him if he knows practically how to handle his horses well; with their peculiarities he must therefore be completely familiar. The linguist, on the other hand, resembles the zoologist, who needs quite a different knowledge of the species equus caballus than the farmer, which he can acquire only through the study of many kinds of animals, but for that reason need not precisely be skilled in the use of the same." [Footnote: See Schleicher: Die Sprachen Europas in systematischer Uebersicht [The Languages of Europe in Systematic Survey], Bonn 1850, pp. 4 and 5.]

Linguistics, or comparative study of languages, is, as is well known, only one science, but nevertheless its adherents have already been able to divide into two camps. There is a comparative study of languages which bears the name of the historical, and another which calls itself the philosophical, the speculative. The adherent of the historical direction restricts himself to a domain where he has to do only with materially related languages, with such languages as belong to the same stock and have a phonetic relationship with one another. His endeavour is directed to penetrating into the original essence of these languages and to showing how they have changed according to definite laws and in the course of time have assumed a different form. With regard to this attention to the process of development of language, this kind of linguistics has received the name of the historical. But one of its most excellent peculiarities consists in this, that, as was said, it builds its results upon the phonetic relationship which subsists between the languages belonging to the same stock.

As regards, on the other hand, the linguistic investigation which calls itself philosophical, this too is of a comparative nature; it does not, however, concern itself with the phonetic relationship of languages among themselves, but looks essentially only to the concept and seeks to set forth its agreement in different languages. It also does not restrict itself to a certain number of more or less related languages, but embraces all human languages, regarded as a system closed in itself. — In order to gain a clear conception of this kind of linguistic investigation, I will here adduce Pott's treatment of the numeral. He has made the observation that the simple numerals close in some languages with 5, in others with 10, in still others with 20. [Footnote: A. F. Pott: Die quinare und vigesimale Zählmethode bei Völkern aller Welttheile [The Quinary and Vigesimal Method of Counting among Peoples of All Parts of the World], Halle 1847.] On this basis he divides all human languages, according to the different methods of counting, into three classes. It may indeed happen here that two phonetically related languages belong to different classes, but to that the philosophical investigator of language naturally cannot pay regard, since he has to do only with the concept, not with the sound.

Of the two kinds of linguistics, or comparative study of languages, just named, the latter, the philosophical or speculative, cannot become the object of our considerations. It is a peculiar, comprehensive science, which demands its own special cultivators. As we have just presented the general essence of this philology, it is clear that its results must rest upon a comparative consideration of all languages known hitherto. But since we have here properly to do only with a single language, philosophical linguistics stands naturally wholly and entirely outside the limits of our activity. Moreover this linguistics still hovers so much in the blue that one cannot yet have any real confidence in its results. The diversely formed organisms of the countless languages are still too little investigated to form a basis for a philosophy of language. One also sees clearly that all philosophical doctrines of language, from Monboddo down to Becker and W. von Humboldt, are built less upon an insight into the nature of languages than upon the philosophical systems valid at each time. These doctrines of language are not therefore to be rejected, for in any case they contain the highest knowledge of the time concerning language; but as regards our time, speculation is not its weak side. It almost seems as if the philosophical system hitherto valid had played out its role and as if another were in the making. Wherever one turns one's glance, one sees the men of science busy collecting facts and ever again new facts. One does not trouble oneself much about combinations, one does not concern oneself to draw results — now only facts count. As well as at any time one also now sees that an aggregate of isolated facts does not suffice to found a science in the higher sense, but the measure of the new facts seems not yet to be full in order to form new systems. However this may be, it is at least settled that the sciences in our time have a predominantly material direction, and so it is also with linguistics or comparative study of languages. Especially historical linguistics, which builds its results upon sounds, words, word-forms and other material elements, is now advancing with rapid strides. This discipline has indeed hitherto had chiefly only the Indo-European languages in view, but generally one already perceives among linguists the need of a more comprehensive investigation of languages. Especially in recent time the Finnish, the Turkish and other languages related to them have found several workers, of whom I will name only Gabelentz, Schott, Böhtlingk, Wiedemann, Böhrig. In this field I too feel most at home, and it would rejoice me if I could win among you, gentlemen, one or another adherent for this cause. What I believe I can assure from my own experience is that he who wishes to gain a scientific insight into the Finnish language can never sufficiently attain his goal if he does not throw himself into the arms of comparative study of languages. To others, however, who do not wish to devote themselves to science, I do not venture to recommend this study of language either, for it is a toilsome branch of knowledge and requires, if it is to bear fruit, almost the strenuous activity of a whole life.

There is still another branch of knowledge which I hold myself obliged, both from my own inclination and for the sake of the matter itself, to make the object of my lectures, namely ethnography. This is a new name for an old thing. By it one understands the science of the religion, the constitution, the manners and customs, the manner of life, the dwellings of peoples, in a word the science of all that belongs to their inner and outer life. One might regard ethnography as a part of the history of civilization, but not all nations have a history in the higher sense; rather, their history consists precisely only in ethnography.

I mentioned in the foregoing that ordinary philology is an auxiliary science of history, and that it was my intention, in explaining our ancient runes, to make use of this auxiliary science in order thereby to throw light upon the oldest history of the Finns. But the Finns too have for this time as yet no real history, but it is only the so-called ethnography that we can take into account in this connection. Unfortunately our runes no longer have their original character, but contain additions down to the most recent time and teem with modern ideas. A strict criticism is required in order to gain from this motley medley of old and new a clear insight into the original life of our forefathers. In most cases this can happen only by our comparing the ideas which occur in our old runes with those of related stocks which have still retained their pure, original nature. I am convinced that such a comparison can furnish equally important results for the ethnography of the Finns as comparative study of languages offers us with respect to the Finnish language. I will seek to explain this relation by a couple of examples. As is well known, the word jumala in our old runes has a double meaning. Sometimes it is used as a proper noun, which designates only one single, a certain, definite god; sometimes it occurs as an epithet of any god whatever, and even magicians are named jumalat. The question now is, which meaning is the original? And connected with this is another, more important question: did the Finns originally venerate one or several gods? To these questions our runes now give no satisfactory answer. But if we consider the ideas of our kindred stocks concerning the deity, they designate with a related word, which in the different languages sounds jumal, juma, num, etc., essentially a single divine being. This being, however, one naturally does not conceive as a free spirit, but it coincides with external nature. Accordingly num, according to the idea of the Samoyeds, is the heaven, the sea, the earth, the sun, the stars, and all that there is of beautiful and glorious in nature. Of such a kind was without doubt also the original idea of the Finns concerning the deity. Everything in nature which constituted the object of their veneration was in the beginning the same Jumala. But it belongs to the essence of the human spirit that during its development objects are separated ever more and more sharply from one another, and in consequence of this the Finns gradually began to conceive the Jumala of heaven as separate from the Jumala of the sea, of the sun, of the moon, etc. The different objects of nature were transformed into different deities, which all received their particular names, but Jumala became an epithet common to them all. — One will perceive the great importance and significance of comparative ethnography for the Finnish still more clearly from the following examples. It is related in our old songs that the three heroes of Kalevala: Väinämöinen, Ilmarinen and Lemminkäinen betook themselves one after another to Pohjola in order to bring home the beautiful daughter of Pohjola. Now Pohjola and Kalevala lived in the most violent enmity with one another, and in consequence of this no suitor had hope of success; rather, their surest prospect was that the bride whom they were to embrace in Pohjola could be nothing other than death. Nevertheless they persisted in their resolve to obtain the beautiful maiden as wife and undertook for this purpose ever new journeys. As these wooing journeys are depicted in the Kalevala, it was the beauty and grace of the maiden which moved our heroes even to stake their lives in order to see their wish fulfilled. This at last indeed succeeded for Ilmarinen, but soon his wife died, and he began to look about for another. Whither now did his mind turn? Again to Pohjola, the only ruinous Pohjola. And when he no longer had any prospect of obtaining a wife from thence, he made an attempt to forge himself a wife out of silver and gold. It thus seems as if Pohjola had been the only place where Kalevala's heroes could seek brides. But why would they not turn in this intention to the daughters of their own land? To this question only comparative ethnography can give a satisfactory answer. It is a general custom among all Finnish, Turkish, Mongolian, Tungus, Samoyed and other related peoples who have still retained their ancient customs, that marriages must not be contracted within one and the same stock, but, like our heroes of antiquity, so also at the present day every Samoyed, every Ostyak, etc., must, either by fair means or by foul, procure himself a wife from a foreign stock. This comparison furnishes us, moreover, with the important result that the Finns in former times were divided into different stocks, and one can assume with tolerable certainty that both their government and their whole constitution must have been approximately of the same character as they still are at the present day among the related peoples.

Comparative ethnography thus becomes indispensably necessary in order that we may rightly comprehend our old songs and the ideas of antiquity in general. At the same time it has, however, also another interest for us. In union with linguistics, comparative ethnography must furnish a decisive result in the question of the relationship of the Finnish people with the other stocks of peoples. Indeed there could hardly be any other sure way of getting on the track of this relationship than that which the comparison of the language, the religion, the manners and the manner of life of peoples offers. It is not unknown to me that the physiologists and anatomists believe the solution of this question to be reserved to themselves; one can, however, have no real confidence in their science as soon as it seeks to decide the relationship of peoples by comparison of skulls. I have even to some extent made myself acquainted with these their investigations, but they have not appeared to me very satisfactory. I will adduce some proofs of the blunders of which our newest and most famous physiologists have been guilty while they undertook with the aid of skulls to determine the relationship of different peoples. The famous Swedish physiologist and anatomist Retzius has recently published a brochure on the form of the skull of the inhabitants of the North. In this work the following peoples are adduced as craniologically related to one another: 1) Slavs, 2) Finns, and other Chud peoples, 3) Afghans, 4) Persians, 5) Turks, 6) Lapps, Yakuts, etc. This combination is surely so untenable and false that it deserves no refutation. Middendorff, physiologist at the St. Petersburg Academy of Sciences, seeks in a treatise to show that the Samoyeds on this side of the Urals have Finnish, but those beyond the Urals Mongolian skulls, and yet both Samoyed stocks are in language, manners and manner of life as closely related to one another as the Savonians and Ostrobothnians in Finland. H. v. Baer, one of the most famous physiologists of Europe, had some years ago received through Hofmann a Karagass skull from Siberia, with the ethnographical remark that the Karagasses were originally Samoyeds, but in consequence of their close contact with some Buryat stocks had adopted the language, manners and manner of life of the Mongols. In his craniological zeal H. v. Baer set himself to write an article on the Karagass skull, and proved as clear as sunlight that the corners of the eyes, the frontal bone, the nape, etc., were Samoyed, but the cheek-bones and certain other parts Mongolian. H. v. Baer consequently finds it, on the basis of his craniological investigations, very probable that the skull in question belonged to a Samoyed, yet not a genuine one, but to a Mongolized one. [Footnote: A. Retzius, Om Formen af Nordboernes Cranier [On the Form of the Skulls of the Inhabitants of the North] (separately reprinted from: Förhandlingar vid Naturforskaremötet i Stockholm år 1842), Stockholm 1843, p. 4. — "Ueber die Samojeden in St. Petersburg als Gegenstand ethnographischer Forschung" ["On the Samoyeds in St. Petersburg as an Object of Ethnographical Investigation"] (from a lecture at the meeting of the Imperial Russian Geographical Society on 5 March 1847) in the St. Petersburgische Zeitung 1847, Nos. 76 and 77.] Some years later I visited these Karagasses, and how great was not my astonishment when I found that they were neither Samoyeds nor Mongols, but only genuine Turks. [Footnote: Vergleichung eines von Herrn Obrist Hofmann mitgebrachten Karagassen-Schädels mit dem von Herrn Dr. Ruprecht mitgebrachten Samojeden-Schädel [Comparison of a Karagass Skull brought by Colonel Hofmann with the Samoyed Skull brought by Dr. Ruprecht] in Bulletin de la Classe physico-mathématique, Vol. III, pp. 477 ff.]

From what has been adduced one might already perceive that the physiologist with all his craniology or cranioscopy floats as upon a sea that leads astray, if the philologist and ethnographer do not guide his investigations. Perhaps the time will come when physiology too can furnish its contribution to the elucidation of the relationship of peoples; for the present, however, nothing can yet be built upon it. It is indeed true that linguistic investigation and ethnography too are not always satisfactory for this purpose, for how often has it not happened that smaller stocks of peoples have lost their nationality and have been assimilated by a mightier stock? But even in this case craniology can seldom give any information; rather, the investigator must then take refuge in history and in the monuments of antiquity. — Perhaps I too shall one day lead you, gentlemen, upon this path; this will, however, happen only in passing and with the greatest possible brevity.

Herewith I believe I have now pretty much indicated the branches of knowledge which I intend gradually to make the object of my lectures. I have sought to show how it is indispensably necessary for the scientific interest of Finnish philology that we do not always keep ourselves captive within the proper limits of Finland, but also seek to procure for ourselves information from other related stocks of peoples. Since these stocks of peoples, however, are for the greater part likely to be unknown to this audience, it is perhaps not inexpedient if, at the beginning, by way of introduction, I expatiate upon all the peoples which stand in a nearer or more remote relationship with the Finns and to which we shall in future have often enough to refer. With regard to the short time which we can now devote to this consideration, I shall seek to make my presentation as short as possible and shall touch principally only upon the historical relations of these peoples.

It has long been a disputed question to which race of man the Finnish stock must be reckoned. Blumenbach, the famous investigator of the different races of the human species, had, on the basis of his physiological investigations, arrived at the remarkable result that one half of our stock belonged to the Caucasian, the other, however, to the Mongolian race. The physiologists of the newest time have sought to correct this blunder, but have thereby, with respect to our real origin, fallen into no small embarrassment. Generally the view has sought to make itself valid among them that the Finns no less than the Turks belong to the Caucasian or the Indo-European race of peoples. This view was first expressed by Cuvier, but has later found numerous adherents among the physiologists. Against this, however, the philologists protest with all zeal. According to their investigations, the language of the Turks has more in common with the language of the Mongols, but deviates, on the other hand, in its whole structure from the Indo-European stock of languages. The same remark holds also of the different branches of the Finnish stock of languages, and with regard to this both Finns and Turks are reckoned by the philologists to the Mongolian race. Hither belong further, according to the views of the philologists, also the Tungus or Manchu, the Samoyed, and perhaps also some East Asiatic peoples whose languages are still too little known. All the peoples just named (the Finns, Turks, Samoyeds, Mongols, Tungus, etc.) I am accustomed to comprise under the name of the Altaic, since they all in grey antiquity seem to have inhabited that part of Asia which borders on the Altaic mountain chain. By other scholars these peoples are called Tatar, Turanian, Scythian, Ural-Altaic, etc. Some of these peoples undeniably stand also with respect to their language in a very remote relation to one another, and this holds in particular of the Finnish stocks on the one hand and the Mongolian and Tungus on the other. But in any case this classification of the peoples named seems to be acceptable precisely on the basis of linguistic agreement. I hope in future to be able to support this assertion with manifold proofs. This time I will principally only indicate one circumstance upon which the philologists lay particular weight in the question of the relationship of the Altaic languages; I mean the character of their agglutination. As will probably be known to some of you, gentlemen, all human languages are divided into three different classes: 1) into monosyllabic, 2) into agglutinative, and 3) into inflectional languages. The first-named class comprises Chinese and several other languages in south-eastern Asia. To the second belong, as I already mentioned, the Altaic or Tatar languages, further the Deccan, the Malay, many Indian languages in America, etc. The third class, finally, comprehends all Indo-European languages in itself. — I will attempt to communicate briefly a characterization of the general difference of these three classes of languages.

As regards the monosyllabic languages, they express in sound only the content or the meaning of words, but not their relations; that is, there are in them only word-roots, but they make no distinction between the different parts of speech, between noun, verb, particle, and still less have they different cases, moods and tenses, but all such relations must be expressed by the different position of the words in the sentence. If one wished to translate the sentence: "the tall emperor spoke to his army" into Chinese, one would obtain the following order of words: "tall emperor speak army." This translation is, however, incorrect in so far as we in our languages cannot omit to designate the different parts of speech. In Chinese there is no adjective tall, no verb speak, but one and the same word can express tall, height, to raise; language, to speak, speaker, etc. In the Indo-European languages there are, with few exceptions, only such words as express not only a given concept, but also a relation in which this concept appears, e.g. sun, to shine. Chinese and other monosyllabic languages, on the other hand, express by their words nothing other than only the general concept. "Here," says a famous German investigator of language, "the word is still not at all articulated; it is still undifferentiated strict unity, as in the realm of nature the crystal." [Footnote: Schleicher, Die Sprachen Europas, p. 7.]

In the agglutinative class of languages we find both the concept and its relations expressed by words, but also that part of the word which expresses the relation is here originally an independent, material word. The roots are also in this class monosyllabic; but to these roots other monosyllabic roots are attached by agglutination or composition, which, however, in some languages have gradually lost their material meaning and have received the determination to indicate only the formal relation. The general character of the agglutinative languages is accordingly the following: they can express both the concept and its relation by different sounds. Yet the sounds which serve for the expression of the relation are originally external constituents existing for themselves, or independent roots. In some languages, as e.g. in Mongolian and Manchu, the sounds called by the Germans relational sounds, the sounds which express formal relations, are in writing not even joined with the stem, but are designated as independent words. In ordinary pronunciation the individual constituents indeed melt together into one word, but this unity is not a really organic unity, but a unity brought about, so to speak, by adhesion.

The true, living unity, on the other hand, philologists have believed to find in the Indo-European or in the so-called inflectional languages. Here the concept and its relations do not appear as different constituents, but they form a single inseparable word. Some philologists, and among them Wilhelm von Humboldt, conceive this unity as one arisen through a free development from the word-stem itself, without external additions. As the plant develops from its root stem, leaf and blossom, so also the word is to have produced from its root the parts which serve to express the formal relations. Others, however, like Bopp, Pott, etc., are on the other hand of the opposite view and hold the opinion that in the inflectional languages too an agglutination has taken place. This view has indeed much in its favour, for exact investigations of the Indo-European languages have shown that in them too such sounds as express formal relations can be traced back to certain roots. — It seems to me altogether very acceptable that all languages were originally of the same character as Chinese still is now, i.e. monosyllabic. Stability forms the fundamental trait in the whole national character of the Chinese, and this quality appears also in the language. With other, more mobile nations, more accessible to civilization, language naturally could not become stable either, but developed together with civilization, whose expression language is. In the development of language, however, nothing is simpler, more natural and more ordinary than composition of words. This process continues still at the present day in the Indo-European languages, and essentially hereby new words are formed for the expression of new concepts. But precisely also through the same process, according to the present views of some investigators of language, all differences in human language structure can be explained. For, as I have just set forth, the so-called agglutination process is nothing other than composition, and according to Bopp's theory it is the same also with inflection. As a proof that the inflectional and agglutinative languages were originally monosyllabic, it can also be adduced that the roots in all languages originally consist of a single syllable. And if it be added that a multitude of roots in all languages of the world are related to one another, then it really seems as if agglutination and inflection were only a development of the monosyllabic structure of language, or as if the difference of the classes of languages rested not upon a difference of race, but upon the different degree of civilization which the individual peoples occupy.

But however one may conceive the relation of the three classes of languages to one another, it must nevertheless be conceded that the Altaic languages stand in a certain relationship to one another precisely through the character of their agglutination. This agglutination, however, is not so loose as in some other languages, but almost an inflection. The agglutinated words have here already for the most part lost their material meaning and have assumed the character of real affixes. Almost the only kind of affixes which in Turkish, Mongolian and Manchu have still retained their concrete or material meaning are the personal endings in the verbs; in the Finnish and Samoyed languages, however, these have assumed the character of inflectional endings, which express formal relations. The Altaic languages have accordingly, with respect to the character of their agglutination, a quite peculiar character. Besides this, these languages show a multitude of other more or less general formal agreements, as e.g. that the word tolerates no prefixes; that there are postpositions, not prepositions; that a rather large number of word similarities occurs; that umlaut is lacking and that the stem vowel determines the ending vowel; that two consonants never begin or close a word, and many other laws of euphony; as well as the most certain, that the case endings in the plural are not different from those of the singular.

In the foregoing I have adduced some grounds which entitle me to speak in one connection of the Tungus, Mongolian, Turkish, Finnish and Samoyed peoples, which peoples I have comprised under the common designation of the Altaic. I do not know whether this designation will gain currency in future; I find the same, however, in every respect far more fitting than the commonly adopted, indefinite name Tatars or Tartars. According to a fabulous statement of Abulghasi, Tatar is originally the name of a prince who was a brother of Mongol, both of whom came from Turkey. [Footnote: Histoire généalogique des Tatars, traduite du manuscrit tatare [Genealogical History of the Tatars, translated from the Tatar manuscript], Leyden 1736, p. 37. — Klaproth, Asia polyglotta, Paris 1831, pp. 302 ff.] In Chinese history the name Tatar occurs very often and refers, according to Klaproth, to the whole Mongolian stock, but according to the monk Hyakinth this name has not even so wide an extension. The last-named writer adduces in his work on Mongolia that the people which inhabits the present Chalcha land was named by the Chinese at the beginning of the 11th century Tatan and was divided into different clans, among which Tatar also occurs. Among the Mongols, however, such a custom is said to have prevailed that the stock to which the ruling dynasty belonged transferred its name to the whole people, and for this reason Chinggis Khan is said to have extended the name Tatan over the whole of Mongolia. [Footnote: Hyakinth, Записки о Монголии [Notes on Mongolia], Vol. I, St. Petersburg 1828, pp. 221 ff. — Asia polyglotta, p. 208.] As regards now the name Tatar, Hyakinth reports, as was said above, that the same among the Mongols never designated the whole people, but only a single clan. That Klaproth has given to the word Tatar the same meaning as Tatan rests, according to his view, upon a confusion of these two names. However this may be, it is nevertheless a settled matter that the word Tatar is a designation which originally referred only to a larger or smaller part of the Mongolian people. Later this designation passed over also to some Turkish peoples. For when the son of Chinggis Khan, Juchi Khan, formed the new empire of Kipchak, which was inhabited by Turkish stocks, the subjected peoples received, according to Klaproth, the name of their rulers and were called Tatars, a designation which these peoples themselves have never admitted. Meanwhile in Russia the name Tatars has continued to be used exclusively of all the Turkish stocks dwelling in the said empire. Ignorance of the true meaning of this name has given occasion that it has been used by scholars, and especially by the French, as a collective designation for the three great stocks of peoples of High Asia: for the Turks, Mongols and Tungus. By degrees also the Finnish and other stocks related to the peoples just named have been included under the same name. Accordingly the word Tatar now has a fivefold meaning. It designates namely: 1) the whole Mongolian people (according to Klaproth); 2) a Mongolian clan (according to Hyakinth); 3) the Turkish stocks dwelling in Russia; 4) the peoples of High Asia: Mongols, Manchu and Turks; 5) the whole Mongolian race. It is clear that so ambiguous a designation, leading to misunderstandings, is inadmissible in science. There is besides, for us Finns as for the Hungarians, also another ground for rejecting this designation. According to the popular manner of conception, Tatar is a word synonymous with barbarian, and the cause why in France Tatar has been made into Tartar is no other than that by the designation itself one wished to indicate the wild character of the Tatar peoples. But even if the Finns and Hungarians belong to the same race of peoples, the designation ought not to be defensible from the popular point of view either. For however low our civilization and that of the Hungarians may be rated by the other nations, nevertheless no one will now wish to reckon us among the barbarians.

The celebrated Danish philologist Rask has used for the designation of the peoples here discussed the denomination: Scythian race; this denomination has, however, found no adherents, since the name Scythians in Herodotus, Hippocrates and other Greek authors has a quite indefinite meaning. Without doubt the name Skythai with them also comprised peoples of High Asia; it was, however, at the same time also applied to other stocks foreign to the Greeks, whose nationality investigators of history have hitherto not been able to get on the track of. — We shall later have opportunity to treat this question more closely, and in doing so to set forth more fully the unsuitableness of the name Scythians as a designation of the peoples in question.

Just as little authority has the denomination Turanian peoples been able to acquire for itself. By what the Persians call Turan one properly understands only the western part of the high plateau of Asia bordering on the Caspian Sea, or also what is called by another name Tartary.



The Tungus Peoples

The land has since ancient times been inhabited by Turkish tribes.

And one easily sees that there must be an etymological relationship between Turks and Turan. The designation "Turanian peoples" is accordingly too narrow to comprise the whole race.

In view of the inadmissibility of the names cited above, we shall in what follows always use only the name Altaic peoples. This designation rests, as I have already shown in the preceding, properly on the fact that all the peoples belonging to this race lived concentrated around the Altai Mountains in grey antiquity. It has its opposite in the designation Caucasian race, which, however, is far less suitable to designate all the peoples for which it is used. The land around the Altai is still today inhabited by nothing but Altaic peoples, and it can be shown that all, even the most distant branches of this stock, earlier had their ancestral seat here. By contrast, the Caucasus has been only a point of passage for a multitude of the peoples belonging to the Caucasian race. Some of them have not even been in the neighbourhood of this mountain range, and now almost the whole Caucasian land is inhabited by nothing but Turkish tribes.

We pass herewith to the consideration of the various branches which belong to the Altaic racial stock. The easternmost of these branches form the so-called Tungus.

Fischer remarks of them [Footnote: Sibirische Geschichte. St. Petersburg 1768. Introduction p. 110.] that they are "a cheerful, lively people, endowed by nature with a good understanding," and there must indeed be great endowments in this people which, notwithstanding its relatively small number, has for two hundred years been able to rule the great Chinese Empire and to hold its numerous nations, among others the warlike hordes of the Mongols, superior in population, in check. — The branch of the Tungus which in 1644 took possession of China now bears the name Manchu, and their proper homeland is the north-eastern part of High Asia, the so-called Manchuria; but there are still numerous branches of the Tungus stock in eastern Siberia, where the Tungus live scattered at various places, e.g. in the region of Nerchinsk, on the Sea of Okhotsk as far as the Penzhina Bay, on the Lena, Angara, on the Yenisei, on the upper and lower Tunguska, and on many tributaries which fall into the Yenisei especially from the east. Like other peoples of High and North Asia, they fall into numerous smaller tribes or clans, of which each lives under its own chief in a relation of almost mutual independence with the others. Foreign tribes, however, have nothing in common among themselves, not even the name, but rob and plunder as enemies on each other's territory. Some of them call themselves Boje, which means human being; others Donki, people; Lamut (from lamu, sea), Owon or Otshonki, Chapogirs, and so on. By the Manchu all the other Tungus are named Orochon, which designates a reindeer owner. All the Tungus wandering about in Siberia originally came from Manchuria, from the fertile banks of the Amur. The number of Tungus subject to the Russian sceptre is, notwithstanding the great stretches of land which they occupy, very small, although it cannot be given in figures. In round numbers they are estimated at 50,000 souls, to which are added 2 to 3,000 Lamuts. The inhabitants of Manchuria, by contrast, are said to amount to about four million.

On the basis of their different way of life, the Russian Tungus are divided into Horse- or Cattle-Tungus, into Reindeer-Tungus and Dog-Tungus, beside which one also hears speak of Steppe- and Forest-Tungus. As these names already indicate, this people leads, like almost all natives of Siberia, a nomadic way of life. There are among them indeed also many settled families which have adopted the customs and way of life of the Russians; the by far greater part, however, consists of nomads. Those who nomadize on the steppes dwell in tents and wander about with their herds, which consist of horses, cattle, sheep, and so on. Their way of life agrees entirely with the way of life of the Buryats, and many of them are also with respect to their religion Lama worshippers. Among these Tungus, who for the most part dwell in the district of Nerchinsk, there are also a number of Cossacks who, in common with the Buryat and Russian Cossacks, attend to the guarding of the Chinese frontier. To the Lamuts belong also fishermen, though their number is relatively very small. The greater part of the Tungus roaming about in Siberia are hunters or so-called Forest-Tungus, whose principal property consists in reindeer and dogs. There are, however, such as possess little other goods than their bow, their rifle, and their sword or the so-called palma, and these Klaproth [Footnote: Asia polyglotta p. 289.] calls Tungus on foot — a designation for whose correctness I will not, however, vouch. These Tungus are the purest, most ideal hunting people that dwells in the wastes of Siberia. "It is," says Fischer [Footnote: Sibirische Geschichte. Introduction p. 111 ff.] of these hunter-Tungus in his outdated German, "a people quite peculiar in its way of life, and distinguishes itself thereby from all other peoples of the earth. Other peoples have secure and permanent dwelling places, and build themselves houses or huts. Others indeed roam about in the steppes and nourish themselves from their cattle breeding, but yet remain at one place as long as their circumstances permit, and keep together in community. But the Tungus do not concern themselves with social life, know nothing of huts or other permanent dwellings, also do not stay longer than one or a couple of nights at one place, wander about in the forests and on the mountains, and hold it for their highest good, like other animals on the earth, to rove about under the open sky and in their natural freedom with their reindeer." Even if this description should look somewhat exaggerated, it must nevertheless be admitted that at least in Siberia there is no people so restlessly roaming about and so stripped of all comforts of life as the hunter-Tungus. Like the other savages of Siberia they indeed have their tents; they do not, however, always drag them about with them, but often roam for whole months through forest and field without having any other shelter than that which they find in the bosom of a snowdrift, at the foot of a tree, or in a rocky cleft. Perhaps it is in part precisely this mobile life, filled with dangers and adventures, which has given the Tungus more strength and energy than one finds in all the other natives of Siberia. The Tungus are a quick, nimble, and fearless people. Skilfully they fight even with wild animals, whose most dangerous enemies they are. Moreover they love, in contrast to other Siberian peoples, dance, play, and in general a cheerful life. They dress with the greatest care, adorn themselves with tinsel and beads, tattoo their face and their hands, and apply themselves to all possible elegance. In religion they are devoted to Shamanism.

Like the Russian Tungus, the Chinese also have scattered and divided into various tribes. One such tribe is formed among others by the above-named Manchu, after whom afterwards the whole of Manchuria received its name from the fact that the first founder of the present Chinese dynasty named the whole people after his own tribe Manchu. The ancestors of the Manchu tribe are still very young. They are nowhere named in the older Chinese history; by contrast various other Tungus tribes are mentioned, of which some have played a very significant role in the history of China. The first report which the Chinese give about the inhabitants of present-day Manchuria dates from the 11th century before Christ and refers to the powerful tribe Suchin, still continuing today, whose name is evidently no other than the present Judshi, Juchi, Jutshin, Adshi. From this report, however, one learns nothing more important than that the named Suchin brought the Chinese arrows from a wood which were provided with points of hard stone. [Footnote: J. H. Plath, Geschichte des östlichen Asiens. First Part. Die Mandschurei. Göttingen 1830. pp. 75 ff.]

From this time on, the Chinese chroniclers observe with regard to the inhabitants of Manchuria a complete silence, which continues almost uninterruptedly for several centuries. Only in the year 263 after Christ's birth does a more detailed communication occur, which concerns the same tribe, although it appears there under the name Yleu or Ylou. In the named year the Yleu are said to have sent to the dynasty Goey ruling in China a tribute which consisted of arrows, bows, armour, and sable skins. The land which the Yleu inhabited is said to have been excessively mountainous and the climate extraordinarily cold. The inhabitants, however, pursued agriculture. Their customs were crude. They had neither princes nor chiefs, but their villages, which lay in the midst of forests and in the mountains, were ruled by elders. Many of them kept themselves in subterranean caves, had neither cattle nor sheep, but a great quantity of pigs, whose flesh they used for food, the skin, however, for clothing. In winter they smeared their body with fat in order to protect themselves against the cold; in summer they went naked, only clad with an apron. It was a most unclean, stinking people which never washed itself. Writing they did not have; their word counted for them instead of contracts. When they began their meal, they used first to tread the flesh with feet, and if it was frozen, they sat upon it in order to make it tender and soft. Salt and iron were lacking in their land; the former they replaced by an ash lye. Both men and women wear braids. If one wished to marry, he adorned the head of his beloved with feathers and paid the dowry; but whether his betrothed was a virgin or not troubled him little. Only the strong, vigorous youth counted for something; old age was despised. On the very day of death the dead was buried in the field in a coffin of boards; a dead pig was laid on his grave for food. They had an ugly, cruel character, without compassion for their equals, not even for their parents and relatives. Thieves were killed, without regard to the value of the stolen goods. One carried weapons of an unusual size: the bows were four feet long, the arrows one foot eight inches, and with points of a green, hard stone and poisoned. [illegible] of skins covered with bones were their covering. Although this armament made them terrible to their neighbours, they nevertheless seem never to have gone out on conquests, but always to have quietly inhabited their land.

In the fifth century, under the dynasty Goey, a people dwelling in Manchuria is called by the Chinese Mu-ky, which name later alternated with Mo-kho or Mo-ho and according to Klaproth [Footnote: Asia polyglotta p. 266. Tableaux historiques de l'Asie pp. 85 ff.] is identical with Mongol. It dwelt north of Korea, in a land which was flowed through by the Su-mo, i.e. the Songari, and consisted of several hordes. In older times there occurred seven hordes; in the eighth century, however, only two, of which the one stayed on the Songari, the other on the Amur or Hei-shui. Of the so-called Mu-ky the Chinese relate that they inhabited a poor and damp land. They kept themselves, like the Yleu, in subterranean caves, whither one descended on steps; small earth walls surrounded their dwellings. They had neither cows nor sheep, but only horses and a great number of pigs. They built wheat and other kinds of grain, also legumes, and understood the art of preparing from the ground grain an intoxicating drink. They were a very unclean people, since they washed hands and face with urine. At marriage the wife brought skirts of cloth, the man clothes of pig skins; on his head he had the tail of a tiger or leopard. The first night the man went into the house of his newly married wife and sucked at her breast. If someone reported to the man the unfaithfulness of his wife, he killed her on the spot, but immediately thereafter also him who had reported his dishonour to him; therefore adultery mostly remained hidden. They were likewise good archers and hunters. Their arrows they poisoned, and the poison which they prepared in the seventh and eighth months was so strong that the steam of boiling [illegible] could kill a human being. If their parents died in spring or summer, they buried them on heights and built a little house over the grave against rain and wetness. But if they died in autumn or winter, they made use of the corpses as bait for the martens.

North of the Amur River, in the northernmost parts of present-day Manchuria and in Siberia, there dwelt in the fifth century a tribe which in the Chinese annals is named Chy-Goey. The Chinese make a distinction between southern and northern Chy-Goey. Of the northern Chy-Goey it is said that they inhabited an extraordinarily cold land. They had tame reindeer, made use of sledges, kept themselves in earth caves, partly also in tents of tree bark. Their principal occupation was hunting and fishing. In their land there was a rich supply of sables and greywork. The former, by contrast, the southern Chy-Goey, inhabited a low, swampy, cold, and wooded land, where they were plagued by wild animals and mosquitoes. They had huts of reeds and at the same time also felt tents on wagons which were drawn by oxen. They spoke the language of the Moho, and small pieces of wood served them instead of writing, since laid in different ways they signified different things. They had few horses and no sheep, but many pigs and cattle. They prepared a kind of brandy with which they intoxicated themselves. If one wished to marry, he had to come to an understanding with the parents of the girl and send them oxen and other cattle for the bride. The widow belonged to the deceased husband and was not allowed to marry again.

Among the southern Chy-Goey there was a tribe Khitan, of which one does not rightly know whether it consisted of Tungus or perhaps of Mongols. Originally it dwelt north of Leao-tung and the Yellow River, which in Mongolian is called Schara muren, in Chinese Hoang-ho. Later they were, in consequence of attacks which were committed partly by the Hiungnu, partly by the Chinese, forced to change their dwelling places several times in succession and to submit to the sovereignty of their enemies, namely the Chinese and Hiungnu. In the seventh, eighth, and ninth centuries they rose incessantly against the Chinese, and although they continually lived in a dependent condition, their power nevertheless increased with great strides. In the ninth century there arose among them a man named Apaokhi, who became the founder of the afterwards so powerful Khitan empire. Apaokhi was, legend relates, already before his birth called to this task. His mother had in a dream seen a sun fall down into her lap; a divine light surrounded the house and the most beautiful perfumes scented it. Already at his birth Apaokhi had the form of a three-year-old child and could walk with the help of his hands. The mother raised the miracle child with all care in a special tent, and after three months the boy could already stand on his feet. When he was one year old, he spoke and foresaw future things; superterrestrial beings surrounded him, according to his statement, and served him as guardians. When in the year 901 he was appointed Chaghan, China was already weakened by inner disputes and divided among several different rulers. This condition continued for over 50 years, and during this time the Khitan dynasty was already founded. This empire is also called the empire Liao, since the Khitan tribe stayed on a river of the same name. This empire comprised the whole of Mongolia and Manchuria, extended from the ocean in the east to Kashgar in the west, from the Hingan Mountains in the north to the Chinese Wall in the south. From time to time northern China also was subject to the rule of the Khitan. The Khitan empire continued for 218 years, from 907 to 1125, and was then destroyed by the Golden Empire or the Kin dynasty.

While the Khitan played so significant a political role in the history of High Asia, their culture must naturally also progress to some extent. Properly the progress of this culture consisted only in the fact that the Khitan arranged themselves entirely according to the model of the Chinese. They adopted, like other conquerors of China, the Chinese state constitution, and also the religion of the Chinese asserted itself with them. Of their language one knows nothing more precise. They are said to have been in possession of a writing which was invented in 920; however, no statement occurs about its nature. They are also said to have had a history of their people which was composed in their own written language, but now, as far as one knows, exists only in Chinese translation.

The above-named Kin dynasty proceeded from the already mentioned tribe of the Judshi or Njudshi, which is of Tungus origin. Of this tribe there were two hordes, of which the one bore the name of the peaceful Njudshi, the other that of the wild. The Kin dynasty proceeded from the latter. The ancestral father of this dynasty is said to have been Hian-phu or Sian-phu; the empire of the Kin, however, was founded by Agutha, after he had before destroyed the empire of the Khitan. The empire of the Kin was under Agutha's successors more and more extended by conquests within the territory of China, which continued until the year 1141, when China obligated itself to pay a yearly tribute. Although the Kin dynasty thus ruled China, it nevertheless adopted the religion and constitution of the Chinese. The empire of the Kin existed approximately 100 years, or until 1235, when it on its part was again destroyed by Chingis-Chan, or properly by his successor Ogotai, Oktai.

Since this time the Tungus tribes obeyed first the sovereignty of the Mongols and then of the Chinese, until the Manchu dynasty again appeared. This dynasty also derives its origin from the wild Njudshi and has developed from a small beginning to the greatness that it gradually subjected to itself not only China proper, but also the whole of Mongolia, Manchuria, Tibet, Little Bukharia, Dzungaria, and so on. The Manchu dynasty founded its rule in the year 1634, when Thai-tsung conquered Peking and had himself proclaimed Emperor of China. Since this time the Manchu dynasty has uninterruptedly been in possession of China's throne and has ruled the colossal empire with as great strength as prudence. It hardly needs to be remarked that also the Manchu dynasty, which originally consisted of wild barbarians, has throughout appropriated Chinese civilization. In general all foreign tribes which in the course of time have penetrated into China have the common fate of being assimilated by the Chinese. Some scholars are of the opinion that the numerous inundations by wild hordes which China has suffered have had as their purpose to rejuvenate the Chinese softened under the sun of the south. Perhaps this view also has its true side; yet this rejuvenation has been of no thoroughgoing nature, for the invading hordes have never implanted any new civilization on China, but rather at all times adopted Chinese culture. The author of the history of Manchuria, Plath, expresses himself hereon in the following manner [Footnote: Loc. cit. pp. 87-88.]: "What one fantasizes together about the softening of the southerners and accordingly succeeding subjugation by the more vigorous northerners has no sense at all. Such an inundation and to a certain extent renewal and rejuvenation from the ground up, as France or Italy, e.g., suffered through the Germanic peoples, China has never experienced, even not at the time of the Mongol and Manchu rule. A multitude of the most foreign tribes have settled in its bosom; when China was torn by inner parties, it could indeed happen that its influence outwardly was weakened, and up into its borders — but always under Chinese influence and Chinese co-operation — powerful empires formed; also even in its interior, summoned and supported by Chinese, foreign ruling families have often been able to attain rule over individual parts and even over all China, but this was hardly otherwise than when a foreign military leader became Roman emperor. China has always remained China. The foreigners whom it took up, regardless of whether as rulers or as subjects, it has amalgamated with itself, so that they fitted themselves to its customs, institutions, and laws, or they have sooner or later been expelled again as incompatible."

I have in the preceding cited some data for the illumination of the ethnographic and historical relations of the Chinese Tungus and of the whole Manchu land. There has been talk of various tribes, and especially of such as have formed great and powerful dynasties, like the Judshi with their dynasties Kin and Manchu; Chy-Goey, which formed the dynasty Khitan; Mu-ky or Mokho and Yleu or [illegible]. As we have seen, the reports of the Chinese about these tribes are so scanty that one cannot even determine with certainty to which nation each of them belonged. Not much richer also are the reports which one has received about them through the Catholic missionaries — the Jesuits — and Russian Cossack expeditions. By means of them, however, one is led to the result that there are in present-day Manchuria several different nationalities. Thus the island Tarakai or Sakhalin, partly belonging to Manchuria, partly to Japan, as it has been named through a misunderstanding of the Jesuits, is inhabited by Kurils or Ainos, and of the same origin are also, according to Klaproth [Footnote: Asia polyglotta pp. 300 ff.], those called by the Russians Gilyaks or Gilyaki and Nalki (the Chedshen and Fiaka of the Chinese), which dwell on the lower course of the Amur and on the islands of the sea. In the northern parts of the land there are at present also Yakuts, who are of Turkish origin, and Buryats, which belong to the Mongolian stock; these peoples, however, have only in later time immigrated hither from Siberia. In the eastern part of the land, in the province Hing-King, a tribe Koelka-Tatars is named, which also seems originally to have been of Turkish origin, but later adopted the Manchu language. Of genuine Tungus tribes there are named by the Cossacks and missionaries: in the eastern part of the land the above-mentioned Njudshi, who are called by the Russians Ducheri, the Daurs and actual Tungus; further in the province Hingking the Manchu, Ilan-chala and the just-touched-on Koelka-Tatars, and in the western parts of the land Solons, Taragusins, and Daurs.

With respect to the customs and way of life of these tribal peoples, there shows itself in different parts of the land a great diversity. There are among them fishermen, hunters, herdsmen, and agriculturalists. To the fishermen belong especially the inhabitants of the island of Tarakai and their presumed tribal relatives, the before-named Gilyaks and Nalki. With hunting various tribes occupy themselves; most remarkable in this respect, however, may be the so-called Solons, which are descendants of the Njudshi. Of their hunting journeys the missionaries report that both men and women set out on the first of October for the sable hunt. They



The Mongols

They wear short, close-fitting coats of wolf skins, have a cap of the same fur on their heads, and a bow on their backs. Some of the horses they take with them carry sacks of millet and the long coats of fox or tiger skins with which they protect themselves against the cold, especially at night. They also have hunting dogs that know how to track game. Neither the cold of winter, which freezes all the rivers, nor the danger of meeting tigers, nor the death of their companions can prevent them from undertaking these dangerous enterprises, for through them they obtain all their wealth. There are, however, among the Solons also many who occupy themselves with agriculture and are provided with cattle, sheep, and camels. Most of the other tribes, such as the Daurians and their kindred the Targusins, Manchus, Ilan-Chala, and Koelka-Tatses, practise both cattle-breeding and agriculture; some also engage in trade and mining. As regards their religion, all the inhabitants of Manchuria are given to shamanism.

The nearest western neighbours of the Manchus in High Asia form the great and powerful stock that now bears the name of the Mongols and extends from Siberia in the north to China in the south, from Manchuria in the east to the so-called High Tartary in the west. But the Mongol stock is not restricted merely to the borders of High Asia; powerful branches of it also occur in Russia and in southern Siberia. The whole Mongol stock is usually divided into three branches: the Eastern Mongols, the Buryats, and the Kalmyks. Of these, the Eastern Mongols live within the borders of central High Asia just indicated. The Buryats inhabit the southern parts of the government of Irkutsk in Siberia, and especially the country around Lake Baikal. As for the Kalmyks, one part of them lives in High Asia near Koko Nor and Ili, while another has gone into European Russia and is found on the banks of the Yaik, the Volga, and the Don. All these stocks divide again into a multitude of larger or smaller hordes, which we shall briefly touch upon in what follows.

The Mongols are by nature a sluggish and phlegmatic people; they do not have the quickness, cleverness, and energy that distinguish their neighbours, the Tungus or Manchus, but love a quiet, still, and peaceful life. Of all the peoples of High Asia, the Mongols are at present the most powerful. It would without doubt be in their power to annihilate the Manchu dynasty utterly and make themselves masters of China; but they do not strive for this honour, and think they can reconcile themselves quite well to their fate when they are called China's allies and are in fact its vassals. Precisely their inborn sluggishness makes this nation less dangerous to the Chinese; but that they have the strength to accomplish immeasurable things, the warlike hosts of Genghis Khan and Tamerlane have given mankind a bloody testimony. The Mongols are known to have founded the greatest empire there ever was on earth; they conquered all Asia and made themselves masters of a great part of Europe, which managed with great difficulty to rescue its civilisation from their barbaric attack. Such an empire they will certainly never found again; in the future Europe will have nothing to fear from their devastations. But China and all Asia will learn sooner or later that among the Mongols every man is a warrior. One circumstance, however, which serves in a high degree to protect the world in future from an invasion of the Mongols is that they are now zealous adherents of Buddhism, a religion which, like Christianity, teaches peace and reconciliation. Klaproth supposes that it was precisely through the influence of this teaching that the Mongols in the last few centuries have become so peaceful, pious, and submissive a people.

This supposition certainly has its deep truth; but since even the adherents of Christianity and the most civilised nations of the world do not shrink from advancing with fire and sword, it cannot be supposed that the Mongols have forsworn the wild game of war forever. It is much more probable that their patience will sooner or later come to an end, and that then the threads of the net in which the subtle policy of the Chinese has now snared them will be torn asunder in an instant. Chinese policy has been directed toward weakening the power of the Mongols by every possible means. Their strong hordes have been split up, Manchus have come into the country, and the highest offices are in the hands of the latter; the Mongols have borne all this with long-suffering, but I do not believe that the system will hold in the long run. It needs only a man like Genghis Khan or Tamerlane, and the walls of China will collapse together as if struck by a thunderbolt. But let us let the future look after itself and cast only a fleeting glance into the past.

As for the earliest fortunes of the Mongol stock, they are wrapped in impenetrable darkness. There is indeed a history of the Eastern Mongols by a native author, Sanang Setsen Chungtaidshi, but although this history begins with the creation of the world, one obtains from it down to the times of Genghis Khan little more than fabulous traditions. Of the same character are also the reports in the well-known work which, at the end of the thirteenth century by order of Ghazan Khan, a successor of Genghis Khan in Persia, was composed about the peoples of Asia by Rashid al-Din, Ghazan Khan's secretary. From it Abulghasi and other Mohammedan historians drew their reports, which in this way must be equally unreliable. The surest source even for the earliest history of the Mongols is therefore the Chinese chronicles. These name as the first powerful people in present-day Mongolia the so-called Hiongnu.

But in the meagre annals of the Chinese one finds no reliable statement whatever about the nationality of this people, and what more recent investigations have determined in this respect is not very satisfying. Deguignes, the famous author of the History of the Huns and Turks, regards them as Turks, and Klaproth, Ritter, and several of the greatest authorities of the present day share the same view. Against this, the clergyman Hyacinth has set up the hypothesis that these Hiongnu were of Mongol origin; Neumann also holds this view, but neither he nor Hyacinth has been able to adduce reasons for his view, whereas Deguignes and Klaproth have for the opposite opinion especially the support that of the few known words of the Hiongnu language some are of Turkish origin. It may in consequence indeed be regarded as a settled matter that at least a part of the Hiongnu people was Turkish; but it is very probable that the empire of the Hiongnu in its flowering time comprised not only Turks but also Mongols and Manchus, and even Finnish stocks as well. Whether the ruling people consisted of Turks or Mongols must remain undecided for the present. [Footnote: Allgemeine Geschichte der Hunnen und Türken. Introduction. Equally obscure is also the question of the origin of the western Huns. Deguignes regards them as a branch of the Hiongnu people and consequently lets them count as Turks. Neumann likewise conceives the Hiongnu and Huns as synonymous designations, and in that case the Huns would naturally have been Mongols. A third view of the origin of the Huns is expressed by Klaproth, according to which the western Huns have nothing in common with the Hiongnu of the Orient, but form a separate stock and are Finns. Klaproth's view indeed has much to be said for it, but it cannot yet be regarded as proved. I intend in future to make the question of the Hiongnu and Huns the subject of special investigations and for this reason will not touch upon their fortunes here, especially those which, at least according to my present views, do not properly belong to Mongol history. I shall of course return to the so-called Hiongnu on the occasion of the Turkish stocks; but as for the Huns, I shall probably not be able to consider them more closely this time. [Footnote: Tableaux historiques, p. 243.]

Besides the Hiongnu, the Chinese annalists mention, as I have already remarked above, in the fifth century a people Muky, Mokho, or Moho, which seems to have belonged to the Mongol stock and originally inhabited the country north of Korea. In the tenth century, under the Tang dynasty, the name Munggu or Munggus already occurs, and in 1135 the Mongols are known under their present name Mungku or Mungkus. This name, however, occurs very rarely in the Chinese annals. More usual is the designation Tata (Ta-ta-öl), which is first encountered in the year 880. In later times the Chinese, out of ignorance of the different languages and of the nationality of foreign peoples, applied this name also to designate other High Asian peoples, and it is therefore doubtful how much of all that the Chinese annals relate about the Tata is applicable to the Mongols. Nevertheless I will here adduce some of the data that have been obtained from Chinese sources concerning the Tatars and Mongols. [Footnote: Tableaux historiques de l'Asie, p. 154 ff. — Asia polyglotta, p. 266.]

When the Khitan stock among the Tungus had grown in power and might, it attacked the above-mentioned Mokho and defeated them in a bloody engagement in the year 824 after the birth of Christ. Thereupon the Mokho became in part vassals of the Liao dynasty, in part took to flight and scattered in central High Asia. The greatest part of the latter moved west of the Hoangho into the Inshan mountains and the country of Tangut. Some time afterward, in 874, an uprising was brought about in China by Chuan-Tschao; the insurgents, however, suffered a great defeat in 880 and their leader Li-khe-yung had to seek his salvation in flight. Meanwhile the revolt continued and had the result that Chuan-Tschao took both capitals. In his distress the court in China then resolved to pardon the former leader of the rebels, Li-khe-yung, and to appoint him leader against them. Li-khe-yung now gathered auxiliary troops among the Tatars in the Inshan, among whom he had stayed during his flight, and won a brilliant victory over Chuan-Tschao, whereupon he settled with his Tatars in the Chinese province of Shan-si and was rewarded with a royal dignity. From this time (880) the above-cited designation Tata occurs for the first time in Chinese history as the name of the wild hordes in the Inshan descended from the Mokho. Later this designation became quite common among the Chinese and received an ever greater and greater extension, although it did not displace other names of the same peoples. [Footnote: Asia polyglotta, p. 204 ff.]

As for the Mongols proper, they seem down to the time of Genghis Khan to have played no significant part in history, but to have lived mostly as vassals of the above-mentioned Tungus dynasties of the Khitan and the Kin. That their power was nevertheless increasing at that time is reported by the Chinese imperial geography under the Ming dynasty in the eleventh century, for it mentions expressly among the stocks that had become powerful the Mungku (Mongols), Taitschdu (Taichigod, also a Mongol stock), and Kelie (Kerait), which seems to have been a Kalmyk stock identical with the Turgut. All these hordes were united by Genghis Khan and later formed the basis of the mighty world empire that he founded. Genghis Khan himself derived his origin from the Tata stock, or, as it is more correctly named, from the Black Tatar stock. Besides this, other Tatar stocks are already mentioned in his time. Thus the Chinese then knew White Tatars, who were probably Turks, Water Tatars or Manchus, Wild Tatars, and so on. Of these peoples, the so-called Black Tatars or Mongols long obeyed the Turks or the White Tatars, but Genghis Khan's father, Jesugei, shook off their yoke after he had first united all the Tatars belonging to his stock, namely the Black Tatars, into one people. It is related that the White Tatars were earlier tributary to the Tungus Khitan dynasty and later to the Kin dynasty. The same was also the lot of the Black Tatars or Mongols, for although they had overpowered their masters, the White Tatars, they nevertheless still had continually to submit to pay a tribute to the Tungus dynasties.
