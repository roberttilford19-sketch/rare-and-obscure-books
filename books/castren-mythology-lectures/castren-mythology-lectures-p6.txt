Alten Bürtjük seeks her boy for several days and also comes to the place where the six men stand under the rock and play. When Alten Bürtjük saw their game, she suspected that these men had killed her son. Then she returned to the tent and wept for her lost child. The whole day she wept, and only late at night did she fall asleep. On awakening she saw beyond the sea an extraordinary building. All the people wondered at this great building, and no one knew what to think of it. The day passed, and all went to rest. But when they awoke in the morning, half of the people and cattle had been carried over to the great building beyond the sea. Now Alten Bürtjük suspected that all this had been brought about by the six men whom she had seen playing on the seashore. When evening comes again, Alten Bürtjük transforms herself into an "iron swallow" and flies over the sea to the great building. From there a falcon flies toward her. Alten Bürtjük fears the falcon and wants to flee from him; but the falcon asks her to halt and promises to do her good. Alten Bürtjük halts; the falcon comes to her, has a copper fire-steel in its beak and a golden box in its claws. Then the falcon asks Alten Bürtjük to follow him; then flies over the sea and sits on a high rock. Then the falcon transforms itself into a man, and in him they recognized the same Tasol who had been thrown into the sea together with the boy by the six heroes. Alten Bürtjük also took her former shape again.

Sitting on the rock, Tasol speaks to Alten Bürtjük: "This fire-steel formerly belonged to Jedai-Chan and was stolen from him by the six heroes who played under the rock. With this fire-steel Jedai-Chan ruled all people, for every time one strikes with it, a hundred heroes are born. With it one can bring forth everything one wants: make bridges over the sea, split rocks, and so on. I have stolen this fire-steel from the six heroes, and thereby their power is at an end. In the golden box the soul of your son is kept. It was likewise put in by the six heroes, and I have taken the box from them." Then Tasol took the fire-steel, struck twice against the flint with it, and in an instant two hundred heroes arose. Tasol now commands the heroes to kill and burn the six men and to carry all the remaining people and all cattle over the sea. Hardly had this happened when Alten Kök came forth again from the earth and returned to his wife and his people. Then Tasol took the boy's soul from the box and made him too a man again. Tasol and Alten Kök now became brothers; Alten Kök gave him a horse, and then they lived their whole time in harmony.



7. Kan Mirgän, Komdei Mirgän and Kanna Kalas

Under a high mountain by the white sea stood an ulus; the steppe was full of people and full of cattle. Over this ulus ruled a hero, Kulate Mirgän, with a white-blue horse. His wife was called Kubäsen, and she had a daughter named Kubaiko and a son, Komdei Mirgän, with a white-brown horse. The girl Kubaiko was five years old, the son three years. This night they sleep. In the morning Kulate Mirgän rises, dresses, saddles his horse, and arms himself. His wife comes and asks him: "Where are you going, mighty hero?" The old man answers: "Our girl has grown for five years, and for so long I have not seen my cattle. It is finally time to go and hold reckoning over the cattle. My son has grown for three years, and during this whole time I have not looked after the people. Now I will go to look after my people too." So he spoke and set out on the journey. He traveled first through the open steppe and then comes onto a high mountain. From the mountain he looks down and sees his many people and his rich herds. He himself wonders and speaks: "How much people and how much cattle has God not granted me!"

While he stands and praises himself, his white-blue horse asks him what he stands and speaks with himself. Kulate Mirgän replies: "O my white-blue horse! Do you believe that anyone in this bright world of God has as much people and as much cattle as I?" The horse answers: "O my host Kulate Mirgän! There are heroes in the world who are far richer and stronger than you." Kulate Mirgän continues: "What heroes do you know who would be superior to me?" The horse answers: "Beyond nine lands from here lives a hero, Kalangar Taidji, with a piebald horse, and another, Katai-Chan, with a white-brown horse. They are both brothers and mighty heroes, both far superior to you. They also have a brother-in-law, Sokai Alten, with a tiger-spotted horse." "Today," the horse continues, "a message comes from them to you to demand tribute from you. They take tribute from all heroes in this land, and from you too they will henceforth demand tribute."

Kulate Mirgän thinks that he will never in his life pay tribute to any Chan, however mighty he may be. Then he rides home, but on the way, not far from home, he sees tracks of a hero's horse. He came home, and sitting in the saddle he calls his son and his daughter out to him. Then he asks them what hero had ridden to the ulus. They answered: "During your absence a mighty hero with a red-haired horse, named Kan Mirgän, rode to us; but we do not know who he is and where he has come from." The father continues to ask the son: "What did this hero say and announce to you?" — "He said," the son replies, "that two brothers, Kalangar Taidji with a white-gray horse and Katai-Chan with a white-brown horse, as well as their brother-in-law Sokai Alten with a tiger-spotted horse, demand tribute from him, and that he has fled from them for forty years because alone he would not be able to fight with the three mighty heroes. In union with you he trusts himself to stand up to the two hero-brothers and their brother-in-law. After he had said this, he rode away with the words: 'If a message comes from the two hero-brothers, do not tell the messenger that I have been here.'"

When Kulate Mirgän had heard this, he climbed from the saddle, and in the same moment the message from the two hero-brothers also came. When the messenger came, he screamed with such strength that Kulate Mirgän fell down together with his horse. The messenger asks: "Has Kan Mirgän with a red-haired horse been here?" Frightened, Kulate Mirgän answers: "During my absence he has been here; my children have seen him and spoken with him!" The messenger continues: "Tomorrow early you must present yourself at my lords Kalangar Taidji and Katai-Chan and bring them tribute!" As soon as he had said this, he struck at his horse and followed Kan Mirgän in his tracks.

Come into his tent, Kulate Mirgän asks his wife what kind of tribute the two hero-brothers could demand from him: people, cattle, or what possessions? The wife answers: "Tomorrow go yourself to them and ask them what they demand from you." Kulate Mirgän stood up in the morning, dressed, saddled his horse, armed himself, and set out on the way. The two hero-brothers live beyond nine lands. As soon as Kulate Mirgän had come halfway, he sees a steppe full of people. He asks the people why they had assembled, and one answers: "There are two mighty hero-brothers who demand tribute from us, and we are now on the way to them with the tribute." Kulate Mirgän asks the people with what they pay their tribute to the hero-brothers, and one from the people replies that they pay their dues with three mountain-high heaps of sables. "I," says Kulate Mirgän to this, "have caught no sables and will pay them the tribute for my head with my head itself, which I will now bring into their hands." With this Kulate Mirgän rode his way, covered the other half of the way, and came to a mountain at the ulus of the two hero-brothers. From the mountain he sees the ulus and calls to them, but they do not hear the call, for they are celebrating a feast. Again he calls to the two hero-brothers: "You desire that I pay a tribute in sables for my head, but since I have caught no sables, I bring you my head itself." When the two hero-brothers still did not hear the call, he seized his bow, shot an arrow, and killed both heroes with it.

Their brother-in-law Sokai Alten, when he learned of the death of the two hero-brothers, sat on his tiger-spotted horse and rode to meet Kulate Mirgän. Come onto the mountain, he climbed from the horse, and Kulate Mirgän also climbed from the saddle. Now they began to wrestle. They wrestled thus for seven months, whereupon Sokai Alten took Kulate-Chan's life. His horse ran at once home, but on the way the herdsmen who guarded the tabuns of the deceased Kulate Mirgän saw it and did not let the horse into the ulus. Nine days the herdsmen kept the horse in the tabun, but on the ninth the youngest of them said: "We act wrongly if we keep the white-blue horse here. The heroes have killed Kulate Mirgän, and we prevent the horse from bringing the message home. If his son Komdei Mirgän learns this, he will cut off all our heads." The herdsmen found these words very wise and let the horse loose.

When the horse now came home, the widow, the son, and the daughter met it, hung on the horse's neck, and wept for Kulate Mirgän's death. Finally Komdei Mirgän said: "Weeping does not help here; rather I will ride out myself to avenge my father's death." He then saddles his white-brown horse, sits in the saddle, rides over the nine lands, and so came to the mountain where the father had been killed. Here he calls with a hero's voice: "Do not boast, Sokai Alten, that you have killed my father!" When Sokai Alten heard this call, he mounted his horse and rode to meet Komdei Mirgän on the mountain. Sokai Alten asks Komdei Mirgän with what weapons he wishes to begin the fight, and Komdei Mirgän declares himself satisfied to fight with any kind of weapons. Sokai Alten chooses the bow, and the two heroes each went onto a separate mountain. Sokai Alten calls to Komdei Mirgän that he should send the first shot. Komdei Mirgän on his part gives the first shot to Sokai Alten, and the latter at once begins to draw his bow, shoots an arrow, but the arrow does not hit Komdei Mirgän. Now Komdei Mirgän also drew his bow, and when he shot the arrow, Sokai Alten fell at once dead to the ground.

Komdei Mirgän now sets out to the ulus to take possession of all property, but at the foot of the mountain he sees a black fox run past him. Komdei Mirgän turns his horse around and sets about chasing the fox; he rode in its tracks back over the mountain and so comes to a high, steep mountain. The fox ran over the mountain, but the horse stumbled and fell back from the mountain, whereby Komdei Mirgän broke his leg and lay on the ground. While he lies there, a bull with forty horns rises from the earth, and on it rides a Djilbegän (monster) with nine heads. Djilbegän came to the lying man, seized him with the left hand by the shoulder, and with the right cuts off Komdei Mirgän's head. He took the head with him and went with it under the earth; but Komdei's horse returned home.

When mother and sister saw the horse return without a master, they wept seven days long, day and night without ceasing. On the seventh day Kubaiko puts on her best clothes, and she was such a beautiful maiden that there was none like her on earth. Then she mounted her brother's horse, dressed in the best clothes, and rode away to seek her brother. She rode forward and saw only high mountains and wide seas. On the way her horse asks her why it brings her into such inaccessible regions; and the horse answers that heroes and hero horses never travel on better ways. "Then lead me," spoke the girl, "where it pleases you, but only show me the place where my father and my brother were killed." Come to the place where the father had been killed, the girl began to weep and wept without ceasing nine days at her father's corpse. Then she came to the place where the brother had been killed and weeps there too three days long. But she does not notice that the brother is without a head. When she finally notices this on the third day, she speaks to the horse and asks: "Do you perhaps know where my brother's head has come?" The horse began to speak and said that her brother had broken his leg on the fox hunt and that Djilbegän, while he lay, had come and taken his head. Then the girl asks the horse to lead her the same way that Djilbegän had gone with her brother's head.

Come under the earth, the girl sees a level way, and on it traces of Djilbegän still appear. Here she sees seven clay jugs by the way, and beside the jugs stands an old woman who busily pours milk from one jug into the other. Come past this place, she sees a horse that is bound to a rope three fathoms long. The horse stands on a sandy surface where there is neither grass nor water; nevertheless the horse is very fat. The girl wondered at this, rode her way on, and again saw a piece from the way another horse that, bound to a very long rope, stood by a running brook. The grass grew to the knees; nevertheless the horse was very lean. The girl wonders at this, rides again onward, and sees half of a human body lying by the way. A brook runs against the dead body and stops in its course crosswise before the corpse. The girl could not understand how half a human body could be able to dam a whole river, and so she rode her way onward again. A piece further she sees a whole human body lying by the way. Against this body flows a similar river as the former; but this body, although whole, does not hinder the course of the river, but the water flows over the corpse. The girl wondered ever more at what she saw, but does not halt; rather continues to ride.

As she rides thus, a girl meets her on the way. This girl sat down on the earth at the sight of Kubaiko, and Kubaiko at the same time stopped her horse. The sitting girl addresses Kubaiko and asks her to climb down from the horse. Kubaiko at once climbed down from the horse and sat at the side of the sitting girl. Kubaiko asks the sitting girl whether she is an underworld being or perhaps born in the land of the white light. The sitting girl answers that she was created by God, that she had lived on earth and had a brother, Kan Mirgän. "In one night," the sitting girl continued, "when Kan Mirgän slept in his tent, a messenger came from the two hero-brothers Kalangar Taidji and Katai-Chan. The messenger bound my brother hand and foot while he slept; then he took him and brought him to the Irle-Chans under the earth. Of these Irle-Chans there are eight, and the ninth is their ataman. This ataman now lets my brother burn, and I have come here to see whether I cannot free him. Come into Irle-Chan's dwelling, I heard such a strong noise of hammer blows that I did not dare to go further, but returned." The sitting girl Kanarko adds: "If you come to my brother, give him this silk cloth from me, so that he can dry the sweat from himself while he is roasted on the fire." Then Kanarko asks Kubaiko why she had gone into the underworld, and Kubaiko answers that she seeks her brother, whose head Djilbegän had brought there. To this Kanarko adds further: "If you go further on this way, you come to the bank of a river that flows under a high mountain. On this bank you see a stone house with forty corners, and in this house lives Irle-Chan. Before the door of this house stand nine larch trees that grow from one and the same root. This is the post to which the nine Irle-Chans bind their horses."

When Kanarko had ended her speech, she went up to the sun-land, but Kubaiko continued her wandering still deeper into the underworld. The more she approached the dwelling of the Irle-Chans, the stronger the hammer blows sound in her ears. Sitting on the horse, she sees forty men who forge hammers and another forty who forge saws, and still another forty who forge tongs. Then she came to the larch tree, climbed from the horse, and always followed Djilbegän's tracks, which lead to Irle-Chan's door. Before the girl entered, she stopped by the larch tree and saw there an inscription that read thus: "When Kudai created earth and heaven, this larch tree was also created, and except for Irle-Chan no man and no animal has come alive to it to this day." The girl bound her horse to the larch tree, entered Irle-Chan's dwelling, and closed the door behind her. Inside it is so dark that Kubaiko finds the way neither forward nor backward, but loses herself. In the darkness they seize Kubaiko, tear at her clothes, pull and torment her; but when Kubaiko stretches out her hands and wants to seize her tormenting spirits, she can catch none, for they had no bodies. In her fright she screams; at once a door is opened, the room becomes bright, and the ataman enters. Kubaiko rises; the ataman perceives her and turns back without uttering a word. Kubaiko follows in his tracks. The ataman goes from one chamber into another, and the chambers still stand empty. The ataman goes from one chamber into another and opens the doors. Kubaiko closes each door and follows the ataman in his tracks. Finally they came to a chamber that was filled with old women who spun flax. Then they came into another chamber that was likewise filled with women, all old and frail. They did nothing at all, but sat and tormented themselves, for they were all sick. All seemed to want to swallow something, but could not bring it down. Further they come into a third chamber, likewise filled with women who stood in their middle years. Around their arms and their necks large stones were bound, which they could not move. Then they come into a fourth room, where men sat on whose necks large trees hung, fastened with nooses at their necks. From the weight of the trees their eyes stood out of their heads and their tongues hung out of their mouths. In a fifth chamber men ran with firearms and were shot through the middle of the body. They ran and lamented in the chamber. In a sixth chamber Kubaiko saw knife-armed men who had cut themselves with their knives. The blood runs down from these men, and they run lamenting and wailing around in the chamber. Then she came to a seventh chamber, filled with mad dogs and mad men bitten by the dogs. In an eighth room lie men with their wives under large covers sewn together from nine sheepskins. Each has his own cover, but however large it is, it covers only the one half of the couple, so that when one of the two pulls the cover over himself, the other always remains without. In a ninth chamber lie also men with their wives. Their covers consist of only a single sheepskin; however small they are, a third could still lie under the same cover. From here she came into a tenth compartment that was large as a steppe. Kubaiko looks around in this room and perceives eight Irle-Chans sitting; and in their circle the ataman also sat down as the ninth. Kubaiko stands and bows before them and asks for what reason their servant spirit Djilbegän had cut off her brother's head and dragged it away. The Irle-Chans reply that this had happened at their command, and that the head was still in their keeping, but could not be regained in kindness. "If you want," the Irle-Chans continue, "to come into possession of your brother's head, see that you do not lose your own in the process. However, we will give you your brother's head back if you can carry out the works that we impose on you. We have a ram that sits fast deep in the earth, so that only the head looks out of the earth. This ram has seven horns, and if you can pull it out by the horns, we give you your brother's head. In the opposite case we cut off your own head and lay it beside your brother's." Thereupon all Irle-Chans stood up, took the girl with them, and went out of the chamber.

They then went through nine other chambers, all filled with human heads. Kubaiko recognized her brother's head in the middle chamber in the midst of a multitude of others. When Kubaiko saw her brother's head, she stopped and began to weep. The eight Irle-Chans spoke: "See, there now lies your brother's head, and if you happily carry out the task imposed on you, you will regain the head here; in the opposite case your own head will be set up in this chamber." Thereupon the Irle-Chans went out of the chamber, and the girl followed them through all chambers into the tenth. In the tenth the ram lay in the earth with its head and the seven horns upward. The Irle-Chans admonished the girl to set hand to the work, on the condition that she should pull the ram out of the earth with three tugs and lift it onto her shoulder. The girl seizes the ram by the head, lifted it at the first tug to the knees, at the second to the belt, and at the third onto her shoulder. Now the Irle-Chans fall at the girl's feet, bow before her, and promise to give her back her brother's head. They return into the chamber where the head was kept, take the head, and bring it into the chamber where the nine Irle-Chans sat. Here the Irle-Chans bring forth a large book and begin to read. In the book the whole dispute between Kulate Mirgän and Komdei Mirgän on the one side and the two hero-brothers and Sokai Alten on the other side is described.

When the Irle-Chans had found that Kulate Mirgän and Komdei Mirgän were justified in this fight, they told Kubaiko that she could take her brother's head with her. Then they gave the head to the girl and said: "To us a messenger from the two hero-brothers Kalangar Taidji and Katai-Chan has brought a hero, Kan Mirgän, whom they have long burned in the fire without being able to burn him. You, who are a mighty heroine, do you not know some advice to burn him?" The girl now desires to see Kan Mirgän, and the Irle-Chans lead her to the place where the smiths were busy with hammers. Here too there was a dwelling with nine chambers, and after they had gone through all, they came to a tenth, where Kan Mirgän was being burned. When he saw the girl, he remembered his sister left at home, began to weep, and asked Kubaiko the cause of her appearance. Then he asked Kubaiko to take his sister Kanarko, who remained at home, into her tent and treat her like her own sister. On their part the Irle-Chans also began to speak to Kubaiko and to ask her about the manner in which Kan Mirgän could be burned. The girl answers that she must first know for what reason the Irle-Chans torment a mighty and good hero in this way. They answer that this happened because Kan Mirgän had refused to pay tribute to his lords, the two hero-brothers Kalangar Taidji and Katai-Chan. Kubaiko says that this had not happened according to right and fairness, that Kan Mirgän would still free himself and take revenge on the Irle-Chans if they did not release him in kindness. Then she threw Kan Mirgän his sister's cloth and went back in company with the nine Irle-Chans. When they had come out, the girl asks to be allowed to see all the wonders that there were at the Irle-Chans. At a call from them, six card players and seven violin players at once appeared, and the Irle-Chans said: "These people are tormented here for their disorderly life, for they have wasted their time uselessly, gotten drunk and fought, and the card players have also cheated each other." They went further and so came to the larch tree to which Kubaiko had bound her horse. She untied the horse, climbed into the saddle, and asked the Irle-Chans to show her the way. The Irle-Chans did not dare to refuse, but accompanied the girl, who on the way asked why the men and horses that she had seen on the journey there were tormented in such a way under the earth. The Irle-Chans answer: "The one you saw pouring milk from one bowl into another is tormented because she gave her guests milk mixed with water. She has now been charged here to separate the milk from the water, and she will suffer this punishment for all eternity." — "The half body that dams the river," the Irle-Chans continue, "suffers no punishment. It lies there now to remind passersby that a wise man, even if he is deprived of his limbs and joints, can accomplish mighty things with his understanding, while an unwise man with his whole body can accomplish nothing at all. The whole body over which the river runs was by nature a strong but very unwise man. As the river now runs over him, so every matter has also passed over his understanding without his being able to grasp it or carry anything out with wisdom." The Irle-Chans add: "The fat horse recalls a man who cares for his horse and always keeps it in condition, however great the lack of pasture and water may be, while on the other hand the lean one is a proof that a horse cannot thrive even at the best pasture if the host does not look after it and take care of it.

Now the girl asks: "But what were those creatures that seized me in the dark chamber, tore my clothes, and tormented me, but had no bodies?" The Irle-Chans reply: "These are our invisible servant spirits, which do every evil to every evil man and can even kill him, but at all times keep away from good men and are not able to do them any harm." The girl continued to ask about the offenses of the men she had seen locked in the chambers, and the Irle-Chans answer: "The women who sat and spun in the first chamber spun on earth after sunset, at which time it is not allowed to busy oneself with any work. The women, however, who cannot swallow and sit without occupation in the second chamber, received skeins from other men to wind; they made the balls large but left them hollow inside and put yarn in their bosoms. These skeins they are now condemned to swallow, but the balls sit in their throats for eternal time. The young girls you saw with stones on their arms and necks salted butter and put stones in the butter to increase the weight. Therefore heavy stones now press their own necks, and their punishment will continue for eternity. In the fourth chamber you saw men with blocks on their necks and nooses around their necks: these are suicides who hanged themselves. The men with rifles in their hands in the fifth chamber are also suicides who shot themselves for the reason that they lived in discord with their wives. The men in the sixth chamber who carry knives damaged themselves in drunkenness with the knives and killed themselves by suicide. In the seventh chamber the men have gone mad because they did not take care before mad dogs, but teased them and were bitten. In the eighth chamber you saw men and women lying under large covers that were nevertheless too small for them. These are punished because during their lifetime they lived in discord with each other and each half of the couple only looked after its own advantage, whereby both suffered want. On the other hand you saw in the ninth chamber that men and women who live in harmony can be content with small possessions. These suffer no punishment, but are here merely as an example for others, and so that the evil ones through their sight feel their punishment only so much more."

When the girl had learned all this, she parted from the Irle-Chans, traveled up to the sun-land, and returned with her brother's head to the dead body. Sitting by the deceased, Kubaiko weeps, sad and troubled, since she knows no means to bring him back to life. While she weeps thus, Kudai has pity on her tears and sends her life-water. The girl took the life-water, sprinkled from it on the remains of the deceased, and when she had sprinkled them three times with the water, the brother's corpse began to move. The girl became very glad at this and only waited for the brother to stand up and regain his senses. Meanwhile hoofbeats of a hero's horse are heard. The girl was frightened and believed that a hero would appear to kill the brother again. At once she transformed herself into a swallow and flew away. After she had flown a small stretch, she halted to see what the arrived hero would undertake. He lifted Komdei Mirgän onto his horse, set him behind himself on the saddle, and Komdei Mirgän came to life again. The arrived hero now speaks to Komdei Mirgän: "I am a fatherless and motherless child who heard of your death and came to either bury you or give you a new life. No one has given me a name, but I call myself Kanna Kalas with a red-haired horse. If this name does not please you, you can give me another." Komdei Mirgän found the name good, and they now rode their way onward. But when Kubaiko noticed that the two heroes were in agreement, she flew to them. She now tells how everything had happened and advises the two heroes to return home. She herself wants to go to her father to call him too back to life with the life-water. Then she asks Kanna Kalas whether he had not seen somewhere a black fox three fathoms long. Kanna Kalas replies: "This fox is a girl called Utjün Arax, and her father is Uzüt-Chan. He lives with his daughter under the earth, and they do all evil that they only can on earth. I have sought this girl already for a long time, for in my childhood I lay forty years under a stone, and she went in the shape of a fox around the stone to eat me up. Therefore I have sought her already for a long time and well believe that I will find her once more." Komdei Mirgän says: "Since you are without parents and relatives, let us both become brothers and stand one for the other the whole life through. If you die before me, I will bury you; but if I should die before you, you will bury my body." Kanna Kalas agreed to this proposal, and they at once agreed to punish Djilbegän and the black fox.

Then they went under the earth, did not rest on the way, and soon came to the Irle-Chans. Irle-Chan himself came to meet them in the courtyard. Komdei Mirgän at once seized his bow, drew the bow, and wanted to shoot at him, but Irle-Chan called: "Refrain from shooting at me, Komdei Mirgän; I am lord under the earth and have here the same power as Kudai on earth. To kill me is neither possible nor allowed." Komdei Mirgän now asks Irle-Chan why he had his head cut off and kept it with him. Irle-Chan replies to this that this had happened because Komdei Mirgän had killed a mighty hero. Kanna Kalas now said that he himself would kill Irle-Chan and would only let him live on the condition that he release Kan Mirgän. Irle-Chan agreed to the proposal and at once brought Kan Mirgän to the other heroes. These three heroes now became three brothers: Kan Mirgän the eldest, Komdei Mirgän the middle, and Kanna Kalas the youngest brother. Kan Mirgän, as the eldest, now asked the younger brothers to obey his commands exactly, and then set out to ride ever deeper under the earth.

When they had ridden a stretch of way, they met an old man who was dressed in green clothing, with a dark-gray horse. Seven dogs accompanied him, all dark-gray. Kan Mirgän asked the old man who he was, and the old man promised to give information about himself if Kan Mirgän and the other heroes would have the goodness to climb down from their horses. They did this too, as did the old man. All now sat down on the earth, and the old man began: "You, Komdei Mirgän, seek Djilbegän; you, Kanna Kalas, want to have information about the black fox three fathoms long; and you, Kan Mirgän, wish to come to your right over the messenger from the two hero-brothers. Two earth-layers below there is a sea, and by this sea lives Talai-Chan, who has a son named Taze Mökö. Djilbegän and the messenger have gone to Talai-Chan to find help with him against Komdei Mirgän and Kan Mirgän. Talai-Chan is a man-eater, and when Djilbegän with the messenger came to him, he put them in a kettle, cooked and ate them up. But the black fox lies in his bed and now sleeps at its best in a house that stands on this way. If you want to know anything more, we shall see each other again in the sun-land." Thereupon the old man stood up, but the three heroes went straight to Talai-Chan. Kan Mirgän went into his tent and asked his two battle-brothers to wait outside for him. Taze Mökö comes to meet him and says: "My father has tried for nine years to cook and eat me. He has just eaten up Djilbegän and the messenger whom you seek. You, who are a great hero, come and help me, and I will on the spot cook and eat him up." At the same time Talai-Chan came and rushed at both to eat them up; but they seized and bound him, put him in a cauldron, and cooked him.

Kan Mirgän then returns to his battle-brothers and continues with them the journey to Uzüt-Chan. Arrived, the heroes climbed from their horses, went to Uzüt-Chan, and asked after his daughter. The old man told that he had much trouble from her and wished with all his heart for her downfall. At the same time he said that the daughter had just gone from him and showed the heroes the still fresh tracks. The three heroes mounted their horses and set out to pursue the black fox. They chased it and came onto a steppe on which stood a large stable. On this steppe they caught sight of the black fox, which ran into the stable. The heroes followed the fox into the stable too; here, however, it was so dark that they saw nothing but all three lost themselves. As they went in the darkness, Kanna Kalas advised his battle-brothers to draw their swords. Kan Mirgän drew his sword, which was so bright that by its shine they saw the fox's tracks. Then Komdei Mirgän also drew his sword, and by the shine of the swords they follow the fox tracks. While they rode thus, Kanna Kalas suddenly sprang from the horse's back down onto Kan Mirgän's sword, whereby he fell to the earth in two pieces and died. The two battle-brothers wept for him three days, and when they stopped weeping, their horses had vanished. Only Kanna Kalas's horse stood at their side. Now Kan Mirgän and Komdei Mirgän went in different directions to find their horses. They lost each other and went so long until from weariness and hunger they fell to the earth and remained lying there. So they lay a long time, and when they awoke, the stable was gone and they themselves lay on a bright field. Now Kanna Kalas came to them, leading their horses, and brought the black fox bound to a rope with him. All three now set about whipping the black fox and whipped it to death. Then they went into the sun-land, and had hardly come out of the hole when the old man with the seven dogs came to meet them.

The three heroes at once fell at the old man's feet and asked him what kind of man he was. The old man answers: "God has ordained that I shall wander both on and under the earth, and has given me such a power that I can comfort and gladden the troubled and on the other hand trouble the all-too-glad. The temper of those who exert themselves all too much I can likewise change, so that they also love cheerful pastime. I am called Kögöl-Chan and am a shaman who knows the future, the past, and everything that happens in the present both over and under the earth." — "Then let us know," says Kanna Kalas, "what they are doing at home with us, far in the homeland; but if you do not speak the truth, we will cut off your neck." The old man put on his shaman clothing and began to conjure. He conjured and told them all the pure and real truth. Among other things he told that the three brothers were the greatest heroes of the earth: Kan Mirgän the greatest, Komdei Mirgän the middle, and Kanna Kalas the smallest of them. To Komdei Mirgän the old man said that he would give his sister to Kanna Kalas in marriage and himself take Kan Mirgän's sister, Kanarko, as wife. But to Kan Mirgän the old man says that he was already married and that his sister was weeping for him in the tent. When the old man had said this, he mounted his horse and rode away.

The three heroes now went to Komdei Mirgän, arranged a feast, ate and drank for many days. Here Kanna Kalas took Kubaiko as wife, and Komdei Mirgän accompanied his sister and the two heroes to Kan Mirgän. Here Komdei Mirgän married Kanarko. The wedding is celebrated, and when the feast was at an end, Komdei Mirgän and Kanna Kalas traveled into their homeland, but Kan Mirgän remained at home in his own tent. Henceforth the three heroes lived at home in peace and quiet. Neither sickness nor death had power over them.
