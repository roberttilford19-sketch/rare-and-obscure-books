Scarcely had Kubasen Arga spoken these words when the earth began to shake under the thunder-like sound of hoofbeats. Kubasen Arga and Alten Arga make a hole in the tent wall and see Alten Aira riding down the mountain. Alten Arga trembles with fear and can hardly keep upright, but Kubasen Arga sits calmly in her place and looks at the hero. He rides straight toward the golden post, and Alten Arga begins to weep; but suddenly Alten Aira turns around and ties his horse to the post of larch wood. Then he goes to the tent of the eldest brother, Kan Töngös. Kubasen Arga says to Alten Arga: "Do not grieve and do not hope, but only ask Kudai for aid, and wait here until I come again." Kubasen Arga then followed Alten Aira into the eldest brother's tent, bade the guest welcome, and began to entertain him with food and drink. Alten Aira eats everything he can, but the wine he asks Kubasen Arga to save for her brothers. Kubasen Arga had prepared a very sweet and tasty wine for Alten Aira and asks him to empty a bowl of this wine to the well-being of Kan Töngös and Kum Töngös. Alten Aira does not want to drink, but rises from his seat and goes out of the tent. Kubasen Arga follows him with the wine to the door and tries to persuade him at least to dip his finger into the wine and taste it in her honour. Alten Aira could not refuse, since Kubasen Arga asked so nicely; he dipped his finger into the wine, which he found so sweet and tasty that he emptied the whole bowl. Then he sat down again on the bed and asked Kubasen Arga to give him more wine of the same kind. Without Alten Aira noticing, Kubasen Arga sets a stronger wine before him. Alten Aira keeps demanding more, but each time Kubasen Arga sets a stronger kind of wine before him, until at last she begins to entertain him with airan and kumys. [Footnote: Airan and kumys are Tatar drinks, the former prepared from cow's milk, the latter from mare's milk.] Alten Aira sits on the bed and drinks, at first from small bowls, then from large vessels. He drinks so for one month, two months, three whole months, without eating the least thing. However much he drinks, he still does not become intoxicated, and never gets enough, but keeps demanding more and more. When he had drunk for a whole year, he had drunk up all the wine that was in Kan Töngös's tent. Then Kubasen Arga invites him into the tent of the younger brother. Alten Aira followed her there and went on drinking as before, without becoming intoxicated. When he had also drunk there for half a year without eating, the wine at last began to have its effect. Intoxicated, Alten Aira left the tent, but at the door he fell down and fell asleep with half his body in the tent and the other half outside. Kubasen Arga gathers all her people to lift Alten Aira up and prepare a bed for him, but the hero was so heavy that all the people were not able to move his arm.

So Alten Aira slept for a whole year, and when this came to an end, Kan Töngös and Kum Töngös returned to their tents. When they saw Alten Aira lying stretched out on the ground, they began to scold their sister because she had not spread a bed under the hero. Then they began to lift the hero up, but with all their strength they could not move his arm. In amazement at such a hero they stand speechless at his side, shake their heads, and strike their hands against their hips. The brothers sit down to eat, but their fear of Alten Aira was so great that they could not eat properly, for their hands trembled so that the soup was spilled from the spoons, and their teeth chattered so that they could not chew any food. While they sit trembling in Kum Töngös's tent, Alten Aira awoke from his deep sleep. The brothers stood up, seized Alten Aira, after he had stood up, each by one hand, and led him to the bed in Kum Töngös's tent. Then they set food before him to eat and wine to drink. Alten Aira eats and drinks, and the brothers keep him company. But after they have eaten their fill in Kum Töngös's tent, Kan Töngös also wants to hold a feast in Alten Aira's honour. With the intention of providing for the feast, he leaves Alten Aira in his brother's tent and goes alone into his own yurt. Meanwhile Alten Arga had sat in the tent with Kubasen Arga, but when she saw Kan Töngös go alone into his tent, she hurried after him on his tracks. Having come into the tent, she threw herself on her knees before Kan Töngös and asked for aid against Alten Aira. Kan Töngös replied: "You can return the same way you came; I have never seen you in my whole life and have nothing to do with you." With this answer Alten Arga returns to Kubasen Arga, and right after that his younger brother together with Alten Aira came to Kan Töngös. Now the three heroes began again to eat and drink in Kan Töngös's tent. During the meal Kan Töngös and Kum Töngös ask Alten Aira: "Tell us why you pursue Alten Arga so; do you want to kill her, or do you want to have her as your wife? If you want to kill her, that is nothing to us; but if you take her as your wife, we will provide her with bridal gifts."

At the same moment one hears the hoofbeat of a hero's horse beyond seven lands. The heroes consider who the rider could be, but one knows no more than the other. Soon one hears the hoofbeat quite close; the earth begins to sway, the trees in the forest break, the air resounds, the sea swells and pours over the steppes. The three heroes sit dismayed in the tent, and even Alten Aira is seized by fear. — Kubasen Arga and Alten Arga look through the hole in the tent wall and see Alten Taktai riding down the mountain. Kubasen Arga again begins to read the future in Alten Arga's hand and says: "If Alten Taktai ties his horse to the golden post, he will be victor; but if he ties his horse to the post of larch wood, Alten Aira will be victorious." When Alten Taktai came riding down the mountain, he steered his horse toward the post of larch wood, at which Alten Arga was very grieved; but suddenly he turned his horse and tied it to the golden post. Then he entered Kan Töngös's tent, where the three heroes sat at the meal. Alten Taktai stopped at the door before the tent and asked Kum Töngös together with Kan Töngös: "How many tabuns do you have, how many wethers and other creatures, that you feed a monster who devours all your cattle and on top of that has eaten his own father and his own mother?" When he had spoken so, he went at Alten Aira and gave him such a powerful blow on the cheek with his fist that the hero crashed to the ground. At once Alten Aira stood up and threw his opponent to the ground with just as powerful a blow. After Alten Taktai had stood up, the two fighters went out of the tent and began to wrestle outside on the steppe. As they wrestled, one heard a crashing through the whole world, up to heaven and down into the innermost womb of the earth. The earth sways, and all living beings are seized by horror. Even Kudai and Aina marvel at this fight. The heroes wrestle for three months, without either being able to overcome the other. With every grip they tear great pieces of flesh from each other's bodies, which they throw to the dogs. After a year Alten Taktai gains the upper hand. He clasps Alten Aira with both hands, lifts him up high, shatters his back in six places, and then strikes him against the ground with such force that of his whole body nothing remained for the dogs to smell and nothing for the crows and magpies to peck into. At the same moment Aken Tüktü Agai At tore its halter and began to run away; but Alten Taktai seized his bow and shot the horse down while it ran.

Then Alten Taktai went into the tent to his sister, took her by the hand, kissed her, and spoke tender words to her. Alten Taktai sat a whole month with Kubasen Arga alone with his sister. Then Kubasen Arga took Alten Arga by the hand and led her to the bed; but Kum Töngös and Kan Töngös seized Alten Taktai, each by a hand, and invited him into their tents. Then the brothers arranged a great feast, killed horses, and invited much people to the banquet. When the feast had lasted a month, Alten Taktai goes to his sister and again begins to speak with her. Then Alten Arga asks her brother: "Who helped you down from the iron hook, since neither gods nor men could help you?" Alten Taktai replies: "Never ask me about that. The one who helped me down from the hook forbade me to tell even my own sister." Alten Arga spoke again and said: "How should you not be allowed to name for me the one who did good to us and gave us new life? I owe him just as much as you, and it is right that I too should come to know him and remember him my whole life long." Moved by these words, Alten Taktai said: "When I sat on the iron hook, gods and heroes came to me who had pity on me and would gladly have helped me down from the hook, if only they could. So there also came an old, grey-haired woman of very poor appearance. She wore a hairy mantle that reached only to her knees. In her hand she carried a staff seven fathoms long. She was small of stature, but her nose was three spans long. She took me down from the hook; but when I asked her who she was and where she lived, she answered that I must never say a word about her."

Scarcely had he spoken these words when his horse before the tent began to neigh and stamp. Alten Taktai hurried out to his horse, but saw the sixfold silver halter broken and the horse vanished. Alten Taktai returned into the tent and said to his sister: "Now go home and take care of our property, while I go to seek my vanished horse." Alten Arga took leave, put on her garment with eagle's wings, and flew away. Kum Töngös and Kan Töngös bring their horses to Alten Taktai and ask him to choose any one he likes. Alten Taktai tries to seat himself on the younger brother's horse, but scarcely had he climbed into the saddle when the horse's back collapsed. Then Alten Taktai set out on the way on foot and took spear, bow, arrows, and sword with him. As soon as he had climbed the mountain, he called his horse three times. Then one heard the horse neigh beyond three lands, and Alten Taktai suddenly saw the grey-haired old woman, who had taken him down from the iron hook, riding his horse Alten Tüktü away. The old woman holds the forelegs clasped with both arms over her breast, while the hind legs drag on the ground. When Alten Taktai saw this, he became very grieved in his mind and returned home.

Already before him Alten Arga had come home, and on her arrival the seven Kudais sent a heavenly message with the news that their parents during their lives had been very rich, but had hidden all their people and their property in a mountain, so that everything would remain in good keeping until the children had grown up. The messenger added: "Now the gods have permitted me to open the entrance to the mountain, which is recognizable by an inscription that your parents cut into it." Then the heavenly messenger went to the mountain and brought out of it all the people and all the property. But when Alten Taktai returned home, he was very astonished to see the people and cattle moving around the tent like ants. Alten Arga told him everything that had happened during his absence. She also thought that Alten Taktai could now console himself over the loss of Alten Tüktü. But Alten Taktai was sad in his mind and could not forget his good horse. One day he called nine smiths together and asked them to forge him an iron staff ninety fathoms long. Then he called three shoemakers to him and ordered them to sew, from the back pieces of horsehides, a pair of shoes with ninety soles. When the staff and the shoes were finished, Alten Taktai took leave of his sister and said to her: "If I do not return within twelve years, regard me no longer as living." So he set out on the way on foot and went now over high mountains, now along the coasts of the sea. Where the sun sets, there he took his way.

When he had gone over three lands, he came to a forest island where a river rose. Late in the evening he begins to go along the bank of the river and looks downstream. Suddenly he sees something like an otter swimming up the river. At once he recognizes the grey-haired old woman who had helped him down from the iron hook. She has on her former hairy clothing and carries her seven-fathom-long staff in her hand. Alten Taktai is very angry at the old woman and thinks in his mind: "She should rather have left me on the iron hook than steal my horse from me." Then he goes at the old woman, seizes her by the hand, and wants to kill her. At once the old woman pulled off her hairy garment and threw it over Alten Taktai, who fell to the ground in an instant. So he lay three whole days without consciousness; when he came to his senses again, he saw neither the forest, nor the river, nor the old woman — only a wide-reaching steppe.

By now Alten Taktai had gone over three lands, and on this wandering had worn away thirty fathoms of the staff and thirty soles of the shoes. He continues his wandering and goes many months, until late in the evening he comes to the coast of a great sea. Here again the grey-haired old woman comes swimming through the rushes toward Alten Taktai. As the first time, Alten Taktai wants to kill her and seizes her by the hand, but the old woman again threw her hairy garment over the hero, who fell senseless to the ground. When he awoke after seven whole days, the sea, the rushes, and the old woman had vanished, and all around Alten Taktai sees only a wide-reaching steppe. Lying on the earth, he looks at his staff and his shoes and says: "Now I have gone over six lands, wearing away sixty fathoms of my staff and sixty shoe-soles. My clothes too are worn out, and my strength is taxed. All this I have my sister to thank, who persuaded me to speak of the grey-haired old woman." Then he stands up from the ground and wants to go on, but at the same moment he notices a stone on the earth, with an inscription on it. Alten Taktai understood how to read the writing; he lifted the stone and found on it the following words: "Your horse is with Jedai-Khan; take it from him if you can." Alten Taktai then set out again on the wandering and one day came to a high mountain at a black sea. At the black sea lives a black khan (Kara Khan), and the black khan rules over much people. Since Alten Taktai went on foot and had worn-out clothes on, he was ashamed to enter the tent in bright daylight. Therefore he stayed on the mountain and lay there the whole day. But when it began to grow dusk, he went into the khan's ulus, but did not at once enter the tent; instead he stayed standing outside and looked around for a poor tent into which he could enter with his worn-out clothes. At one end of the ulus stood a very poor tent, and through the slits in the tent Alten Taktai sees an old man and an old woman sitting by a fire. They were very ragged and had nothing to eat. The old man already lay down and slept, but the old woman woke him and began to speak: "Do you know, my husband, that Kara Khan's daughter, Kara Djüstük (Black Ring), is tomorrow to be married to Kan Mirgän, who is a brother of Alten Aira's? I have foreseen that Alten Taktai will come to the wedding, but Kan Mirgän seems not to have forgotten that Alten Taktai killed his brother, and I fear that Kan Mirgän will kill him tomorrow. If he were here now, I would teach him how he could overcome Kan Mirgän. I would send him in the night to my son Albang Djas, who serves with Kara Khan. Alten Taktai would surely find him where he sleeps by the door in the khan's tent. While the khan sleeps intoxicated, it would be an easy thing for Alten Taktai to enter the tent and wake Albang Djas, without the khan himself awakening. Then both heroes together would see how strong Kan Mirgän is." So spoke the old woman, who therefore wished ill upon Kara Khan and his whole house, because he had made her husband, who was formerly also a khan, into his slave.

When Alten Taktai had heard this speech of the old woman, he went into Kara Khan's tent and quietly woke Albang Djas, told him his name, and led him out. Now Albang Djas took the word and said to Alten Taktai: "You go and lie down in the tent of the khan's daughter, but leave me your bow." Alten Taktai replies: "It is better that I shoot Kan Mirgän, for he can be wounded in only one place on the back, when he bends down, and this place I know." Albang Djas said: "Leave to me the care of killing him, and you go into Kara Djüstük's tent." So Alten Taktai went into the tent of the khan's daughter and lay down to sleep in a remote part of the tent.

Scarcely had he fallen asleep when he awoke again and, by the weak light of the coal fire, saw a figure kneel down and look him in the face. This was Kara Khan's wife, who likewise wished ill upon Kan Mirgän, because Kara Khan wanted to force his daughter to take Kan Mirgän as her husband against her own will. When the old woman had recognized Alten Taktai, she went and woke Kara Djüstük, saying: "Stand up, my daughter, and set food before him, for a good guest has come to us." Kara Djüstük stood up, greeted Alten Taktai, and entertained him with food and drink. But when Alten Taktai had finished the meal, the khan's wife returned to her tent and everyone lay down again to rest. At midnight Kan Mirgän wakes and calls out at once: "I scent Alten Taktai; but where shall I find him?" He stood up, took his sword and his bow, and went out of the tent. Kan Mirgän directed his steps to the tent of the khan's daughter, but Albang Djas followed him softly on his tracks. On the way Albang Djas begins to draw the bow, but does not have strength enough to draw it. He sends a prayer to Kudai with the words: "It is not I who draw the bow, but Alten Taktai draws it; it is not I who shoot the arrow off, but Alten Taktai shoots it off." Then he received strength to draw the bow, and when Kan Mirgän bent down to enter Kara Djüstük's tent, Albang Djas shot off an arrow that struck Kan Mirgän in the back. Lifeless, Kan Mirgän sank down with one half of his body into the tent and with the other half outside. When Albang Djas had killed Kan Mirgän, he also killed the horse Karoi Tjokor At (the black-blue piebald).

Thereupon Albang Djas went to Alten Taktai, woke him, and asked to be allowed to seek Alten Tüktü together with him. Alten Taktai gladly consents, and the two heroes agreed to regard each other in future as brothers. Alten Taktai was the elder and Albang Djas the younger brother. Without taking leave of Kara Khan, the hero brothers set out on the way. Albang Djas rode a horse named Ak Sabder At (the white sweat-fox); but Alten Taktai was forced to go on foot, since no horse was able to carry him on its back. They now travelled a long time together, but Alten Taktai had only one fathom of the staff left, and only one sole on the shoes. In one night they came to a high mountain; from there they see the white sea, and at the sea an ulus that belonged to Jedai-Khan. This Jedai-Khan Kudai had made into a khan over all khans, into a great khan (Ulu Khan). Jedai-Khan was born when the light was born, and death has no power over him. As the moon's disc changes, so Jedai-Khan's age also changed. When the moon was young, Jedai-Khan was also young; but as soon as the moon grew old, Jedai-Khan also grew old. Although it was night, Alten Taktai and Albang Djas, standing on the mountain, saw that much people and much cattle moved around Jedai-Khan's ulus. At the golden post Alten Taktai sees his horse tied with three lassos [Footnote: Arkan is a horse-noose made of horsehair.] at the neck and an iron chain at the foot. At the same post also stands Jedai-Khan's horse Dju Kules Bözeragat (the reddish-white horse of seven fathoms). In the dark night the two horses shone like two moons.

Alten Taktai and Albang Djas spent the night on the mountain; but as soon as day dawned, the hero brothers went into the ulus and went straight to Jedai-Khan's tent. Jedai-Khan stood up, went to meet the hero brothers, led them to the bed, and had food and drink set before them. When the heroes had eaten and drunk, Alten Taktai stood up and asked Jedai-Khan how Alten Tüktü Arkar At had come into his power. Jedai-Khan replied: "One morning, when I stood up, my people told me that a strange horse had come to the tent. I saw that the horse was good and had the horse tied up well, so that it would remain with me in safe keeping, until its owner might perhaps demand it back from me again." When Alten Taktai heard these words, he became very glad, fell on Jedai-Khan's neck, and kissed him for having taken such care of Aken Tüktü Ai Kor At. But Jedai-Khan led Alten Taktai again to the bed and asked him to sit down. He himself took a great book with gods' writing and read to Alten Taktai from this book that the one who had helped Alten Taktai from the iron hook was called Alten Burtjük (Gold Leaf), and was destined by Kudai to be Alten Taktai's wife. Jedai-Khan added: "She is still a young girl, although out of modesty she showed herself to Alten Taktai in the shape of a grey-haired old woman. A hero, Kan Mirgän, is now wooing her, who wants to force her by violence to become his wife. But let us go to Alten Burtjük, and I will reconcile you."

All three heroes saddled their horses and set out on the journey. Alten Burtjük lived beneath a gold mountain at a gold lake; much people served her, and much cattle grazed around her ulus. Alten Burtjük was herself mistress of the ulus, for her father and her mother had long been dead. When the three heroes came to the ulus, they saw Kan Mirgän's horse standing tied at the golden post. They tied their horses to the same post and entered the tent. Here Kan Mirgän sits on the bed, but Alten Burtjük at a separate place, and sews clothes. Around Alten Burtjük sit seventy heroes, who are all her own subjects and keep watch over the maiden. At the entrance of the three heroes, Alten Burtjük rose, led them to the bed, and entertained them with airan and kumys. Jedai-Khan took his book from his bosom and said to Alten Burtjük: "In this book it is written that the time has come at which you shall enter into marriage. There are now two suitors here: do you want to have Kan Mirgän or Alten Taktai as your husband?" Alten Burtjük replies: "Let them settle my fate between themselves." Jedai-Khan says: "It is better that you go to the seven Kudais and ask counsel from them." Alten Burtjük now lays her sewing aside, opened a gold casket, and took out her garment with eagle's wings. With this garment she flew to the seven Kudais and asked the heroes to await her return in the tent.

When Alten Burtjük had come to the seven Kudais, she stopped before the tent and changed her clothes. The seven Kudais sat in their tent behind a curtain, but when Alten Burtjük entered the tent, they drew the curtain aside, and Alten Burtjük saw before them a great book, in which they recorded the born and the deceased. Alten Burtjük bowed before the seven Kudais and asked which of the two she should take as her husband, Kan Mirgän or Alten Taktai. The seven Kudais reply: "You shall take Alten Taktai as your husband, and tell him that he should give his sister to Kan Mirgän." With this answer Alten Burtjük returned to her tent. Now a great wedding was arranged; but when the wedding had been celebrated, Alten Burtjük made her foremost servant the steward of her property. Kan Mirgän demanded from Alten Taktai his sister for himself as wife, but Alten Taktai replied: "My sister is older than I, and therefore I cannot marry her off, but you must woo her yourself." Now all four heroes, together with Alten Burtjük, set out on the journey, and Alten Burtjük rode on a horse with three ears, named Us Kulag Agai At Alten Tüktü (the golden-haired whitish-blue horse with three ears). They passed by Jedai-Khan's tent without calling on him, and then came to Kara Khan. Since Kara Khan was already old, Jedai-Khan made Albang Djas khan in his place and gave Kara Khan's daughter, Kara Djüstük, to Albang Djas in marriage. After the wedding Jedai-Khan took leave and returned home, but the three remaining heroes continued their journey to Alten Taktai.

At home Alten Arga already knew three days beforehand that Alten Taktai was on the way home with his wife. Therefore she had a tent erected for the newly married pair that reached up to the clouds. The tent was covered with gold and silver, and inside full of chests and other household goods. Alten Taktai had travelled twenty-four years in foreign lands when he at last returned home. After his homecoming, Kan Mirgän woos Alten Arga, but she leaves it to Alten Taktai to dispose of her fate. Alten Taktai gives his sister to Kan Mirgän and arranges a splendid wedding. After the wedding he saddles for his sister Alten Asaktak Ala Djorog At (the six-footed piebald horse). At the departure he accompanies Kan Mirgän, his sister, and Albang Djas halfway, then takes leave and returns home. Now neither heroes nor Ainas can get at him.



2. Ak Khan

At the white sea, at the foot of the white mountain, lives Ak-Khan (the white prince). A right hand serves him, named Sädei Mirgän. Ak-Khan has a horse named Ag At (white horse), and Sädei Mirgän's horse was called Ak Sar At (white-yellow horse). The khan is married, and his wife is called Alten Arga. They are childless, but very rich in horses, horned cattle, and all other property. Ak-Khan speaks to his wife: "We are both old and have no children; perhaps a stranger will come, kill me, and rob me of all my property." The wife answers: "What shall we do, since God has given us no children?" Ak-Khan says: "Rather than sit at home and wait until someone takes my life, I will set out to fight in foreign lands, where I either sink into the dust or return home with rich treasures." The old woman tries indeed with many pleas to keep him at home, but he does not let himself be moved; instead he mounts the horse's back and sets out on the journey. When he had come to the height of the mountain, he still heard his wife calling: "Turn back, I am pregnant; turn back, Ak-Khan!" — "You are deceiving me," answers Ak-Khan; "young, you bore no children; how should you become a mother in your old age?" When he had spoken so, he continued his journey without listening to his wife's words. He rode through many lands and came to a mountain from which he saw the white sea. On the shore of the sea stands an ulus, and around the ulus much people and much cattle move. Over this ulus ruled a prince who was called Katai-Khan. At his tent stands a golden post, and tied to the post a bull with forty horns, called Karak Azer Müs Kar Buga (the grey-dapple bull horned with forty ends). Not far from the ulus sixty little boys play, and these are watched over by six heroes. The sixty boys have their own balagan a little distance from the ulus. Ak-Khan rode down the mountain and tied his horse to a separate post at Katai-Khan's tent. He entered his tent and began to look at Katai-Khan, where he rested on his bed, covered with a silken blanket and playing on a harp with forty strings. When he saw Ak-Khan enter the tent, he stood up from his couch, laid the harp aside, took Ak-Khan by the hand, and led him to sit on the golden bed. Then he ordered his wife to entertain Ak-Khan with kumys and airan, and to set food of every kind before him.

When the table was laid, both princes sat down to eat and drink. During the meal the host addresses Ak-Khan with the following words: "During your absence your wife has borne you at home a son and a daughter; will you not give me the daughter?" Ak-Khan would not believe that his wife in her old days had borne children, but Katai-Khan continues: "I desire nothing more than that you promise me the daughter, and give me your word to bring her here after seven days, if she has come into the world. But if you do not give me this promise, I will kill you and take possession of all your property." Ak-Khan was already well entertained and promised Katai-Khan not only his daughter, but also his son.

Right after that he returns home, to bring the promised children to Katai-Khan. Drunk, he arrived in his homeland and tied his horse to the golden post. He entered the tent and sees there the two children lying in a golden and a silver cradle. He sat down on the bed and was very glad that God had given him a son and a daughter. Alten Arga held an ell-long iron bar in her hand, and when the man had sat down on the bed, she struck him with it on the head, so that the blood spurted out through his hair. Then she began to scold her husband and said: "Why did you have to go and promise my children to Katai-Khan, and why did you not believe me when I told you that I was pregnant?" Ak-Khan saw his mistake, and both resolved not to give their children willingly to Katai-Khan.

When seven days had passed, at midday one heard the hoofbeats of Katai-Khan's bull, and at that the earth shakes, the sea swells, the tents collapse, and so on. Sitting on the bull, Katai-Khan calls before the tent: "Bring me the children you promised me, or I will take your life!" Alten Arga fills two golden bowls with kumys and airan, goes with her husband to Katai-Khan, and asks him to be allowed to keep the children at home with her. Katai-Khan took the golden bowls in his hand and threw them on the ground, without drinking of the wine. Then he seized Ak-Khan by the beard and struck him against the golden post, so that his head parted from his body and remained in his hand. Then he said to the old woman: "Bring me your children willingly, or I will kill you too!" Forced by need, the old woman brought her children. Katai-Khan puts them in a sack. At the same moment Ak-Khan's horse tore its halter and vanished, before Katai-Khan could kill it. With the stolen children he returned home, without, however, killing Alten Arga or driving away her cattle.

When he rides down the last mountain to his own ulus, Ak-Khan's little son looks out through a hole in the sack and looks at the ulus in which Katai-Khan lived. He sees the sixty children playing at the balagan, guarded by six heroes. To this place Katai-Khan now brings the two children and orders a seventh hero to keep watch over them. Then he went to his own tent, tied the bull fast, and entered the tent. He ate, lay down on the bed, and began to play on his harp with forty strings. While he played, an ataman comes and says to Katai-Khan: "Already yesterday Kiro Balak (the old fish) called and demanded food." This fish came every year to Katai-Khan, and he gathered children to feed Kiro Balak with them. Every year he gave the fish sixty children, and now also he ordered the ataman to feed Kiro Balak with the stolen hero children. The ataman went with this answer and had all the children bound with one arkan, but the strap was no longer enough for two children. Then the ataman went to Katai-Khan and asked him what should be done with the two children. Katai-Khan answers that the rope should be lengthened, and then the two remaining children also tied fast with it. As Katai-Khan had ordered, the ataman had the rope lengthened and the two remaining children, who were Ak-Khan's children, tied fast with it. Then Katai-Khan again continued to play on his harp with forty strings. The sixty heroes then carry the children bound with the rope to the seashore, to deliver them to Kiro Balak. They came to the strand. There lies Kiro Balak with his mouth on the sand, with his tail, which shines like the sun, beyond the sea. The fish's jaws are wide open, and its teeth are spans long. The heroes threw the children into the fish's jaws, whereupon the fish at once went back into the sea. But the strap with which Ak-Khan's children were tied had torn, before the fish got them into its jaws, and the children remained floating on the white sea, without Katai-Khan or anyone else knowing of it.

Soon after Ak-Khan was killed, his wife also died, and Sädei Mirgän administers the property. Under his horse he has spread white felt and covered its back with beautiful blankets. He himself lies on soft down and covers himself with silken blankets. He has a girl who had gone for water with two buckets. When she wants to draw water from the sea, she sees on the strand two dead children, tied to a piece of rope. She looks at the children, recognizes them as Ak-Khan's children, and begins to weep bitterly. She left her bucket on the shore and ran to the ataman, to report to him what she had found on the seashore. When the ataman heard this, he gathered his people and ordered Ak-Khan's drowned children to be fetched from the seashore; the people obeyed him, for he was now khan in the ulus, and the children were brought to the tent, whereupon the ataman ordered them to be laid on the cold earth. Then he has his heroes dig a grave seventy fathoms deep on the mountain and lay the children in the pit. At the bottom of the grave he has seven lances set up with points directed upward. He also has two swords laid crosswise in the grave, with edges directed upward. Then he has the two children thrown into the grave, covers them with earth, and has a great stone laid on the grave. Finally he orders seven heroes to watch day and night at the grave. All this he did with the intention of being all the more sure of Ak-Khan's property. He himself lies in the tent under silken blankets, and all the people regard him as their khan.

One morning seven herdsmen come running to him and report: "A dun stallion and a dun mare have foaled a white foal. Scarcely had it come out of its mother's body when it ate grass; and as soon as the foal was born, it leaped three times over its mother and swam three times across the sea. This is likely to be a hero's horse." — Sädei Mirgän has sixty men surround the foal, in order to take its life. When the sixty men, equipped with bows and arrows, had gathered around the foal, a sudden darkness arose, and the foal ran away. The darkness lasts three whole days, and when it grows light again, Sädei Mirgän begins to seek the foal's tracks. Where the sun rises, there the foal had run. The foal had run with such haste that Sädei Mirgän had to ride half a day between the tracks of the fore and hind feet. Within three days the foal had run over three lands and returned to the herd. Meanwhile Sädei Mirgän continues to follow the foal on its tracks. In one night the young foal turned into a six-year-old girl and went into Ak-Khan's tent, while Sädei Mirgän was away. Then the girl took seven skins of wine, went with them to the grave of the two children, and begins to entertain the watchmen with wine. At that she begins to sing, and sings so beautifully that all wild animals and all birds gather to listen to the singing. When she has finished singing, she says to the watchmen: "Why do you stand here and guard the dead bodies?" The watchmen reply that they were instructed to do so by Sädei Mirgän and must obey his commands. The girl teaches them that they are no worse than Sädei Mirgän and not obliged to obey his commands. At the same time she continued to entertain the watchmen with wine; and when they were quite drunk, they left the grave and went into the ulus. When they had gone away, the girl leaped three times over the grave and turned into a seven-year-old boy. Then she lifted the gravestone off and began to dig up the grave. When she had dug seventy fathoms deep, she came to the corpses, which were already decayed and evil-smelling. She took them out of the grave, made two felt sacks, and tied these on her back.

Then she turns again into a foal and began to run in the direction where the sun sets. Having come into the forest, the foal hears a strong sound, through which the earth is set shaking, mountains sink in, and rocks burst. Frightened by the sound, the foal hid in the forest. At the same moment it sees Ak-Khan's former horse, Ag At, hurry past. When the foal recognized Ag At, it began to neigh, and Ag At also neighed, since it recognized the foal's voice. Then they ran toward each other, and the foal tells Ag At that it has the bones of Ak-Khan's dead children on its back. Ag At asks the foal to throw the sacks with the bones on the earth and leap three times over the sacks. The foal obeyed the command and thereupon turned into a boy. Ag At also leaped over the sacks with the bones and turned into an old man. The boy asks the old man: "Where have you been so long?" The old man replies: "Since Ak-Khan died, I have been striving to find a means to bring him back to life. I have run six years and come through twelve lands. All men and all animals I have asked for a remedy for Ak-Khan, but no one could give me any counsel, except the falcon, who pointed me to a birch with golden leaves and golden bark. The falcon told me that this birch grows on a high mountain beyond twelve lands. By the birch, said the falcon, a span deep in the earth lies a golden vessel with water of life, and the birch itself is overgrown from the root up to the top with white grass. The falcon instructed me that Kudai has placed a hero as watchman by the birch; he was called Aken Taia and had a horse named Ala Kul At. The falcon asked me to go to the hero and beg from him a little grass and a little water. This grass the falcon asked me to dry and sprinkle three times on the bones. Then he asked me to take the water of life into my mouth and sprinkle the bones three times with it." Ag At then took the grass and the water of life out of his bosom, sprinkled the bones with the grass, and sprinkled them with the water of life. When Ag At the first time sprinkled grass on the bones and sprinkled them with water of life, the bones gathered into two heaps, and each bone joined itself to its proper place. The second time the bones were clothed with flesh, and after the third time Ak-Khan's children became alive again. Then Ag At spoke to the foal: "Now you may nourish and keep the children as you can; I, meanwhile, go to see whether I cannot with the grass and water of life also bring Ak-Khan back to life." Ag At turned again into a horse and vanished, but the foal began to pick berries as food for the children. On the same day that Ak-Khan's children came back to life, this became known to Katai-Khan; he saddles his forty-horned bull and goes to the children. In his hand he has a great wooden hammer, which he uses instead of a whip and with which he strikes the bull on the head. While the foal picks berries for the children, it hears the hoofbeats of the bull and the blows of the great wooden hammer. At once the foal took its horse shape again, left the berries lying, ran to the children, took them on its back, and set about escaping Katai-Khan. Riding on the mountain, Katai-Khan sees the foal running away with the two children; he reaches for his bow and shoots an arrow at the fleeing ones. While the arrow flies through the air, the foal managed to escape; the shot arrow struck against a mountain, which through the shot sprang into two parts. Out of anger that he had missed, Katai-Khan took his wooden hammer and knocked Kar Buga on the head with it, so that the bull fell on its face. But it rose again, became so angry that its eyes became bloodshot, and began to pursue the foal in the wildest run. Katai-Khan called to the foal: "Turn back willingly; you shall not escape me! It is too high to fly to heaven, and the earth is too hard for you to penetrate into it." The foal replies: "My hooves are of steel, and under the steel are hard bones. As long as the steel stays on my hooves, and as long as I still have a little piece of bone on my feet, I will not give you Ak-Khan's children willingly." With these words the foal continued to run away, and Katai-Khan followed on its tracks.

At the white sea beneath a high mountain lived a good hero, Jebet-Khan, and he had a horse called Dju Kules Bozerag At (the fox-red horse of seven fathoms). The foal comes fleeing to Jebet-Khan, bows before him, and asks for help against Katai-Khan. Jebet-Khan answers: "For Ak-Khan and his house I will gladly face a fight to the death against Katai-Khan. Nevertheless you must not rely on my strength, but continue your way farther." The foal hurried away from Jebet-Khan with this answer, but when it came to the first mountain, it saw Katai-Khan already come riding up on the nearest ridge. Jebet-Khan meanwhile has saddled his horse, armed himself with spear and sword, and rides to meet Katai-Khan. When they clashed, they struck at each other with the sword, without harming each other. Then they both dismounted and began to wrestle, and Katai-Khan shattered Jebet-Khan at the first grip, so that he fell to the ground in two pieces.

When the foal from the mountain saw Jebet-Khan's misfortune, it began to run with all haste and came again to a white sea. At the white sea lives a hero, Alten Kus, and he has a horse named Alten Tüktü Kalter At. The foal also demands help from Alten Kus for Ak-Khan's children. Alten Kus answers, as Jebet-Khan had answered, that for Ak-Khan's children he would live and die. With this answer the foal ran off, but Alten Kus saddled his horse, girded himself with the sword, took the lance, and went to meet Katai-Khan. The foal ran from Alten Kus over nine mountains and stopped on a high, white mountain. On the white mountain is a white lake, and around the lake grows green grass. After so long a journey the foal was hungry and thirsty and began now to eat and drink on the mountain. Ak-Khan's children sit at the lake and pick berries for themselves. While the foal and the children eat, they see Alten Kus fighting with Katai-Khan. They fight three years on the mountain, which through their wrestling becomes flat like a steppe. Neither can defeat the other, until at last in the fourth year Katai-Khan overcomes Alten Kus. When the foal saw how Alten Kus perished, it hid the girl in a dense forest near the lake and hurried away with the boy.

At the same moment Katai-Khan also struck the bull's forehead three times with his hammer and began to pursue the foal. On its way the foal comes upon an iron mountain, so high that not even the birds could fly over it. The foal began to bow before the sun and the moon, that they might grant it help to get over the mountain. To the boy it says: "Break off a good birch twig and strike me with it as much as you are able, when I run up the mountain." The boy did as the foal had instructed him, and they happily came up the mountain. Here the foal stops and sees the water at one place seething as in a boiling pot. The boy was thirsty and drank a little of this water; but as soon as he had drunk, he fell into a deep sleep and lay on the mountain. The boy sleeps three whole days, and the foal meanwhile eats grass on the mountain. Then Katai-Khan also came to the mountain and drove his bull up it, but when he was seven fathoms from the mountaintop, he fell back again. The foal wanted to wake the boy and flee, but the boy does not wake. Katai-Khan drove up the mountain the second time, but fell back again. When for the third time he struck his bull with utmost force with the wooden hammer, he at last reached the mountaintop. Then the foal ran to the boy and struck him with a hind foot, so that the boy flew seven fathoms from the place. Angrily the boy stood up, rushed at Katai-Khan, seized him by the hand, and pulled him off the bull down to the earth. Then the boy begins to wrestle with Katai-Khan. When they grip each other, great pieces of flesh remain in their fists. So they fight three years, without being able to defeat each other. At last they lose all their strength, so that they can no longer stand on their legs, but wrestle with each other lying on their knees. They fall to the ground and wrestle also still lying down. With effort the boy rises above Katai-Khan, but cannot take the old man's life. When Kar Buga saw his master lying under the boy, he fell into anger, butted his horns against the earth, and gathered his strength to attack the boy. The foal sees this, and in a moment two swords formed on its forelegs, with which it attacks the bull and cuts it into two parts. Thereupon the boy also succeeded in taking Katai-Khan's life, but he himself was barely still alive. He crawled with effort to the seething water spring, drank from it, and sank again into a deep sleep. He sleeps seven days, sleeps nine days, and finally wakes on the tenth day as a fully grown hero.

Meanwhile the foal too had become a fully grown horse; it was saddled, provided with bridles and other equipment. The boy looks with joy at his white foal, which shines like the sun and the moon. While the boy looks at his beautiful horse, he notices a writing on the earth, which he picked up and began to read. The writing was to the effect that the boy should henceforth call himself Aidölei Mirgän, and his sister Alten Kuruptju (Gold Thimble). Thereupon Aidölei Mirgän mounted his horse and returned to his father's ulus. On the way he seeks out his sister, but she had meanwhile died of hunger. The horse began to speak with a human voice and said to Aidölei Mirgän: "Ag At has left me grass and water, to wake the dead back to life." Then it asked Aidölei Mirgän to sprinkle grass on the dead sister and sprinkle water on her three times. Aidölei Mirgän did so, and the sister awoke again to life. Then Aidölei Mirgän turns her into a ring, puts this on his finger, and takes her home. On the way the boy wakes Alten Kus and Jebet-Khan back to life with the grass and water of life that Ag At had procured for him. Jebet-Khan also had a daughter, Intei Arga, whom Aidölei desires from him and receives as his wife. The father saddles a six-legged horse for his daughter and accompanies her a stretch of the way, whereupon he returns home again. When Aidölei Mirgän comes to the hill of his homeland, he sees Ag At tied at the golden post, and Ak-Khan walking up and down at the tent. Aidölei Mirgän rode down the mountain, greeted his father, and they were glad to see each other again. A new tent was set up for the young pair, everything was prepared for the wedding, tabuns were slaughtered, and all Ak-Khan's subjects invited to the wedding. Then Aidölei Mirgän held judgment over Sädei Mirgän and pronounced the verdict on him, that he should be forged alive, with his horse, to a rock. Meanwhile Alten Kuruptju had already on her homecoming regained her true shape, and now Alten Kus came and wooed her. The father gave him his daughter, and a new wedding was celebrated, whereupon Alten Kus returns home with his wife and is accompanied halfway by Aidölei Mirgän. Then Aidölei Mirgän returns home again, and henceforth neither heroes nor Ainas dared to fight with him.



3. Katai Khan

At the white sea beneath the high mountain lives Katai-Khan. He has two daughters, the elder Kara Kuruptju (black thimble) and the younger Kesel Djibäk (red silk), and a three-year-old son. The younger girl has a garment with swan's wings and flies with it from time to time to the seven Kudais, who live above in the high heaven. The seven Kudais have seven daughters, and these also fly about in a garment with swan's wings. With these Kesel Djibäk plays and swims in the gold lake. Meanwhile the girl's father, Katai-Khan, goes into the forest and hunts game. Katai-Khan was such a skilled hunter that no bird and no animal escaped him. Every time he went out into the forest, he returned home with both sides of the horse laden with game. Having come home, he gathered his subjects and divided all the flesh among them; he himself kept only the hides.

One evening, having returned late from the hunt, Katai-Khan lay down to sleep and slept long into the morning. He had seven heroes who served him as atamans. While he still slept, the seven heroes came into the tent, woke the khan, and said: "Stand up, Katai-Khan, and see what is shining there on the mountain like a sun." Katai-Khan stood up, put on a sable fur, and stepped out of the tent. Having come out of the tent, Katai-Khan was frightened when he saw the strange light on the mountaintop. He recognized the snake with the golden skin, the silver horn, and the eyes that sparkled like precious stones. The snake's head is so large that Katai-Khan counts twelve spans between its eyes, and the snake's tail reaches far into another land. Katai-Khan returned into the tent, took off his sable fur, saddled his horse, took sword and lance, bow and arrows, swung himself into the saddle, but did not dare to ride against the snake; instead he stood as if petrified in one and the same place.

While he stood so, the snake swung its tail and struck Katai-Khan, so that he fell down with his horse and lay on the ground. In the tent the three-year-old boy sits on his bed. The snake came down from the mountain, took the boy on its tongue, and vanished with him. Seven days afterward Katai-Khan regained his senses. When he saw that the boy had vanished, he became very grieved and said: "As long as my eyes sit in my head, I will not willingly give my son to the snake." He sits again in the saddle and sets out on the journey; his wife calls to him: "You have a gold arrow in the chest; take it with you, when you go into the fight with the snake." The old man turned back, took the arrow, put it in the quiver, and, thus armed, set out on the journey to seek out the snake. Having come to a high mountain, he sees the snake, shining like a sun, coming toward him. Katai-Khan took his living arrow from the quiver and aims the arrow against the snake. The arrow strikes the snake's forehead and splits the snake into two parts. The snake's stomach is filled with living and dead heroes. Some still sit on horseback, just as they were swallowed by the snake. Katai-Khan seeks in the snake's stomach his three-year-old son, finds in a gut a wooden chest, in the wooden chest a gold chest, and in the gold chest his son, who was barely still alive and very hungry. Katai-Khan was far from his homeland and found nothing to feed his son on the mountain. He already began to fear that the boy would die of hunger, when the horse began to speak and said: "Take the Keitjum [Footnote: Perhaps some drinking vessel or a satchel that the Tatars carry with them on journeys.] off the saddle, and I will give of the milk that I have kept in store since the time when I sucked at my mother." When the boy had taken of the milk, he became very quick. The father took him up on the saddle and brought him home.

On arriving at the mountain of his homeland, his horse Kesel Kar At (the red, grey-dapple horse) stopped and spoke: "Your arrow has gone over seven lands and killed much people; today it comes home again, but neither stone nor iron can check its course, only my hoof. Now put a stone under my foot, so that the arrow on its return may strike into my hoof!" Katai-Khan did as the horse had instructed him, and Kesel Kar At stood there waiting for the arrow. Already one hears the sound of the arrow beyond several lands, and one could hardly blink with the eyes before it already struck against the horse's hoof. Then it rebounded nine fathoms back from the horse and lay there on the ground. Katai-Khan picked his arrow up again and put it in his quiver, sat again in the saddle with his boy, and came back into his ulus. He took his living gold arrow out of the quiver and laid it again in the gold chest.

After they had eaten and the boy had recovered, he said to his father: "Give me a name, and let me catch a horse from the tabun for myself." The father gave the seven atamans the order to drive the tabuns up; the boy himself, however, took a sixfold-coiled arkan and caught a hero's horse, which received the name Alten Tüktü Kesel Kar At. To his son the father gave the name Busälei Mirgän. Soon after that, Katai-Khan and his wife fell ill. When Katai-Khan was already near death, he spoke to his son: "When I die, you must not bury me in the earth, but make a coffin, bind the tops of nine larch trees together, and set the coffin on these tops." Then he added: "Never rely on your elder sister, for she has a bad mind; but on your younger sister you can safely rely." Then Katai-Khan died, and at the same time also his wife, as well as his horse Kesel Kar At. The boy buries his father and his mother in a coffin on the tops of nine larch trees. The horse, however, he buried in the earth, by the roots of the nine trees.

Soon after that the sea by the ulus dried up, and at the same time such extraordinary heat arose that people and cattle perished from it. Busälei Mirgän himself walks about full of care and does not know what to undertake in this need. Then the horse began to speak with a human tongue and said: "Sit on my back; let us ride to Kudai and call on him for help!" Busälei sat in the saddle, and suddenly the horse received silver wings on both sides. Busälei then flew on his horse toward heaven, tied his horse to the golden post, and entered Kudai's tent. Having entered the tent, he asks: "Good Kudai, for what reason has the sea dried up?" Kudai replies: "Do not ask me, but go to the sources of the sea and seek the cause there. Then come to me and say what you have seen." Busälei Mirgän returned home and did not even take time to eat, but at once continued his journey to the sources of the sea. He rides along the dried-up seabed and hears from afar the noise of rushing water. Cautiously he approaches the rushing place and thinks in his mind: "Surely there is someone there who dams the sea." In the distance he sees Kükat (the swan woman), who sits in the middle of the sea and with the two skirts of her garment pushes back all the water from both sides. Kükat has a nose seven spans long, and on one cheek a braid of hair hangs down.

Angrily Busälei Mirgän seized his bow, took an arrow from the quiver, and began to aim at the old woman. But he reflected and resolved to return home, to ask his elder sister whether he should shoot the old woman dead or not. The sister advised him not to shoot, but promised herself to try to win Kükat over in kindness. Kara Kuruptju sat on the horse's back and rode off to Kükat. Then she called from afar to Kükat: "Why do you dam the sea for us?" Kükat answers: "I have dammed the sea in order to induce you to come to me for a conversation. I am Kükat; my husband is called Djilbegen, and I have a son, Djider Mos, for whom I would like to have you as wife. Are you ready to take my son?" Kara Kuruptju answers: "How can I take your son as my husband without my brother's permission? Surely my brother would then kill me. If my brother were dead, I would indeed become your son's wife." Kükat promises to kill him, but Kara Kuruptju says: "How will you kill him, the greatest hero of the earth?" Kükat replies: "When your brother rides out hunting, I will come into the tent, turn myself into ashes, and let myself be drunk by him together with water. Having come into his stomach, I will cut his entrails with my knife." — "Then you will first let the sea return," Kara Kuruptju answers, "and I will tell my brother that I have taken your life." Kükat was satisfied with that, and both returned home, after Kara Kuruptju had promised to send her brother out hunting the following day.

Having come home, Kara Kuruptju tells Busälei Mirgän what she had promised the old woman, and Busälei Mirgän believes her word. In the morning she asked her brother to catch her some game for food, and the brother set out hunting. Soon after he had ridden away, Kükat appeared in the tent, and Kara Kuruptju entertained her in the best way. Toward evening one hears the hoofbeats of the horse Kesel Kar At. At once Kükat turns into a fly and sits on the kettle hook. Busälei Mirgän ties his horse to the post, enters the tent, and complains to his sister that during the whole day he had not found a single game animal. He demands something to drink from his sister, and Kara Kuruptju holds out to him a golden bowl full of airan. At the same moment the fly flew into the bowl, turned into ashes, and Busälei Mirgän drank her, knowing nothing, together with the drink into himself. In an instant Kükat cut with a knife into his heart. Busälei Mirgän saw at once that his sister had betrayed him and spoke: "Sister, you have eaten my life, for I, fool, forgot my father's warning!" Right after that Busälei Mirgän died, and at the same time also his horse; but Kükat took her former shape again, took Kara Kuruptju with her, and drove all the people and all the cattle away to her home.

Beneath the earth there is a hole, and through this hole Kükat leads her whole following with her under the earth. When they have gone through the hole, they come to the mouth of three black rivers, which at their outflow unite into one. Here Kükat had her tent, where she lived with her husband Djilbegen and her son Djider Mos. For Djider Mos and Kara Kuruptju she has a new tent erected, where they began to live together. In the first night Kara Kuruptju dreamed that her brother and his horse came back to life, and that Busälei Mirgän struck her, Kükat, and all the people who were found in the two tents dead. This dream she tells Kükat; the old woman, however, does not believe in the dream and begins to comfort Kara Kuruptju.

While she was still speaking, one hears hoofbeats, and, frightened, Kara Kuruptju runs back into her tent. She makes a hole in the tent wall and sees through the hole Busälei Mirgän riding up on his horse, with a whip in his hand. Kara Kuruptju runs weeping to meet her brother and says: "When you died, Kükat and her people came to me, bound me, and carried me away by force, together with all our property." Without saying a word, Busälei Mirgän bound her by her feet to the saddle, so that her head dragged on the ground. Now Kükat came running to meet Busälei Mirgän, to fight with him; but the hero strikes her with his whip, so that she sinks to the earth in two pieces. Then he took his sword, went to Djilbegen and Djider Mos, and killed them both. Then he returned home, dragging his sister behind him and whipping her incessantly on the way. All his cattle he also brought back home. Having come home, he was received in the tent by Kesel Djibäk, Kubai Kös, and Kubasen Arga. When Busälei Mirgän had died, Kesel Djibäk had asked Kudai to make him alive again. Kudai sent Kubai Kös and his sister Kubasen Arga to her, who called Busälei Mirgän back to life. Now these sat there and awaited Busälei Mirgän. When he came back, he took Kubasen Arga as his wife. Soon after that there came from another land Alten Mirgän and wooed Kesel Djibäk; Busälei Mirgän but answers: "If you promise to live with me, I will give you my sister; but if you want to have her at your own home, I will not give her to you." Alten Mirgän promised to live together with Buslei Mirgän, and now another wedding was celebrated. But over Kara Kuruptju the brother pronounced such a judgment that she should be burned on a pyre. Then Kubai Kös returned home, and Buslei Mirgän accompanied him halfway. Alten Mirgän then drove all his cattle to Buslei Mirgän, and now there was so much people and so much cattle at the tents that one could not get around them in less than three days. — After that neither heroes nor Ainas dared to touch them.



4. Küreldei Mirgän and Kümüs Arga

By the white sea under a high mountain lives Küreldei Mirgän with his sister Kümüs Arga. Their parents are long dead, and there is no other people in the tent. But horses and other cattle they have in plenty. One day Kümüs Arga says to her brother: "It is time for you to look around for a housewife. Kara-Chan has a daughter, Kairal Tjiseäk, who would be a good housekeeper for you. If you go to woo her, you will meet on the way seven foxes, which will run around you and bark at you like dogs. But do not touch them; go past as if you did not notice them. When you have gone away from them, you will meet seven wolves, which will likewise surround you and bark at you. Take care not to come near them, but go your way onward as if you did not notice them. When you have then come onto a high ridge, you will be seized by a violent horror; then you must take care not to look around."

Küreldei saddled his horse, swung himself into the saddle, and set out on the journey. Suddenly in the middle of the steppe seven foxes appeared, which surrounded Küreldei and barked at him. Küreldei remembered his sister's warning and paid no heed to the foxes, which soon left him. Küreldei continued his way across the steppe and was then attacked by seven wolves, which at once left him again when he gave the appearance of not perceiving them. When he then rode up the mountain from the steppe, he was unexpectedly seized by such fear that his whole body began to tremble. Although his sister had warned him not to look around, he nevertheless forgets his sister's warning and looks around on all sides. As he then goes down the mountain, a whistling is heard from all sides. Küreldei Mirgän looks around once more and sees a black dog with fiery eyes and bloody mouth. The dog sniffs at the ground, runs, sniffs again, and approaches Küreldei Mirgän. Küreldei Mirgän turns his horse around, seizes his sword, and rushes at the dog. In an instant the dog vanished, without Küreldei Mirgän noticing where it took its way. In the same moment Küreldei Mirgän felt very ill and decided to return home. With difficulty he rides back, lying across the saddle. When he was still far from the tent, he fell from the horse onto the steppe and died on the spot. The horse remains standing by its master and guards his dead body.

Kümüs Arga sits at home in the tent and knows everything that befalls her brother. When Küreldei Mirgän dies on the steppe, she takes a garment with eagle wings from the chest and flies weeping to her dead brother. Kümüs Arga loved her brother very much and sat down to guard him together with the horse. She covers him with leaves and clothes so that flies and worms might not come at the dead man. Kümüs Arga herself is ready to die beside her brother. Sitting by his body, she sees the dog with fiery eyes and bloody mouth running around, and behind the dog runs a red-haired horse. On the horse sits a man who has at his belt a spear many fathoms long and a sword in his hand. The dog runs past Kümüs Arga, and after it gallops at full speed the man on his unsaddled horse. Soon the man returns with his horse and leads the dog behind him on an iron halter. The man has forged the dog's mouth shut with an iron ring. The man went past the girl without speaking a word. Kümüs Arga decides to follow in his tracks; she sat on Küreldei Mirgän's horse Kümüs tüktü Kurag at (the silver-haired brown-white horse) and rode away. She rode across the steppe, up the mountain, and saw by the white sea the tent of the man who had led the dog. The horse stands there bound to the post. The girl rode downhill and bound her horse to the same post. Then she went into the tent. The host sleeps on the bed under a silk cover; by the fire sits a beautiful girl and sews at a garment. Kümüs Arga hears a sound behind her, turns around, and sees the dog with fiery eyes bound at the tent door. Kümüs Arga is silent, and the other also speaks no word; the host continues to sleep.

When the host finally awoke, he took Kümüs Arga by the hand and led her so that she sat on the bed. The host was called Kan Mirgän, and his horse bore the name Kan bozerag at (the blood-red horse). His sister, who sits by the fire, is called Alten Bös. Kan Mirgän orders Alten Bös to give Kümüs Arga a good garment instead of her own, which was soaked by the rain. The sister does not move from the spot, but Kan Mirgän himself lays a silk garment around Kümüs Arga. Then Kan Mirgän entertains her with food, and after she has eaten, Kan Mirgän asks Kümüs Arga who she is. Kümüs Arga names her name and adds that her brother Küreldei lies dead on the steppe. Kan Mirgän has sixty remedies and seventy words (properly tongues), and he decides to try his art on Küreldei. He leaves Kümüs Arga behind in his tent and asks his sister to care for her. He orders his sister to keep the dog bound, for there is no hero in the world who could measure himself with this dog, which is no dog but an Aina. Without saddling his horse, Kan Mirgän rides away. Then Alten Bös takes an iron bar, strikes Kümüs Arga on the head with it, takes the silk garment from her, and says: "You did not help me sew, so you shall not wear my garment either. Not Kan Mirgän is host here, but I." Then she unties the dog, takes the ring from its mouth, and lets it run away. Then Alten Bös and Kümüs Arga sit in the tent without speaking a word with each other.

While they sit, hoofbeats of more than one horse are heard from the mountain down. The riders who have arrived bind their horses to the post. Kan Mirgän entered the tent with a joyful face, and Küreldei Mirgän follows him, alive again. While brother and sister welcome each other, Kan Mirgän notices that the dog is gone and asks his sister how it got loose. The sister answers no word, but Kan Mirgän seizes Alten Bös by her sixty hair-braids and strikes her on the back with the whip. Küreldei Mirgän pleads for Alten Bös, and Kan Mirgän stops whipping his sister. Kan Mirgän and Küreldei now became good friends and brothers. Kümüs Arga then returned home, but Küreldei Mirgän went to Kara-Chan to woo his daughter. Kan Mirgän remained behind in his tent.

Küreldei Mirgän rides over steppes and mountains and after a long journey came to a high mountain by a black sea. On the shore of the sea lives Kara-Chan, and around his tent horses stand bound at all posts, and the tents do not contain the people. Küreldei Mirgän saw that Kara-Chan was celebrating a feast and stands on the mountain full of wonder at the multitude of people and horses. Kara-Chan's people perceived Küreldei Mirgän as he stood on the mountain and reported the hero's arrival to Kara-Chan. When Kara-Chan heard this, he spoke: "A good man must be honored! Six men shall bind his horse and seven men bring him here." The attendants obeyed his command, bound the horse, and led Küreldei into the Chan's tent. In the tent sit sixty heroes who have come to woo Kara-Chan's daughter. As soon as Küreldei entered, all sixty heroes stood up. Kara-Chan leads Küreldei to the bed, has airan set before him, and asks his name. Küreldei Mirgän tells him who he is, and Kara-Chan says that he had already heard of his fame. Kara-Chan asks further about his purpose, and Küreldei Mirgän answers: "If one meets a red goat, one shoots it; if one finds a beautiful girl, one takes her. I have come," Küreldei continues, "to woo your daughter." Kara-Chan at once promises him his daughter and asks his people to arrange the wedding; in twelve cauldrons food is cooked, and the airan flows in large vessels.

While the wedding is being celebrated, hoofbeats are heard in the distance and the earth trembles at it. It is Kan Mirgän's horse, which comes running to Kara-Chan without its master. When Küreldei Mirgän saw the horse of his hero-brother, he was frightened, went to meet the horse, and embraces its neck. The horse begins to speak with a human voice: "While Kan Mirgän slept, the dog with the fiery eyes took him in its mouth and ran away with him. Sit on my back and follow me, and I will show you the place where Kan Mirgän now is." Küreldei sits on the horse's back, and the horse begins to speak: "Beyond seven lands stands a high, white mountain, and on this mountain is now the dog with Kan Mirgän. Today the dog sleeps on the mountain, and after that it does not sleep for seven years. If we do not come to the mountain today, your brother will perish." Then the horse instructs Küreldei not to strike the dog, because the dog could awaken from the blow. Bozerag at itself then ran with such light steps that the hoofbeats were not heard, and Kurag at follows its master in the tracks without a rider.

At midday Küreldei came to the mountain and found the dog sleeping on its side. Küreldei climbed down from the horse's back and approached the dog with light steps, armed with a sword, an iron chain, and an iron ring. Quietly he fastened the ring around the dog's mouth and the chain around its neck, bound its feet fast, sharpened his sword, and opened the dog's belly. From the belly Kan Mirgän came forth healthy and unharmed, only he was deprived of his hair. Then Küreldei Mirgän took him to himself, washed him with seawater and wild rosemary, whereupon Kan Mirgän received his hair again. Then Kan Mirgän thanked Küreldei heartily, who had saved him from death; but Küreldei Mirgän reminded Kan Mirgän that he had earlier given him his life back. "See here," said Kan Mirgän, "this black dog, which is worse than all evil. But worse than this dog is my sister. If we do not kill her, we will not live long on earth." After they had burned the dog, both hero-brothers went on their horses to Kara-Chan and continued the wedding already begun.

When the wedding was over, Kara-Chan had a six-footed horse caught, presented Küreldei Mirgän with the horse and rich gifts, and took leave of him and of his daughter. Küreldei with his wife and Kan Mirgän traveled together, and Kan Mirgän invited Küreldei with his wife on the way into his tent. They went into the tent, and when Kan Mirgän entered, he drew his sword and gave his sister such a blow with it that she died at once. Then he dragged her onto a high mountain and burned her body to ashes. Thereupon Küreldei Mirgän invites his battle-brother to himself and promises him his sister in marriage. Kan Mirgän accepted the invitation, took all his property with him, and followed Küreldei to his tent. Arrived there, they prepared two tents and arranged a wedding for Kan Mirgän and Kümüs Arga. They celebrated the wedding seven days long, nine days long, and then lived in peace, without a hero or an Aina daring to come at them.



5. Alten Kök

In a tent lived a three-year-old boy and a seven-year-old girl, without father, without mother. They lived alone in the tent, but had many horses and other cattle. The boy had a horse, but neither he nor his horse had a name. One morning, when the boy and the girl had just risen, hoofbeats were heard in the distance. The sister makes a hole in the tent wall and sees two heroes riding up on two horses. They bound their horses to the golden post and entered the yurt. The two heroes bowed before the boy and said: "A fired bullet does not fear the stone, and a sent man does not fear even princes." The heroes continued: "We are sent to you by seventy Chans, seventy heroes, and seven Kudais. From the earth an Aina has come up who is called Ai-kün (Moon and Sun). The gods, heroes, and princes now ask you to come and fight with this Aina. No hero can defeat him. The seven Kudais weep, but from the earth seven Ainas have come forth who rejoice and sing songs. Perhaps you can defeat him."

The sister will not let her brother go from her, since he is still too young and weak for such fights, but the boy scolds his sister and speaks: "You are foolish! Whom else should one obey but the gods, princes, and heroes?" He saddled his horse, sat on its back, and was about to ride away. But in the same moment the sister seizes the halter and says that she would not let him go unless he took her with him on the horse. The boy falls into anger, takes his sword, cuts the halter through, and pushes the sister away from him. He struck his horse and rides away together with the two heroes. He came to a high mountain by a white sea. There stands a tent, and by the sea wander Chans and heroes, and on a cloud sit seven Kudais looking down on the earth. On the mountain lies Ai-kün with his hand under his ears and looks around. When he looks toward heaven, the clouds flame; when he looks toward the earth, the grass flames. The seven Ainas stand under the mountain, protruding to the middle from the earth, laugh and sing.

When Ai-kün sees the boy, he smiles and speaks: "They told me that from the white mountain a white horse would come; this, however, is a hare. They spoke of a poplar-high hero; this here is only a little boy." The boy became angry in his mind, sprang down from the horse's back, and gave Ai-kün a blow on the cheek with his clenched fist. Ai-kün now stood up and began to wrestle with the boy. They wrestled three whole days, and Ai-kün gains the upper hand, yet cannot kill the boy. On the fourth day the boy's strength increased, so that both fought each other equally. The boy tries to pull the Aina up the mountain, but the Aina wants to pull the boy to the sea. The Aina succeeded in getting the boy down from the mountain, and now they continued to wrestle on the seashore. They wrestle a whole year, and the fight is watched by the seven Kudais and the seven Ainas and seventy heroes and Chans. As they wrestle, the earth trembles and the sea swells, so that the water floods the steppe and the cattle drown. Men and cattle remain on the mountain, but the boy and the Aina fight in the water. In the sea one sees red and black boiling streams of blood. The Kudais say: "The red blood comes from the boy, and the black is the blood of the Aina." While they fight, the boy takes the Aina on his shoulders and carries him up the mountain. While he carries the Aina on his shoulders, he also binds his hands and feet. Having come onto the mountain, the boy takes his sword, cuts large pieces of flesh from the Aina's body with it, and forces him to eat his own flesh by threatening him that he would otherwise burn him. Here the boy asks the Aina: "Say, where is your soul? For if you had it with you, I would have killed you long ago." The Aina answers: "On my horse a sack is bound fast; in the sack is a snake; the snake has twelve heads, and my soul the snake has. If you kill the snake, you kill me too." The boy went to the horse, untied the sack, and with one blow cut off all twelve heads of the snake, whereupon both the Aina and the snake perished. The horse also perished. In the same moment the seven Ainas vanished; the seven Kudais, the seventy princes and heroes, however, bowed before the boy and admired him. Then they gave him the name Alten Kök (Gold-Cuckoo), and the sister received the name Alten Argöl (Gold Cloth). — When Alten Kök had overcome the Aina, he bowed before the seven Kudais and asked them to send someone to his sister with the message that he was still alive. The messenger returned and reported that the sister still stood on the same spot where the brother had pushed her before the departure, that she was indeed still alive but very weak. At the news of her brother she had become very glad and had recovered.

In the ulus there was a beautiful girl, Ajazen Ko, and the seven Kudais and all princes and heroes gave her to Alten Kök as wife. The seven Kudais bless Alten Kök and Ajazen Ko, but at the wedding Alten Kök is served by all Chans and princes. The wedding is celebrated seven days, nine days long. Then the seven Kudais make Alten Kök Chan over all seventy Chans.

When the wedding had been celebrated, Alten Kök returns to his homeland, and the seven Kudais command that on the way six heroes shall ride before and seven behind him. The girl's father, Alten Chan, accompanied Alten Kök on the way. Come home, Alten Kök arranged a great feast. While the feast is being celebrated, Alten Kök hears his horse neigh at night. He went out of the tent and found on the horse a writing that read thus: "The seven Kudais call Alten Argöl to live with them and not to marry." Alten Argöl washed herself with wild rosemary and seawater, took leave of her brother, and went with her garment with eagle wings to the seven Kudais.

Alten Kök lives at home in peace; neither heroes nor Ainas dare to touch him.



6. Alten Bürtjük

In a wretched otak[Footnote: otak is a poor camp that serves instead of a tent.] covered with hay lived an eight-year-old boy who was very poor and wore very bad clothes. The boy was fatherless and motherless and lived alone in his otak. His only property consists of a three-year-old horse, and his only work is to feed, stroke, and care for the foal. To fill his own stomach, he sets traps for hares and capercaillies. He had no other food. The young foal that the boy had ate as strongly as a whole tabun. Within one day it ate the grass of a whole steppe. One evening, when the horse had stopped eating, the boy bound it for the night to a tree and went himself into his otak. He lay down to sleep, and as soon as the sun rose in the morning, he went again to look after his foal. Arrived at the place, he sees only the head, the feet, the tail, and the mane of the foal; everything else the wolf had eaten up. At this sight the boy became anxious and began to weep; he consoled himself, however, and spoke: "I endure hunger and bear hardships."

Then he made a noose around the remains of his foal and asks Kudai, speaking: "If the wolf has eaten my foal, then may the wolf fall into the noose, and if an Aina has eaten the foal, then may the Aina be caught in it!" He then went again into his otak, looked on the way at his hare and capercaillie traps, and so came home. He prepared his evening meal and lay down to sleep; but from grief over his eaten foal he could not fall asleep. The following morning he stood up early and went again to see the remains of the dear animal. Going up the mountain, he sees something shining like a sun in the noose that he had set beside the corpse: it is a white, gold-haired wolf three fathoms long. The wolf is still alive and struggles in the noose. The boy approaches the wolf, seizes him by the tail, and strikes him with his whip on the back so that all the hair falls out. In his distress the wolf asks the boy for mercy and speaks: "If you stop striking me, I promise to do for you whatever you may demand of me." The boy replies: "What good has anyone ever seen a wolf do? You have eaten up my horse, and for that I will whip the life out of you." The wolf continued to beg him and spoke: "I am Bärü-Chan (the Wolf-Chan), and six hundred wolves obey me; and one of the Chans of the earth and seventy Chans are subject to me. I can live as wolf and as man. If you stop striking me, I promise you by God that I will give you from my tabun as many horses as you wish." The boy hesitated at first to release the wolf-prince, but when the latter had sworn his words by God, the boy nevertheless let him out of the noose and followed in his tracks.

When they had gone a stretch of way, they came onto a steppe that was full of horses, all of which belonged to the wolf-prince. These horses were guarded by nine herdsmen, who at the wolf-prince's command bowed before the boy and led before him nine of the best horses and nine of the most beautiful garments, from which the boy could choose what pleased him most. The boy looks at the horses and garments, which were all so splendid that for a long time he was undecided which of them he should choose. Bärü-Chan had meanwhile removed himself, and while the boy stands and chooses, an old, gray-haired man appears, who gives the boy the advice to touch neither the garments nor the horses, but to follow Bärü-Chan on foot in his tracks. Further the old man instructs him that still further on the way nine horses and nine garments will be brought to him, but he should not accept them. Finally the old man warns him not to reveal to Bärü-Chan who had given him the advice not to accept the gifts.

The boy followed the old man's advice and went on the same way that Bärü-Chan had already gone. Soon he came onto a steppe that was likewise filled with horses belonging to Bärü-Chan and guarded by nine herdsmen. These too had been instructed by Bärü-Chan to offer the boy nine of the very best horses and nine of the stateliest garments, and all these things were so lovely that the boy forgot the old man's warning and was already about to choose the best for himself, when in the same moment the gray-haired old man came to him and asked him to leave Bärü-Chan's property untouched. The boy obeyed the old man's warning and went again on foot further, following Bärü-Chan in his tracks. So he came to a third steppe, which was likewise filled with Bärü-Chan's horses. Here too nine horses had been chosen and nine garments prepared for the boy, and nine herdsmen came to the boy, bowed before him, and asked him to choose a horse and a garment. Again the boy forgot the warning he had received and wanted to choose a horse and a garment, when the gray-haired old man appeared and asked the boy to continue his way on foot in his old clothing. At the same time he tells the boy that he will soon come to Bärü-Chan, who wants to give him half of his property; he warns the boy, however, not to accept Bärü-Chan's offer, but advises him to ask from him only a cat, which he would see in Bärü-Chan's tent. After he had given this advice, the old man vanished, and the boy went to Bärü-Chan. Come to a mountain, he sees from the mountain top the ulus of Bärü-Chan by the white sea. The boy descends the mountain and goes straight into Bärü-Chan's tent. Here Bärü-Chan lies on his divan, with a golden staff in his hand. As soon as the boy entered, Bärü-Chan rose, led him to the divan, and asked him to sit close beside him. Then he had the boy clothed in a splendid garment, food set before him, and honored him like a Chan. For three days Bärü-Chan entertains him; on the fourth he leads him out of the tent, has his servants drive all horses and all his cattle together, and offers the boy half of his herds. The boy asks the Chan to leave the division for the time being, takes Bärü-Chan by the hand, and leads him back into the tent. Come into the tent, Bärü-Chan has all his clothes and his other possessions brought out and continues to offer the boy the promised half of his property. Then the boy speaks: "What should I do with your herd and your property? I have neither wife nor servant to guard such great treasures. It is better that you give me your cat, for with this I can manage even unmarried and without servants." Bärü-Chan looks at the boy with wide-open mouth, and tears come into his eyes. But at the thought of his promise sworn by God, he must resign himself to give up the cat, although he would rather have given half of his property. The boy took a rope, bound the cat with it, put on his old clothing, put the cat in his bosom, and took leave of Bärü-Chan. At the boy's departure Bärü-Chan accompanied him to the nearby mountain, and while he speaks with the boy, his eyes are directed at the boy's bosom. When they finally part, Bärü-Chan says: "When you come into your otak, do not forget to take good care of the cat, so that it suffers neither hunger nor cold. Nourish it with the same food that you yourself enjoy, and clothe it with such clothes as you yourself wear." Then Bärü-Chan returns to his tent, and the boy continues his way home.

Without any adventure the boy reached his otak. Here he found dried hare and capercaillie meat, which he cooked and began to eat at once after his homecoming. He takes the cat with him to eat. Every piece that he eats he shares with the cat; and from every bowl of soup that he drinks, he also lets the cat get its share. When he lies down to sleep, he binds the cat to a pole in the otak and covers it with his own clothes. When the boy sleeps, the cat also sleeps, and when the boy awakes at night, the cat also awakes. The boy wonders at the cat's understanding, which seemed to him not like human understanding. In the morning the boy stood up, went out to catch hares and capercaillies, and left the cat bound in the otak. After he had caught as much as he wished, he returned to his otak. On the way he hears a singing like from a girl, and the singing is so beautiful that birds and other animals sit still and listen to it. This singing seems to him to come from the otak, but when he had come into the otak, he sees no one there except his cat, which, bound to the otak pole, sits quite still.

The boy now prepares a midday meal and feeds the cat as Bärü-Chan had wished. After the midday meal he lay down to rest, but soon awoke from an extraordinary noise that is heard around him. On awakening the boy finds himself in a tent that is adorned just like Bärü-Chan's, and around the tent the steppe is full of servants, horses, and other cattle. The cat had vanished, but instead of it he sees a girl loosening her many hair-braids and making them into two braids again, as a sign that she is no longer a girl but the boy's wife.[Footnote: Tatar girls wear up to twelve braids; married women, however, only two.] Properly she was Bärü-Chan's daughter, who at the boy's arrival at Bärü-Chan's had only been transformed into a cat and now received her former shape again. After the boy's departure Bärü-Chan had sent half of his property as his daughter's dowry. At the same time he had also sent an ataman, Albang Mirgän, to guard and administer his daughter's property.

At the time when this happened, there lived on earth a mighty prince, Jedai-Chan, who had a son, Jöbet Mirgän; both were mighty heroes. Jöbet Mirgän had long wooed Bärü-Chan's daughter, Alten Bürtjük, but when he heard that she had been given to the boy, he became very angry and decided to take revenge on the boy. The father tries to hold his son back, in the opinion that there are still other women on earth for him, but the son asks the father most urgently to be allowed to go away to take revenge and to carry off Alten Bürtjük from the boy, who because of his low origin and his poverty is not worthy of such a wife. Against his will Jedai-Chan finally had to grant his son's desire, and Jöbet Mirgän set out on the journey. Come to the boy's tent, he bound his horse to the golden post and entered the tent. Here he sat on the bed, and Alten Bürtjük entertained him with food and drink, but the boy sits on his place and speaks no word. At first Jöbet Mirgän will drink no wine, but unnoticed pours everything that Alten Bürtjük sets before him into his bosom. Alten Bürtjük notices this and also takes care herself. However much Jöbet Mirgän urges her to drink, she only brings the bowl to her mouth and pours the wine into her sleeve. But the boy drinks as much as he can. When the boy was already a little drunk, Jöbet Mirgän spoke: "Let us enter into a wager; we will hide ourselves and seek each other. If you find me in my hiding place, you take all my property and I become your slave; but if I find you, I take you, your wife, and all your property." The boy agreed to this proposal, and Jöbet Mirgän went on his way home. The boy prepares to follow in his tracks, and Alten Bürtjük gives him at his departure the following advice: "When you come into Jöbet Mirgän's tent, you see a bow and arrow on a chest. Take the bow and the arrow, throw and bend the bow and the arrow as you find good, for you must know that Jöbet Mirgän has transformed himself into this bow and arrow."

With this advice the boy goes away, making the way on foot. Jöbet Mirgän meanwhile comes to his tent, and Jedai-Chan receives him with sorrow and grief, speaking: "In vain you enter into a dispute with Alten Bürtjük, for this dispute ends with our common death. See, the seven Kudais of heaven lived with us in harmony, the seven Ainas of the underworld were subject to us, and seventy princes of the earth obeyed us. Now everything is lost to us through your unfortunate dispute with Alten Bürtjük, and she will also turn us into stones if you do not stop disputing with Alten Bürtjük." Hardly had the old man ended these words when the boy also entered the tent, saw a bow and arrow on the chest, and began to bend and tread on the bow with all his strength. Jöbet Mirgän thereby falls into fear and distress and asks the boy for mercy. Jedai-Chan also stood up, bowed before the boy, and asked him to reconcile himself with Jöbet Mirgän. The boy will enter into no reconciliation, but asks Jöbet Mirgän now on his part to seek him. Thereupon the boy goes home. Jöbet Mirgän follows him close in his tracks and will not let him out of his eyes. When the boy entered the tent, Jöbet Mirgän already stood on the last mountain. Come home, the boy considers where he could best hide from Jöbet Mirgän. But Alten Bürtjük transforms him into a needle and hides the needle in her fur coat.

Come into the tent, Jöbet Mirgän seeks the boy everywhere, but cannot find him. The whole day he seeks the boy in vain, but when the sun went down, Jöbet Mirgän and his horse were transformed into stone. Alten Bürtjük now tells the boy that Jedai-Chan too had been transformed into a stone, and asks the boy to set out to bring Jedai-Chan's property home. She adds further that half of Jedai-Chan's people had been defeated in war and were under the rule of the Aina. Alten Bürtjük asks the boy not to take this people home with him, but to assign them a special place as dwelling place. Further Alten Bürtjük gives the boy the following advice: "In Jedai-Chan's tent you find among the other possessions a hidden chest, and in this chest Jedai-Chan has hidden the soul of your foal, after he had sought it in the other world and therefore taken possession of it, because the owner of this foal is the greatest hero on earth. If you open the chest, you find in it a sword, and this you must yourself bring to the tent with your own hands; but see that you do not let the sword fall from your hands on the way, for this is your death. But if you come home happily with the sword, you will be the greatest hero of the earth, and then death will never have power over you."

The boy sets out, comes to Jedai-Chan's tent, sees the horse petrified at the post, and in the tent Jedai-Chan himself sits petrified. The seven Kudais rejoice over the boy's luck, for Jedai-Chan was half from the lineage of the Ainas, and Kudai had no power over him. Meanwhile the boy opens the chest and finds in it a sword that shines like a burning flame. The boy took the sword and charged the ataman to bring all people and all property home; but he asks the ataman to let all the warred people dwell on the army shore, a day's journey from his tent. The boy came happily to the tent with his sword, and now Alten Bürtjük asks what the boy had done with the head, the mane, the tail, and the feet of the foal, which had not been eaten by the wolf. All this the boy had hidden in his otak and still had it in his keeping. Alten Bürtjük took the remains of the foal into her care and asked the boy to go to rest.

When he awoke in the morning and stepped out of the tent, he sees a gold-haired horse bound to the post, saddled and hung with bow, arrows, and a hero's armor. He named his horse after its color Alten tüktü bözerag at (the gold-haired, red-colored horse), and himself took the name Alten Kök (Gold-Cuckoo). Now Alten Kök arranges a great wedding, to which he invites his father-in-law Bärü-Chan and much other people. At the wedding Bärü-Chan expresses that in his high age he wants to live not alone, but together with his daughter and his son-in-law. He then has all his cattle driven to Alten Kök, and the whole seacoast is taken up by Alten Kök's cattle and other possessions. Now Alten Bürtjük says to Alten Kök: "Under the earth there live at Jedai-Chan's seventy boyars and seven hundred other men. After three days they come to make war on you, and either you kill them or they take the life from you and the people." Alten Kök, instead of waiting for them at home, arms himself and rides on his gold-colored horse to meet them, promising at the same time to return after three years if he should still be alive. He thus set out on the journey and remained away so long that Alten Bürtjük in the meantime could bear him a son. The boy from the hero lineage grew up quickly, began to speak and to walk, and played outside on the field.

One evening it happened that the boy was nowhere to be found on the field. He had gone to Jedai-Chan's warred people, and here he had seen six men standing under a rock and a seventh Tasol, that is, a stone-head. These men were strong heroes who played a hero game that consisted in throwing large stones into the sea. And every stone that they threw down into the water in the morning came swimming back to the coast at midday. The six men held counsel with each other and wanted to murder the boy, but the seventh advised them against their murder plan. Thereupon the six men took a rope, bound Tasol and the boy with it, and threw them out into the wide sea.
