# Forty Wedding Songs — A SELECTION

**from Antanas & Jonas Juška's *Lietuviškos svotbinės dainos*
("Lithuanian Wedding Songs"), Kazan, 1883**
A fresh English rendering, plain and singable

---

**A fresh translation, not a reprint — and a selection, not the
whole book.** This booklet renders forty wedding songs, chosen
from the roughly eleven hundred in the Juška brothers' 1883
*Svotbinės dainos*, into plain modern English. The forty are
picked for variety across the whole course of the old Lithuanian
wedding — the wooing and the matchmaker's embassy, the betrothal
bargain, the bride's farewell to her parents, the carting of the
dowry chest, the wedding morning, the feast and its jests, and
married life after — and for the completeness of the Lithuanian
text in the scan. What is here is the editor's choosing: forty
songs out of some eleven hundred, numbered 1–40 in this
selection's own order.

Original: Lithuanian wedding songs collected by Antanas Juška
(1819–1880), edited after his death by his brother Jonas Juška
(1815–1886), printed at Kazan, 1883. Basis for this rendering: the
Internet Archive's digitization of the 1883 Kazan printing,
identifier `lietuviskos-svotbines-dainos-1883` (OCR text
`..._djvu.txt`, about 1.15 MB, read 2026-10-11). The forty songs
were drawn from all three parts of the volume — the songs before
the wedding, the songs sung during it, and the songs of the
return — with the book's own ritual sections (the wooing, the
matchmaking, the betrothal, the viewing, the maiden's evening,
the send-off, the dowry-carting, the church-going, the bedding,
the headdressing, the korovai, and the rest) guiding the choice.

**How it is presented:** the songs in clear modern English, plain
enough for a humble singer to follow, in short lyric lines as
sung. Translated from the Lithuanian verses themselves — not from
the Russian summaries interleaved in the volume. This is a
selection of forty from about eleven hundred: the songs are
numbered 1–40 for this booklet and grouped by the wedding's
course, with a short English descriptive title over each. Where
the old orthography or the OCR defeats a certain reading, the
English says what can be said and marks the rest —
[obscured in the scan] — rather than inventing it. A short
preface explains the conventions, and a note describes the state
of the Lithuanian text.

**Reader note:** These are village ritual songs — sung at the
table, on the road, at the dancing, at the leave-taking. Some are
tender, some are bargaining, some are pure mischief (a rat served
for the wedding roast; a matchmaker mistaking a red cat for the
bride). Nothing in them is praised or modernized — they are
presented as the singers sang them, for the reader to judge.
The rue wreath, the painted dowry chest, the bay horses, the
cuckoo that is the married daughter come home: these are the
songs' own furniture, kept as found.

## Dedication

**Antanas Juška (1819–1880) and Jonas Juška (1815–1886)** — two
Lithuanian priest-brothers who did their collecting under the
Tsarist Lithuanian press ban, which had outlawed their people's
printed word. Antanas gathered some 1,586 songs and compiled a
70,000-word dictionary of the language; he moved to Kazan to see
the work through the press, sickened there, and died on
1 November 1880, thirteen months into the printing. Jonas carried
the 1,100 wedding songs into print posthumously in 1883, then gave
his remaining years to his brother's dictionary — and died on
11 May 1886, two days after a letter arrived saying the Academy
was taking the work from him. The dictionary reached the letter K
by 1922 and was never finished.

This booklet is a selection of forty songs from their wedding-song
volume — forty out of the eleven hundred, chosen by an editor,
not the whole book. The brothers' larger *Dainos* collection
still waits on a verified original-edition scan before any
Englishing is attempted.

## How to read this book

The singers' own words are kept in Lithuanian in parentheses at
first use, since the translation cannot carry all of them:

- *svotba* — the wedding; *svotbinės dainos* — wedding songs.
- *piršlys* — the matchmaker, the go-between who rides to ask for
  the girl; *svočia* — the married woman who manages the bride's
  side of the feast.
- *kraitis* — the dowry chest (the painted *skrynė*), carted from
  the bride's granary; *pasoga* — the dowry itself.
- *vajnikas* (modern *vainikas*) — the wreath; the bride's wreath
  of green *rūta* (rue) is her maidenhood, taken off at the
  wedding.
- *močiūtė* — mother; *tėvėlis* — father (both in the songs'
  diminutive, affectionate form); *anyta* — the mother-in-law.
- *mergvakaris* — the maiden's evening, the bride's farewell
  party; *vinčius* — the church wedding; *karvojus* (modern
  *karvojus/korovai*) — the wedding bread, shared out at the
  feast; *mūturas* — the married woman's headdress, put on after
  the bedding.
- *sudiėv* — "farewell," said to father, mother, and all kin.

Diminutives are the songs' grammar of affection — nearly every
noun comes small and dear (*žirgėlis*, the dear horse; *sesėlė*,
the dear sister). The English keeps them plain and lets the
smallness stand where the line allows it. Bracketed notes in the
form [Editor's note] are the editor's, not the singers'. Where
the scan defeats the reading, the English marks it
[obscured in the scan] rather than guessing.

## Note on the Lithuanian text

The source is the raw OCR of an 1883 printing in the brothers'
own phonetic spelling — pre-reform orthography with heavy
diacritics and Polish inflection (*kajp* for *kaip*, *s4vu* for
*savu*, *močiūtė* for *motutė*). The translation was made by
reading through the noise to the Lithuanian beneath; uncertain
readings are flagged, never smoothed over. Two transmission scars
are left visible throughout: the print's two-column page layout,
which the OCR interleaves line by line (the English re-sorts the
columns into stanzas), and the page footers (*LIETUVIŠKOS
SVOTBINĖS DAINOS* with the page number) that surface mid-song in
the scan. The volume's Russian summaries, interleaved with the
songs, were used only to find the ritual sections — no line of
the English is translated from the Russian. A duplicate-scan
check of the whole OCR file (every 40-word window hashed) found
no repeated song-text, so each song is translated once, straight
through; where one song's body in the scan runs into the next
song's header, the break is made at the numbered header.

### 29. Why weave a wreath? Wait till morning

### 30. The matchmaker and the red cat

[Sung when he crawls under the coverlet to look for the bride,
and the girls, pressing her wreath on her head with their palms,
fight him off.]

Here doors, there doors —
the matchmaker about the rooms.

He meets an old woman —
says it's the girl.

The matchmaker looks in the corner —
finds a red cat.

The matchmaker says that it's the girl —
here's the red cat.

The matchmaker tried to kiss —
she scratched his beard.

That matchmaker, a good man —
it didn't go well for him.

### 31. Make room — the dance

[Sung after the korovai and the mead have been shared out, going

groom dance with the bride. When they're seated, all who wish go

At my father's, my father's homestead,
the floors are laid with maple boards,
strewn pretty with pepper,

Make room on the floor —
I'll dance with my brother.

When I went to dance with my brother,
they carried me young on their arms,
they carried me young on their arms,
going through the hall they fixed my wreath.

Make room on the floor —
I'll dance with the lad.

When I went to dance with the lad,
they tossed me young aside,
they tossed me young aside,
with their spurs they tore my apron.

### 36. The widower mocked

Widower, old man —
what were you thinking,
to set eyes on a young girl?

I heard you didn't love
your first —
you always had an angry heart
toward her.

Your days have already
faded in old age;
they've left you
with little children.

Widower, old man,
you looked for another,
always speaking
your first one's name.

The wind blew
and broke the tree branches,
and people battered
us two young ones with words.

The [obscured] wind
having broken the tree branches,
people kept battering
us two young ones with words.

### 37. The cuckoo daughter comes home

O he lets her go, he gives —
the father his daughter —
into a foreign land.

And he, letting her go,
giving her —
didn't intend to visit.

"O visit, visit me, father,
at least once a year."

"You'll find me in the flax field,
pulling flax."

"And I, waiting and not waiting
for my old father —
I'd turn into a speckled cuckoo
and fly to the green forest."

"I'd fly off, I'd speed off
to father's orchard."

"I'd perch, I'd dart
into the top of the cherry tree.
I'd cuckoo morning and evening —
no one would hear me."

"But old mother would hear
through the glass window."

"O mother pelted and [scared] me
with red apples."

"Don't throw, don't scare me, mother —
I do little harm."

"On one I sat, on the second I [obscured],
from the third I gathered sickness." [obscured in the scan]

"By her voice, by her [obscured],
she'd be my daughter."

"By her magic, by her feathers,
she'd be the forest cuckoo."

### 39. At mother's, at mother-in-law's

When I was with mother,
I lived in great idleness;
I never went through the yard
without a rue wreath.

When I went through the yard,
I brushed the morning dew;
everyone admired me
and lifted their caps.

When I was with my mother-in-law,
I toiled greatly;
I never went through the yard
without bitter tears.

### 40. No mother, no father

I have no mother
or father;
my heart is troubled,
no one to advise me.

My heart is troubled,
no one to advise;
my lad is gone,
no one to talk with.

I go through the field,
through the green forest,
and I hear the cuckoo,
the cuckoo's voice.

Not the cuckoo's voice,
not a forest bird —
only my lad
yearning for me.
