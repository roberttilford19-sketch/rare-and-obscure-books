## Dedication

**Johannes de Muris (Jean des Murs)** — who he was: a French
scholar of the 1300s, mathematician, astronomer, and music
theorist, working in the Paris of the ars nova. The verifiable
record (Grove Music Online, via Wikipedia's "Johannes de Muris")
gives five music treatises with their working dates: *Notitia
artis musicae* (1319-21), *Compendium musicae practicae* (c. 1322),
*Musica speculativa secundum Boetium* (1323), the *Libellus cantus
mensurabilis* (c. 1340), and *Ars contrapuncti* (after 1340); his
latest datable association is 1345, with the Avignon reforms. His
mathematical and astronomical writings — above all the
*Quadripartitum numerorum* of 1343 — were well regarded, but the
same record says his musical writings "were more widely
circulated": many surviving manuscripts, much of them
fifteenth-century and of Italian origin, "suggesting his wide
influence both geographical and temporal."

The circumstances of his death are not given in the record, and
are not pretended here.

Why his work is vital — proved, not praised: the manuscript
record is the proof. Of all medieval music textbooks, few were
copied so widely or used so long: the *Libellus* became the
standard short account of the new measured notation — the five
note-values, the perfect/imperfect machinery, imperfection and
alteration — quoted, borrowed, and argued with by theorists for a
century after. The anonymous *Ars cantus mensurabilis mensurata per
modos iuris* (edited 1994) takes the *Libellus*'s mensural theory,
in the editor's words, as lying "at the heart" of its work. A book
that teaches, clearly and in order, how every note of a motet knows
its length is a book that made the music of its age possible.

Its relation to this rescue: the Boneyard's mensural-notation row
runs Franco of Cologne (the invention of counted note-values),
Aegidius de Murino (the workshop manual for building motets), and
now the *Libellus* — the classroom text that carried the ars nova's
rhythmic system across Europe in copy after copy. Where Franco
announces and Aegidius builds, the *Libellus* teaches. The series
rescues the working knowledge of dead masters; this is the lesson
book by which the masters' apprentices learned the work.

Where a reader goes next: Edmond de Coussemaker, *Scriptorum de
musica medii aevi nova series a Gerbertina altera*, vol. 3 (Paris:
A. Durand, 1864), pp. 46-58 — the printed home of the Latin text;
the Thesaurus Musicarum Latinarum, entry MURLIB, for the
searchable transcription (with further manuscript copies entered
as MURLIBF, MURLIBM, MURLIBR, MURLIBV); and Grove Music Online,
"Muris, Johannes de" (Gushee/Balensuela/Dean), for the life and
the list of works.

## How to read this book

The *Libellus* is a schoolbook: ten short treatises, each in
chapters. Its words are few and exact; the English keeps the Latin
term in parentheses at first use:

- *maxima, longa, brevis, semibrevis, minima* — the five
  note-values, greatest to least.
- *modus, tempus, prolatio* — the three levels of division: modus
  divides the longa, tempus the brevis, prolatio the semibrevis.
- *perfectus / imperfectus* — "perfect" (triple) and "imperfect"
  (duple) at each level.
- *imperfectio* — imperfection: a perfect note losing a third of
  its value to a neighboring smaller (or greater) note.
- *alteratio* — alteration: a note doubling its value to complete
  a perfection.
- *punctus perfectionis / divisionis* — the dot of perfection
  (which perfects) and the dot of division (which divides and
  imperfects).
- *ligatura* — ligature: two or more notes written joined; read by
  shape (*proprietas*).
- *sincopa* — syncopation: a note's value split across separated
  notes, reunited by counting.
- *pausa* — a rest.
- *color / talea* — the repeating pitch-pattern and rhythmic-pattern
  of a motet tenor.
- *diminutio* — diminution: the halving (or thirding) of a tenor's
  values on repeat.

Bracketed notes in the form [Editor's note] are the editor's, not
the author's. Notated examples in the Latin appear in the
transcription as bracketed tokens (e.g. `[MX,L,B,S,M on staff4]`);
each is rendered here as a plain-words description in brackets —
e.g. [a notated example: the five note-values in order] — and no
notation is reproduced or reconstructed.

## Note on the Latin text

The text opens with the compiler's address to the student
(*Quilibet ... affectans* — "whoever desires...") and the note
that what follows is "summarily compiled according to Master John
of Muris" — the attribution is the manuscripts', and modern
scholarship keeps it as an attribution. Chapter-numbering follows
Coussemaker. Two transmission scars are left visible: in the sixth
treatise the rule "if the first note has no dot it is said *sine
perfectione*" (as printed — likely for *sine proprietate*) is
translated as it stands, flagged in a note; and the tenth
treatise's chapter is headed *Capitulum decimum* ("tenth chapter")
where "first" is meant, translated as written and flagged.

## The Little Book of Measured Song, according to John of Muris

Whoever desires to be but moderately learned in the practical art
of measured song, let him diligently write out what follows,
summarily compiled according to Master John of Muris.

### First treatise

#### Chapter 1. On the five parts of prolation

There are five parts of prolation, namely: maxima, longa, brevis,
semibrevis, and minima, as appears here. [A notated example: the

A perfect maxima is worth three longae, whether in perfect or in
imperfect mode — if the longa be [perfect or imperfect]. In
perfect mode a perfect maxima is worth nine *tempora*, in
imperfect six, as here. [A notated example.]

An imperfect maxima is worth two longae, whether in perfect or in
imperfect mode. In perfect mode it is worth 6 *tempora*, in
imperfect mode four, as here. [A notated example.]

From these it is inferred that a maxima can be perfect while the
longae are imperfect, and conversely; a maxima can be imperfect
while the longae are perfect.

A longa in perfect mode is worth three breves, in imperfect mode
two, as here. [A notated example: longa, then three breves, longa,
then two breves.]

A brevis in perfect time is worth three semibreves, in imperfect
two, as here. [A notated example.]

A semibrevis in major prolation is worth three minimae, in minor
two, as the example shows. [A notated example.]

#### Chapter 2. On the twofold modus

Note that modus is twofold: perfect and imperfect. Perfect when
the longa is worth three breves; imperfect when worth two.

#### Chapter 3. On the twofold tempus

Note that tempus is twofold: perfect and imperfect. Perfect when
the brevis is worth three semibreves; imperfect when worth two.

#### Chapter 4. On the twofold prolatio

Note that prolatio is twofold: perfect — called major — and
imperfect — called minor. Perfect or major prolatio is when the
semibrevis is worth three minimae. Imperfect or minor prolatio is
when the semibrevis is worth two minimae. The examples are clear
from what was said above.

#### Chapter 5. On the imperfection of a note

Moreover note that every perfect note can be imperfected by the
abstraction of a third part of its value, and an imperfect [note]
perfected by the addition of half its value. For perfection
consists in the ternary number, imperfection in the binary.

#### Chapter 6. On the imperfection of the maxima

A perfect maxima, in whole and in parts, can be imperfected
doubly: as to the whole, and as to the parts. As to the whole,
doubly: from before and from behind. From before, when a single
longa precedes it. From behind, when a single longa follows it, or
four, or seven, or ten longae, or their value. Then the first longa
— or its value — imperfects the preceding maxima, unless hindered
by a dot, as here. [A notated example.]

As to the parts, also doubly: as to the near parts and as to the
remote parts. The near part of any whole is that into which the
whole is immediately divided — thus the near parts of maximae are
longae, of longae breves, of breves semibreves, etc. The remote
parts are the parts of the near parts; the more remote are the
parts of the parts of the near parts.

A maxima, then, as to the near parts can be imperfected doubly —
from before and from behind — by abstraction of a third part of
the value of each near part, or of one of them.

And likewise as to the remote parts, by abstraction of a third
part of the value of each remote part, or of one of them, etc. And
in its mode the same can be understood of longae, breves, and
semibreves; the examples will appear below.

And for what was said above and will be written below, note these
rules:

The first rule is that a longa before a longa in perfect mode is
always perfect; and a brevis before a brevis in perfect time is
always perfect.

And a semibrevis before a semibrevis in major prolation is also
always perfect, as here. [A notated example.]

The second rule is that whenever any note must be imperfected, a
greater or lesser note must immediately follow it, in form or in a
rest of greater or lesser form — for like before like cannot be
imperfected, as here. [A notated example.]

The third rule is that when after a longa of perfect mode there
follow two breves, or three — yet with no single preceding breve

following ones. For then the first breve imperfects the preceding

The fourth rule is that when between two longae there remains a

And the same of a semibrevis between breves and of a minima

The sixth rule is that every note that is imperfected is

Further note that when any note is imperfected by a near part or

Item, note that whatever is imperfected — as to the near parts —

A perfect brevis of minor prolation can be imperfected by one

[A notated example.]

And note that some singers — namely Guilielmus de Mascandio and

A perfect brevis of major prolation can be imperfected by a single

Or both following or both preceding, as here. [A notated example.]

Or by three minimae, or their values, as to the whole, as here. [A
notated example.]

And by four minimae, or their value, as to the whole and as to one
part, as here. [A notated example.]

And by five minimae, or their value, as to the whole and both
remaining parts, as here. [A notated example.]

And it cannot be more imperfected, for there remains to it only
the value of the imperfect brevis of minor prolation — which
brevis cannot be imperfected, as said.

And whatever is said of imperfection — of minimae with respect to
breves — let it be understood of semibreves with respect to longae,
and of breves with respect to maximae; and also whatever is said of
minimae with respect to semibreves, let it be understood of
semibreves with respect to breves, and of breves with respect to
longae, and of longae with respect to maximae.

Hence, as there is an imperfect brevis worth two semibreves worth
four minimae, so there is an imperfect longa worth two breves
worth four semibreves, and an imperfect maxima worth two imperfect
longae worth four breves.

As there is an imperfect brevis worth two semibreves worth six
minimae, so there is an imperfect longa worth two breves worth six
semibreves, and an imperfect maxima worth two longae worth six
breves.

And as there is a perfect brevis worth three semibreves worth six

semibreves, and a perfect maxima worth three longae worth six
breves.

And as there is a perfect brevis worth three semibreves worth nine
minimae, so there is a perfect longa worth three breves worth nine
semibreves, and a perfect maxima worth three longae worth nine
breves.

From the above said it is sufficiently had how maximae and longae
can be imperfected. Then let the examples that follow be seen.

And so of imperfect longae in mode and time, and of major
prolation, as here. [A notated example.]

Item, of longae imperfect in mode but perfect in time, as here. [A
notated example.]

Item, of longae perfect in mode but imperfect in time, as here. [A
notated example.]

Item, of longae perfect in mode and time, as here. [A notated
example.]

There follow examples of how a maxima can be imperfected. And
first of imperfect maximae whose longae are perfect, as here. [A
notated example.]

Item, of perfect maximae whose longae are imperfect, as here. [A
notated example.]

Item, of perfect maximae whose longae are also imperfect, as here.
[A notated example.]

And let the aforesaid on the imperfection of notes suffice.

[Editor's note -- in plain words: The heart of the book. A
"perfect" note is worth three of the next kind down; a neighbor
note can steal a third of it — that's imperfection — but never a
note's own twin (like before like stays perfect), and always the
nearest thief does the stealing. The *Libellus* works every
combination: maximae, longae, breves, semibreves, in every mix of
perfect and imperfect — and notes the singers (Guilielmus de
Mascandio's lot) who bend the rules by "change of quality."]

### Second treatise

#### Chapter 1. On alteration in music

Alteration in music is the doubling of a note's proper value
according to the note's form, of which such rules are given:

The first rule is that no note can be altered before its like, nor
before a greater than itself.

The second rule is that every note can be altered before the next
greater than itself: as a minima before a semibrevis, or before a
semibrevis rest; a brevis before a longa or before a longa rest; a
longa before a maxima — and not otherwise.

The third rule is that whenever between two longae of perfect mode
— or rests of longae — or between a dot and a longa, two breves are
found with no dot between, the second is altered, that is, worth
two breves. Likewise, when two semibreves are found between two
breves of perfect time, or between a dot and a brevis, or between
rests of breves, the second is altered, that is, worth two
semibreves. The same of two minimae between two semibreves of
major prolation: for whenever two remain with no dot between, the
second is altered.

And note that an altered note can be imperfected from before. An
example of all, as here. [A notated example.]

#### Chapter 2. On the twofold minima

Note that the minima is twofold — [and so] semibrevis, brevis, and
longa: namely *recta* and *altera*. *Recta* when it is put simply
for its value; *altera* when for double.

#### Chapter 3. On the cause of a note's alteration

Item, note that when any note is altered, this is for perfection's
sake — namely that the perfection be completed. Hence the brevis
is altered to perfect the modus, the semibrevis to perfect the
tempus, and the minima to perfect the prolatio. Let these on
alteration suffice.

[Editor's note -- in plain words: Alteration is the opposite of
imperfection: instead of losing a third, a note doubles — the
second of two dotless breves between longae counts as two. It
happens only to fill out a perfection, and only before the next
bigger value up.]

### Third treatise

#### Chapter 1. On the dot

The dot (*punctus*) is twofold: of perfection and of division. The
dot of perfection perfects the longa in either mode, the brevis in
either time, the semibrevis in either prolation. The dot of
division imperfects the longa by dividing breves, and imperfects
the brevis by dividing semibreves, and imperfects the semibrevis
by dividing minimae.

Hence it must be seen by what, or how, the dot of perfection is
known from the dot of division — since one has to perfect figures
and the other to imperfect, as said. When the dot is put after a
longa, it is said to be of perfection. But when immediately after a
minima, it is said to be of division.

And division is threefold: of modus, of tempus, and of prolatio.

Item, note that if a dot is put between two breves, it divides the
modus — unless those breves were of imperfect time, after or
before which is found a single semibrevis which by syncopation
would be reduced to the said brevis, dotted with a dot of
perfection. But if a dot is put between two semibreves, it is
assigned for division of tempus — unless the semibreves were of
minor prolation, after or before which is found a single minima
which by syncopation would be reduced to the said semibrevis
dotted with a dot of perfection. And let these on the dot suffice
for your study. An example of the aforesaid dots: [two notated
examples.]

[Editor's note -- in plain words: One little dot, two opposite
jobs: after a longa it perfects (fills to three); after a minima
it divides (cuts). Between two breves it divides the modus —
unless a syncopated note is secretly borrowing the value, in which
case the dot is perfecting after all.]

Item, modus, tempus, and prolatio are also distinguished by red
notes, or void black ones, and by full black ones, when they vary

void: the black longae are of perfect mode, and the red or void of

Item, if breves are found black, red, or void: the black are of
perfect time, and the red or void of imperfect, as here. [A notated
example.]

And black semibreves are of major prolation; red or void of minor,
as here. [A notated example.]

Item, by the colors written below, by rests and signs, perfect is
distinguished from imperfect, and also known: [a notated example.]

[Editor's note -- in plain words: How the singer knows what world
he's in: a square with three strokes (perfect mode) or two
(imperfect); a circle (perfect time) or semicircle (imperfect);
three dots in the sign (major prolation) or two (minor). And in
the song itself: black notes mean perfect, red or void notes mean
imperfect — at each level.]

### Sixth treatise

#### Chapter 1. On ligatures

Of ligatures, one is ascending, another descending. Ascending when
the second note is higher than the first — namely the first;
descending, conversely, when the first note is higher than the
second, having a stem descending on the left side — whether
figured in square or in oblique body — is said *with proprietas*,
and the first note is a brevis, as here. [A notated example.]

The second rule is that whenever the second note is higher than
the first, and the first has a stem descending on the right side,
it is said *with proprietas* and the first note is a longa, as
here. [A notated example.]

And if the first note has no dot, it is said *without perfection*
— and the first is a brevis, as here it is clear. [A notated
example. Editor's note: the print reads *sine perfectione*; the
sense and the parallel rules suggest *sine proprietate*, "without
proprietas" — translated as printed.]

The third rule is that whenever the first note, square or oblique,
is higher than the second, lacking a stem, it is said *without
proprietas*, and stands for a longa, as here. [A notated example.]

The fourth rule is that in every ligature the first note, having a
stem ascending on the left side, is said *with opposite
proprietas*, and makes the first two be semibreves, as here. [A
notated example.]

The fifth rule is that in every ligature the last note, descending
below the penultimate, placed in a square, is said *with
proprietas*, and is a longa, as here. [A notated example.]

And figured in oblique body, it is said *without proprietas*, and
is a brevis, as here. [A notated example.]

The sixth rule is that every note standing directly above the
penultimate, having a stem on the right side, is said *with
perfection*, and is a longa — and by some is called a longa by
opposite proprietas, as here. [A notated example.]

The seventh rule is that every last note standing to the side
above the penultimate, without a stem — whether square or oblique
— is said *without proprietas*, and is a brevis, as here. [A
notated example.]

The eighth rule is that in every ligature the middle notes are
breves — unless the first were with opposite proprietas, for then
the second remains with the first for one brevis, by the fourth
rule above, as here. [A notated example.]

And let these on ligatures suffice.

[Editor's note -- in plain words: How to read joined notes: the
stems and shapes are the code. Stem down on the left going up —
first note a breve; stem down on the right going up — first note
a longa; stem up on the left — the pair are semibreves; last note
dropped square below the one before — a longa; middles are breves
unless the code says otherwise. Eight rules, and the singer can
read anything.]

### Seventh treatise

#### Chapter 1. On syncopations

Syncopation (*sincopa*) is the division of any figure through
separated parts, which by counting perfections are reduced to one
another; and it can happen in modus, tempus, and prolatio. If it
happens in modus: either in perfect or in imperfect time. If in
perfect, then three separate breves are to be found — or the value
for a perfect longa — which by counting perfections are reduced to
one another. If in imperfect modus, then two separate breves are to
be found — or a longa dotted with a dot of perfection, whose third
part is reduced to some single separate breve, as here. [A notated
example.]

#### Chapter 2. On syncopation, if it happen in perfect or imperfect tempus

If syncopation happens in tempus: either in perfect or in
imperfect time. If in perfect time, then three separate semibreves
are to be found — or the value for a perfect brevis — which by
counting perfections are reduced to one another. If in imperfect
time: two, or one brevis dotted with a dot of perfection, whose
third part is reduced to some single separate semibrevis, as here.
[A notated example.]

Likewise if in prolatio: either in major or in minor. If in major,
then three separate minimae are to be found — or the value for a
semibrevis of major prolation — which by counting perfections are
reduced to one another. If in minor: two, or a semibrevis dotted
with a dot of perfection, whose third part is reduced to some
single separate minima, as here. [A notated example.]

Item, note that a note must never be reduced by syncopation beyond
a greater rest — as a minima beyond a semibrevis rest or greater,
nor a semibrevis beyond a brevis rest or greater, and so of the
others — though some say the contrary, but I know not how. And let
these suffice on syncopation.

[Editor's note -- in plain words: Syncopation is a note's value
scattered across the bar in separate pieces, reunited by counting
— at every level, modus, tempus, prolatio. One hard limit: never
let the scattered value leapfrog a bigger rest. ("Some say the
contrary, but I know not how" — the schoolmaster's dry dismissal.)]

### Eighth treatise

#### Chapter 1. On rests

A rest (*pausa*) is the measured omission or breathing of voices,
for as many *tempora* as measured by spaces — since a rest is
worth as many *tempora* as it occupies spaces. As, if it holds
three spaces, it is worth such *tempora*; if it holds two spaces,
two *tempora*; and if one, one tempus.

And note that a greater rest of three *tempora* must not be put —
for otherwise the rest would have to be redoubled according to the
demand of the modus, whence redoublings would be vain and
unmeasurable. Further note that a semibrevis rest must begin on a
line and descend to the middle of the space; and a minima rest,
conversely, must begin at a line and ascend to the middle of the
space. And semiminima rests are made like minima rests, but with a
semicircle. As here the examples of all appear: [a notated
example.]

Moreover note that a semibrevis rest or greater must not be put
except in a complete prolatio; nor a brevis rest or greater except
in a complete tempus; nor a longa rest except in a complete modus.

Item, note that rests cannot be imperfected, nor altered. And let
what is said on rests suffice.

[Editor's note -- in plain words: A rest's length is read off the
staff-spaces it covers — three spaces, three tempora. Semibreve
rests hang from a line, minim rests sit on one; and a rest belongs
only to a completed perfection of its own level. Rests can neither
be imperfected nor altered — they are what they are.]

### Ninth treatise

#### Chapter 1. On the diminution of motets

The diminution of motets is always made in the tenors, about which
note:

First, that for a maxima often in diminution a longa is put; for a
longa, a brevis; for a brevis, a semibrevis; for a semibrevis, a
minima.

Second, note that when the tenor is of imperfect mode — whether of
perfect or imperfect time — diminution is made only directly
through the half of the notes and rests.

Third, note that when the tenor is of perfect mode and imperfect
time, diminution is also made directly through the half — as: for
a longa worth three breves are put breves worth three semibreves.

Fourth, note that when the tenor is of perfect mode and perfect
time, diminution is made through the third and not through the
half. And let these on diminution suffice; the examples are clear
in motets.

[Editor's note -- in plain words: When a motet's tenor comes round
again diminished, every note steps down one value — maxima to
longa, longa to brevis, and so on. In imperfect mode (and in
perfect mode with imperfect time) halve everything; in perfect
mode with perfect time, third it.]

### Tenth treatise

#### Chapter 10. [So headed in the print; "first" is meant.] On color

Color in music is called the likeness of figures of one process,
repeated many times, set in the same song. For which note that some
singers put a difference between *color* and *talea*: for they call
it *color* when the same pitches are repeated; *talea* when like
figures are repeated — and so figures of diverse pitches are made.
This difference, though kept in many tenors of motets, is yet not
kept in the motets themselves; the examples are clear in motets.

And let the aforesaid suffice for the unlearned wishing to be
introduced into the practical art of measured song.

Amen.

[Editor's note -- in plain words: *Color* is the tune-pattern
coming round again; *talea* the rhythm-pattern coming round again.
Some masters keep the two words strict — same notes is color, same
rhythm-shapes is talea — but in the motets themselves nobody
bothers. End of lessons.]

## Glossary

- *altera / recta* — a note "doubled" (altered) or "straight" (at
  face value).
- *alteratio* — alteration: doubling a note's value to complete a
  perfection.
- *brevis* — the breve; worth 3 semibreves (perfect time) or 2
  (imperfect).
- *color* — the repeating pitch-pattern of a tenor; *talea* its
  rhythmic pattern.
- *diminutio* — diminution: shrinking a tenor's values on repeat.
- *imperfectio* — imperfection: a perfect note's loss of a third of
  its value.
- *ligatura* — joined notes, read by shape and stem (*proprietas*).
- *longa* — the long; worth 3 breves (perfect mode) or 2
  (imperfect).
- *maxima* — the greatest note-value; worth 3 (or 2) longae.
- *minima* — the least note-value of the five.
- *modus* — the division of the longa; *tempus* of the brevis;
  *prolatio* of the semibrevis.
- *mutatio qualitatis* — "change of quality": treating a note as
  if it belonged to the other prolation.
- *pausa* — a rest, measured by staff-spaces.
- *perfectus / imperfectus* — triple / duple at any level.
- *prolatio maior / minor* — major (triple) / minor (duple)
  prolation.
- *proprietas* — the "proper" reading of a ligature-shape; *cum
  / sine proprietate*, *opposita proprietate*.
- *punctus divisionis / perfectionis* — the dot of division /
  perfection.
- *semibrevis* — the semibreve; worth 3 minimae (major) or 2
  (minor).
- *sincopa* — syncopation: a value split and reunited by counting.
- *tempora* — counted time-units.
