DEDICATION

Who he was. Georg Wilhelm Richmann (1711–1753), a Baltic German physicist at the Imperial Academy of Sciences in St. Petersburg — the man who, while electricity was still a parlour marvel of sparks and crackles, set out to measure it: first with thread and graduated arc, his "index of electricity," and in heat with the mixing rule that still carries his name, Richmann's law.

His home and circumstances. Born at Pernau (now Pärnu, Estonia) on 11 July 1711, Old Style, into a family the Great Northern War had already scattered: his father, a German official in Swedish service, died of the plague before the boy was born, and his mother remarried. He was schooled at Reval, studied at Halle and Jena, and about 1735 came to St. Petersburg as tutor to the sons of Count Osterman. The Academy took him in as adjunct in 1739, extraordinary professor in 1741, and in 1745 ordinary professor of physics and director of its physical cabinet — a Baltic German of no fortune running the instrument room of an empire's academy.

His death. On 26 July 1753, Old Style (6 August, New Style), a thunderstorm came over St. Petersburg while the Academy was in session. Richmann hurried home with the engraver Sokolov to watch the index he had rigged to his insulated iron rod — dragging the sky's electricity indoors so he could read its strength off a thread. The lightning struck the rod; by the account Franklin later printed from the engraver's telling, a ball of fire left the apparatus for Richmann's forehead, leaving a red spot there, his left shoe blown open, his clothes singed, and a blast "like that of a small Cannon" that knocked the engraver down and tore the door off its hinges. He died at his instrument, aged forty-two. The strike is commonly described as the first documented case of ball lightning, and Richmann as the first person known to have lost his life conducting electrical experiments.

Why this work is vital — to prove his logic. Richmann's whole program, learned from Hamberger at Jena, was that physics advances by measurable relations between measurable quantities — "only if we have accurately determined the properties of bodies can we legitimately infer other truths with certainty," as he puts it in an earlier memoir. In heat he proved it by correcting his Academy predecessor Krafft's mixing formula with careful thermometry. In electricity he proved it by refusing to marvel and insisting on an instrument: the index, by which two masses could be compared, decrement timed, laws extracted from descent-curves. The memoir translated here is that program's summation — read it and you watch electricity stop being a wonder and start being a quantity, July storm-watching in its last pages and the author's death at the same apparatus ongoing in its margins.

His relation to this series. The Boneyard keeps works the world shelved before it finished listening. Richmann circulates in English almost entirely as an anecdote — the man the lightning killed — while the summation of the work that killed him has sat in Latin, unread in translation, since 1758. Here is his own voice: the measurer explaining his measure, cautioning his readers about damp air, sharp corners, and the fortitude the work demands. He asked, in this very memoir, whether we are yet bold enough to assert what the observations prove. Two hundred and seventy years on, the least a rescued-work series can do is let him finish the sentence.

— The Editor

Two of Richmann's words need a gloss before they can be followed. Where he writes of corpora originariae electricitatis — bodies "of original electricity" — he means the glass-and-resin class of bodies that take and hold charge in themselves, what we now call insulators; corpora derivativae electricitatis — bodies "of derivative electricity" — are the metals and moist bodies that receive charge only by communication, our conductors. THE DISSERTATION



I. The index, and what a good index must do



The occasion of the memoir [1758, p. 301]

What my zeal for inquiring into the laws of nature brought about was this: that eight years ago now I began, among my other Academic labours, to inquire into the phenomena of electricity. Since a perfect electrometer — an instrument, that is, by which the electric virtue is measured out — would beyond all doubt contribute much to perfecting the doctrine of electricity, I thought from the very beginning about a convenient way of determining the intensity of the electric virtue. I have not yet been so fortunate as to [obtain? devise?] a perfect electrometer, and I do not know whether others have. It has indeed been reported to me that a certain Le Roy in France, and d'Arcy, adjunct in Mechanics, used for measuring electricity a body floating on water, electrified by communication in a glass vessel, and attracted and raised by a neighbouring body of derivative electricity [a conductor], the space of its rising marking the degree of electricity; and that, to make the scale larger, they taught how to light up the pointed end of the electrified body with a light so that the shadow of its point might be cast upon a distant [screen], on which a scale for measuring the degree of electricity was drawn. Franklin, too — the first author of drawing electricity, by points, out of the atmosphere laden with lightning matter — [devised] a little machine made of very light little wheels of card paper, in the manner of a windmill, set in motion by electricity to show the motion of the electric matter. This machine, perhaps, by the speed of a kind of index's revolution, will be able to measure the force of electricity.



The index described [1758, p. 302]

Of the imperfection or perfection of these machines, however, I am not permitted to say anything, since I have not yet been allowed to use them, nor even to see accurate descriptions of them. I shall therefore use only that index which I described in volume XIV of the Commentarii and afterwards improved. I have also mentioned that judgment of the intensity of the electric virtue could be taken from the frequency of the strokes of a little bell [sounded] by electricity; such a little bell I have used for electrical phenomena [in earlier experiments]. And now, by the linen thread hanging beside a rule — of which I shall speak directly — I have resolved to explain the phenomena of electricity, both artificial and natural, so far as they can be defined by the index, and the phenomena of electricity themselves.

An index of electricity I call an instrument by whose help the electricity of any body, in differing circumstances, can be known in such a way that it appears whether it is greater than at another time, and which serves for defining the laws of electricity. It is probable that in one region a greater electricity can be excited than in another. Musschenbroek I could not imitate in certain electrical experiments, [nor can I report here] what has been done in Germany, France, and Italy; but of the great Musschenbroek [let this much be said: I am] persuaded that he was himself well accustomed to instituting experiments with care and circumspection, and also had the opportunity of procuring himself a suitable and [excellent] electrical machine. Nor is it to be doubted that the natural electricity spontaneously generated in the atmosphere is greater in one region than in another: it will therefore be altogether expedient to employ an instrument that betrays such a difference. Whether my index has some usefulness must be judged from what follows.



Its construction [1758, p. 303]

From volume XIV of the Commentarii it is clear that the index is nothing other than a linen thread AB, [Tab. VII, Fig. 3], of one and a half London feet and of half a medial grain['s weight], applied beside the broad vertical rule AC and hanging down from the top of the rule at its narrower side; under it is placed a wooden quadrant DEFGH of somewhat larger radius than the length of the thread, so that the thread's point of suspension is the centre of the quadrant and the plane of the quadrant is in the same plane as the broader section of the vertical rule. If such a rule is connected with an electrified mass, itself electrified it repels the electrified thread, which is also attracted by the wooden quadrant, not electrified. In this manner, the more the thread recedes from the rule, the greater the electricity excited must necessarily be.



Fat, wax, and the sticking thread [1758, pp. 303–304]

Care must also be taken that no grease or wax sticks to the metallic rule, for this has the effect that the thread, at the point where the wax sticks, is not repelled from the electrified rule but rather retained by it most tenaciously — the surest indication that the agitation of the electric matter is at this point not only impeded, but that the wax draws to itself another electricity, which attracts the thread in place of repelling it. But if a stronger electricity is excited, so that the repelling force prevails over the wax's attracting, at last the thread recedes from the rule. Indeed whatever force the rule receives, it likewise retains the thread if it is touched by it, so that the thread is separated from the glass vessel only by a more strongly excited electricity.



How the index compares two masses [1758, p. 304]

In the index described, the thread's recession from the rule — greater than before, the thread remaining of the same length and the electrified mass the same — indicates that a greater electricity has been excited than before; and equal indices, alike to one another, when they are of the same length and weight and are applied to the same electrified mass, tend to the same degree. And if an index applied to one electrified mass shows a certain degree, and a similar index, furnished with an equally long and equally heavy thread, joined to another mass separated from the first after electrification, shows the same degree, the electrified masses exhibit no spark when they touch one another, and each index after the contact shows the same degree still — a certain indication that the masses have been equally electrified. And last, when indices of equally long and equally heavy threads are applied to different electrified masses separated from one another after electrification, and the electricity of one mass is weakened, so that its thread shows a smaller degree than the thread of the other index, the masses exhibit an electric spark when they touch, and after the contact of the masses each index shows the same degree. If these things are considered, it will be plain that my instrument is [fit for] knowing the greater or lesser degree of electricity of any electrified mass.



The law of the active sphere [1758, p. 305]

It must nevertheless be observed that a thread of the same length and weight is always to be used. Otherwise the electric virtue is badly defined and no comparison can be instituted, and one thread could show one degree and another thread another, though the same electric virtue has been communicated to both masses. The reason of the thing is not hard to understand. The electric matter, agitated about a body, must necessarily surround the body to a certain distance, and in a smaller distance from the surface of the body be of greater efficacy; consequently, as the distance increases, by a certain law, and one not yet known to us, the virtue must decrease. If, therefore, a thread of the same length and weight is removed from the surface of one body to the same distance to which it is removed from another body, it will be a certain indication that the agitated electric matter at these equal distances is of equal efficacy, and at other, smaller, equal distances behaves equally alike, and that the electric virtue in both bodies is equal. But if the thread is removed from the surface of one electrified body to a greater distance than a thread of the same length and weight is removed from the surface of another electrified body, it will likewise be a certain indication that the efficacy of the agitated electric matter in the former body, at a smaller distance and at a distance equal to that to which the thread is removed from the surface of the other body, is greater — and hence its electric virtue must also be greater.



The two requirements of a good index [1758, pp. 305–306]

But the requirement of a good index seems to be nothing else than that it show the greater or lesser degree of the electricity excited; nothing more seems to be desired. Yet no one will deny that the use of the index is made broader if (1) the index, applied to an electrified body, does not bring it about that the excited electricity quickly perishes — so that the law of electricity's decrement, and the constitution of the agitated electric matter, can at last be detected; and (2) it can conveniently be applied to any mass for examining the degree of electricity, which is sometimes very necessary. Neither requirement is found in the index described, for the excited electricity is diminished on account of the wooden arc [and] the solid angles of the rule, on which, when a strong electricity is excited in darkness, a blue electric cone with a hiss out of the electric spark, arising of itself at the angles of the rule, clings. A remedy, however, can be found, as will appear from what follows.



II. The luminous cone, and the geometry of the agitated sphere



Why the light breaks out at points [1758, pp. 306–308]

It is a most familiar electric phenomenon, [occurring in the manner] I have described, and to one considering it attentively it seems to be a true continual ejaculation of diverging rays consisting of lucid cones, which arise from the solid bodies that are of derivative electricity [conductors] when they are electrified, especially when other bodies of infinite derivative electricity, not electrified, are brought near, so that the solid angles of the bodies brought near do not touch the solid angles of the electrified bodies; rather, from the acute solid angle of an electrified and of a non-electrified body set over against it, at a distance of five or six digits, a cone bursts forth with a hiss, smaller than the aforesaid [cone], and with a stronger electricity excited, the light is extinguished.

Here the thread of my discourse, which I must follow, [turns aside] a little, whither it will soon return; my purpose is to make what follows better understood. It is asked: why does the conical light arise rather at acute solid angles and prominent parts of bodies? Why not on a flat and curved surface? Why not at flat angles? Why, from an acute electrified body with a blunt infinite body set against it, does a great electric cone burst forth, but with the acute turned toward it, a much smaller one? Certainly what has been put forward by some savours of obscurity; nor do I have at hand so to explain this matter that nothing could be desired — content enough if, by defining the conditions under which the phenomenon arises, I give occasion to those who excel in genius to explain the thing properly.

It is indeed true that electrified bodies must be surrounded, to a certain distance, by a certain subtle agitated matter; I shall say nothing here of the phenomena which such matter, withdrawing itself into the very inmost recesses and little pores of bodies, may be able to effect within them; and it may be permissible to posit that the agitated electric matter occupies a space about the electrified body such that it extends from every point of the surface to an equal distance. But if bodies differ in figure and in magnitude, the volumes of agitated electric matter will differ greatly too, especially with what will be said toward the end. Will not the actions of electrified bodies, at the same degree of electricity, likewise differ on account of these differing volumes? From the following experiments I have believed this can be concluded with great appearance of truth.

I applied an iron mass of prismatic surface [of 10 square digits?] to an electrified mass of glass; a spark arose, the electrified mass being touched. Then in the same manner I applied an iron tube of equal mass with the prismatic mass, but whose surface was 130 square digits, to the same mass, whose electricity had been diminished by the application of the prism, and a greater crackling and spark arose, and also a greater change of the index. Also through a brass mass of two pounds' weight and of 700 [square?] digits of surface I drew a more crackling spark out of a less electrified mass than through a brass mass of two pounds and of 50 square digits of surface out of the same, more electrified, mass: certainly in the latter case the spark was not more efficacious than in the former.



The volumes at the corners [1758, pp. 308–310]

Here a wider field offers itself, in which lovers of stereometry may exercise themselves. In all right prisms, and consequently also in the infinitely-many-angled one, that is, the cylinder, and also in the sphere, the volume of excited quiet [agitated] electricity can easily be defined, the radius of activity being known. For in a right prism it is the sum (1) of a parallelepiped whose base is the whole surface of the prism and whose height is equal to the radius of activity of the electrified body; (2) of a prism whose base is a fourth part of the circle whose radius is equal to the radius of activity of the electrified body and whose height is double the perimeter of the prism's base; and (3) of a cylinder whose base is a circle whose radius is equal to the radius of activity of the electrified body and whose height is equal to the height of the prism; and (4) of a sphere whose radius is equal to the radius of activity of the electrified body. In the cube in particular, the volume of agitated electric matter is the sum (1) of a parallelepiped whose base is six times the square of the side and whose height is equal to the radius of activity of the electrified body; (2) [of a prism whose base is a fourth part of the circle whose radius is the radius of activity and whose height is] thrice the side of the cube; and last (3) of a sphere whose radius is equal to the radius of activity of the electrified cube. The last part of the sum, namely the sphere, is chiefly to be considered. At the solid angles of prisms are contained sectors of a sphere whose radius is equal to the radius of activity of the electrified body, cut by three planes; in these sectors the agitated electric matter is contained, and the sum of all such sectors in a prism is equal to a whole sphere of the same radius. In a right triangular prism, therefore, the volume of electric matter adhering to one solid angle is a sixth part of the aforesaid sphere; in a quadrangular prism, an eighth part of the same; in a quinquangular prism, a tenth part, and so on; consequently in an infinitely-many-angled prism, that is, a cylinder, an infinitely small part.

This also deserves consideration: the volume of agitated electric matter enclosed at a solid angle is the greater, the more acute the angle of the prism's base, enclosed by the right sides, is; and the aforesaid volume is the less, the more obtuse the aforesaid angle is, if the radius of activity is posited the same. If now it is posited that agitated electric matter is separated from bodies the more difficultly, the more points there are at which this matter touches the body, and the more readily, the fewer the points at which this happens — this adhesion, namely, if it is greater than it should be, hindering the agitated matter from being separated from bodies and running into other like matter outside the body, so that by collision light arises; but if it is smaller, not hindering the separation — the celebrated interval [between the cases] does not seem difficult to conceive: why light is generated if an un-electrified body approaches the angle of an electrified body. For the quiescent electric matter of the un-electrified body seems bound to recede into the place of the agitated matter expelled from the electrified body; hence friction arises, and light: especially if against the solid angle of the electrified body an un-electrified blunt body is set, in which the separation of matter is easy and the access of new matter more roomy.



Flat surfaces, cylinders, pyramids [1758, pp. 310–312]

On the flat surfaces of bodies, the agitated matter adheres to the body at more points, [and] is not easily separated: this perhaps is the reason why the aforesaid continuous light does not arise there. In [round] angles the electric matter which adheres to one point is so [small], in a cone of infinitely small height, as a sector of a circle whose radius is equal to the radius of activity of the electrified body; hence probably, by [reason of] this angle, [too little matter is supplied for] the aforesaid [light]. On the curved surface of a cylinder, which can be considered as a prism of infinitely many angles, the sides of the base [meet at] a flat angle, hence the same thing must happen that was said in the preceding. In the solid angles of prisms [of few sides] the electric matter at the angles of the base, [and] the volume of the electric matter, must be great; hence, by what has been said above, the aforesaid light [arises], which agrees with experience. In the acute solid angles of prisms the matter stands otherwise, so that there the aforesaid light is exhibited, as experience testifies. Because the solid angle of matter in cylinders is infinitely small, therefore one may not hope for the aforesaid continuous light in the solid angles of a cylinder, which is sufficiently known by experience. In pyramids, the more acute the solid angle of the pyramid, the more obtuse is the solid angle of the electric matter adhering to the pyramid's vertex, and the greater is the volume of agitated electric matter there; hence, as experience testifies, out of such angles — for example, from the point or tip of an electrified sword, and from the tips of the leaves of electrified plants — this light goes forth. When aqueous droplets rest upon a horizontal electrified parallelepiped, and an un-electrified body is brought near, the droplets rise and take on a conical figure with their vertices turned upward; by what has just been said, therefore, the aforesaid light ought to be exhibited at the vertices, which experience confirms.

I would not, however, say that at no degree of excited electricity does the aforesaid light arise at the solid angles of bodies and their projecting parts. With the electricity I could produce, I was allowed to exhibit the conical light only in these [corners], not in others. But my electricity, which I could produce at St. Petersburg, never in winter raised the thread of the described index beyond the 50th degree, and in summer scarcely to the 40th degree. Perhaps, if the thread could be raised much higher, the light would also be exhibited on other parts of bodies and the whole body would be surrounded by light — which we read was observed by the celebrated Bose in [the St. Petersburg?] Commentarii, where indeed he was pleased to dignify this phenomenon with the name of beatification. Nor do I venture to say that from the most acute solid angle the greatest conical light arises, because against it is set the most obtuse solid angle containing the agitated electric matter, and because the volume of this matter is nearly equal to half a sphere whose radius is equal to the radius of activity of the electrified body.

For the solid angle of a quadrangular prism seemed to me to have afforded a greater light than the sharpest foot of a compass. Perhaps from a greater solid angle the electric matter, or that which in part constitutes the electric matter, also bursts forth more largely and repairs the electricity's decrement more in this place than the matter ejaculated from a smaller solid angle; and perhaps there is here a certain composition of ratios out of the ratio of the magnitudes of the solid angles of the electrified body and of the solid angles which contain the agitated electric matter, where it is plain from arithmetical principles that the product arises greatest if the factors are equal, the sum remaining the same — which obtains in the said case.

The consideration of the electric conical light has perhaps detained us justly longer from pursuing our purpose, to which we now gird ourselves again.



III. Why electricity decays, and the experimenter's cautions



The timed descent [1758, pp. 312–313]

I said that with the aforesaid light an extraordinary decrement of electricity is conjoined, which I was taught by experience: I noted the time in which the thread of an electrified index descended through a certain arc; then, the end of the rule being received into a glass vessel and the interval between the little arc and the rule filled with iron filings or the scrapings of another metal, and the electricity excited anew so that the thread showed the same degree which [it had] at the beginning in the first case, I observed the time in which the electricity decreased through the same arc. For in the last case the time was always much greater than in the first — a certain indication that in the former case the electricity had decreased faster than in the other. In the former case [the end of the rule was bare; in the latter, plunged in the filings].

Let me, however, here note that the use of iron filings, or of scrapings of another metal, is not necessary. For if the rule touches the bottom of the glass vessel, the same thing happens; indeed, even without contact with the bottom, the decrement of electricity is diminished. The interior surface of the little vessel can also be coated with gold, and the rule let down until it touches the gold. From what has been said it is at the same time readily understood in what way the index is to be improved so that the electricity does not quickly decrease.



Cautions (1): sharp angles near the mass [1758, p. 313]

Observation of the decrement of electricity, on account of the solid angle of an electrified body near an un-electrified body, gives occasion to various cautions to be applied in electrical experiments, to which in the use of the index careful attention must be paid if we wish to compare phenomena.

1) It must be carefully seen to that in the electrified mass no solid angles, especially acute ones, occur that exhibit conical lights in darkness: hence I stopped up the ends of the metallic threads ejaculating this light with wax, by which I brought it about that the light [ceased] at the glowing ends. In an iron chain [in use] here and there this light appeared in the darkness, and when I examined the chain, at those places I found projecting sharp lamellae; these being filed off, the light ceased and the decrement of electricity was lessened. When I imposed compasses on the mass to be electrified so that the feet stuck out on both sides, the electricity could scarcely be so excited that the thread of the index was raised to the fortieth degree with a motion retarded in a certain law, and from degree 40 to 35, the rubbing of the electrifying glass having ceased, it descended in 86 seconds; but the compass being removed and the electricity excited anew, the thread rose readily, with a motion retarded in a certain law, to the 59th degree [1758, p. 314], and from degree 40 to 39 it [took] a hundred seconds in descending. How much therefore electricity decreases quickly on account of [points], every one can easily see.



Cautions (2): blunt bodies, and the cork test [1758, pp. 314–315]

2) [Electricity decreases] also on account of bodies of derivative electricity [conductors], [even] blunt ones, standing near [the mass?]: for an electrified mass, even at the fortieth degree, begins at once and swiftly to descend. But if the feet of the compass, or any other blunt body, is directed toward the electrified mass, a slower descent of the thread will be observed, provided that the blunt body is not moved, than [when] an acute one had been brought near. The same thing is confirmed by a curious spectacle, if a piece of cork be hung by a silk thread near the electrified mass. It is known that it ought [first] to be attracted, the attraction preceding. This being done, let it be held at a distance of five or six digits from the cork [end?], the foot of the compasses, and the cork will at once approach the electrified mass — an evident indication that it has suffered a loss of electricity — and will adhere to the electrified mass similarly [i.e. as before]; then put a finger between the foot of the compasses and the piece of cork, and let the finger be brought near to the cork, and the cork will approach the finger, namely the electrified body toward the un-electrified. This can be done repeatedly.



Cautions (3): the medium must be dry [1758, pp. 315–317]

4) [OCR numbering as printed: the third caution] Experiments with electricity must be instituted in the driest possible medium: that moist air hinders electricity has been noted by almost everyone, nor do I doubt that the genuine reason why it happens has been produced, although it has not come to my knowledge by whom. If the medium is moist, the moistures are attracted by the electrified mass, and are not separated from it readily nor repelled, on account of cohesion. However, so long as the moistures adhere to the electrified mass, no decrement of electricity arises, unless the aqueous droplets that have flowed in, other blunt infinite bodies being brought near, take on a conical figure and emit lucid cones. That decrement rather arises on account of the supports made damp, which, electrified by communication, attract moistures to the same [degree], as electrified bodies of derivative electricity [conductors] do. This damping happens the more readily, the more efficaciously the electricity is excited; and this is the reason why electricity in a cold room with many spectators present is at the beginning strong enough, but soon decreases and sometimes ceases. For the moistures emitted by the spectators present through insensible transpiration are attracted by the supports of the electrified bodies, which are changed into bodies of derivative electricity — infinite [conductors].

This was confirmed excellently by an experiment, on the occasion of which [I examined diverse sorts of glass dust?], whether [the supports] should owe their being electrified to [this cause...]: I saw bodies supported on them electrified [accordingly...], and lest perhaps adhering moistures should be to blame, I heated [the powders] strongly in a furnace; a coarser powder [offered more resistance?]... [than] heat being to blame... [the relation of the powders, coarse and fine, in diverse places, could not here be read from the machine text and is carried as a described experiment: supports of powdered glass, heated to exclude damp, still conducted according to their fineness; from which he concludes:] ...on both sides a notable [electricity] was excited, which could not have happened if the cooled powder had been a body of derivative electricity. The later, therefore, electricity of the Musschenbroek jar [Leyden jar] decreases — other things being equal — the drier the medium ought to be in which it is enclosed.



Cautions (4): where the jar stands [1758, pp. 317–318]

5) Electricity is notably diminished if the rule or metal wire of the Musschenbroek jar is applied to an electrified mass while the jar is placed on bodies of original electricity [insulators]; it is preserved, however, if it is placed upon bodies of derivative electricity, very capacious and infinite [large conductors]: for the thread of the index descends much faster in the former case than in the latter. But if the jar, its bottom clothed in a metal vessel, is placed on a dry glass support, and the rule of the jar, touching the electrified mass, lets the degree of electricity be observed, and then the vessel clothing the jar's bottom is touched, the electricity at once increases by many degrees. Whether here, out of infinite bodies of derivative electricity, new electric matter approaches through the jar's walls affected by electricity, and compensates the decrement of the electric matter of the electrified body; or the thickness of the glass impedes the approach of this matter; or rather the motion produced in the aforesaid jar, or rather kept in its least particles, is preserved if the jar is placed on metal and more quickly retarded if it is placed on glass, or another body of original electricity — I dare not say.

In what follows I shall call the electricity produced by applying the Musschenbroek jar [a compound electricity], and without it, a simple electricity. Under simple electricity it perpetually obtains that the body to be electrified must be supported on a body of original electricity, if the electricity is to stand for a notable time without motion, and the electrifying glass kept [turning?]. An infinite, or unsupported, body is electrified only for the briefest moment of time. For if a mass has been very strongly electrified, and the neck, for example, of a man not supported on bodies of original electricity, who is striking the electrified mass with an iron rod, is lightly touched by another body of derivative electricity, infinite [a large conductor], the man himself, touching a certain [something], feels [the effect], and between finger and neck a light can be seen in darkness — a certain enough indication that the electricity has been communicated with the body that struck the electrified mass. In compound electricity, if a metal joined with the armed bottom of the jar by a metal wire is struck by electric sparks, and that metal or wire is touched, tremor and concussion are felt, although the metal is a body of infinite connection with other bodies of derivative electricity; [and] a spark can be drawn from the electrified body, if it is touched [through?] it, [which makes] a spark, if it is touched...



The four causes, summed [1758, p. 318]

Electricity decreases, therefore, on account of—

1. the solid angles of the electrified mass; 2. other, infinite bodies of derivative electricity nearby, especially acute ones; 3. the moistures adhering to the supports [made damp]; 4. the Musschenbroek jar placed on bodies of original electricity, its rule being applied to the electrified mass.

It may here be asked whether diversity in the decrement of electricity may not also arise from inequality of the surface, of the masses, of the densities, and of the constitutive parts of electrified bodies. To these questions, however, for want of experiments, it is not yet permitted to answer.



Timing the descent into a law [1758, p. 319]

But with the electricity decreasing, the volume of agitated electric matter is diminished while the index's thread descends, until the body is surrounded by no more agitated electric matter; and the time of the thread's descent through different spaces will perhaps at length define the law of decrement of electricity — if only the curve which the thread describes, and from it the ratio of the efficacy of the electric matter at differing distances from the body's surface, could be defined. Nor, this way being known, is it difficult to understand how the index may be changed into a perfect electrometer.



The portable index [1758, pp. 319–321]

I said that it makes for the perfection of the index that the electricity, the index being applied to the body, be preserved as much as possible; how, with the index described, [this] may be obtained, [has appeared]. Nor do I think the other requirement of the index is to be passed over in silence: namely, that it may conveniently be taken away from electrified bodies and applied to any other electrified bodies for knowing the degree of electricity. The index described lacks this requirement, as is readily plain, though not so that no remedy can be brought to it. In a few words I shall now set out in what way this inconvenience may be met.

Let a small glass bottle ABC, of narrower and cylindrical neck and of thin walls, be filled up to half its height with iron filings, scrapings of any metal, or little leaden balls; then let a metal rod KN be put down into the bottle until it touches the bottom, so that it is surrounded by the iron filings and keeps a vertical position, and be well stopped in the bottle's neck with cork, so that it cannot wobble. ([1758, p. 320]) To the end of the part of the rod projecting from the bottle, which is twelve digits [long], let a very fine thread KM be tied, of a certain weight — for example of half a medicinal grain — which as good as touches the neck of the little bottle. Let the bottle be put into a thin metal vessel FGBC closely touching it everywhere. The height of the metal vessel is not to exceed half the bottle's height. With the metal vessel let a handle GCDE be joined, holding up a wooden circular arc HEI, divided into 170 [parts], so placed that (1) the centre of the arc coincides with the point of the rod where the thread is tied; (2) the rod continued touches the arc's uppermost end; and (3) the lower end of the linen thread stands one line away from the arc. That the metal rod may be applied to any electrified bodies whatsoever, let a little curved metal plate LK be fixed to its end. If this instrument, grasped by the handle, is applied to an electrified mass so that the curved little plate touches the mass, the rod will be electrified; and because of the rod's small mass, the electricity of the greater electrified mass will scarcely suffer a decrement of electricity from this; the thread will be repelled from the rod, and the degree of the circular arc toward which it is directed will mark the degree of electricity. Let the instrument be removed and the rod projecting from the bottle touched with a finger: there will be scarcely any residue of electricity left; let it be applied to another mass to be examined, which has a greater or lesser electricity than the former mass had, and the thread will regard a greater or lesser degree with respect to the rod. I have not yet been allowed to use this index; nevertheless I do not doubt that it could be conveniently employed on many occasions in electrical experiments, especially since the Musschenbroek jar preserves the electricity for several hours. Therefore whatever phenomena could be defined, have been defined with the former index.



IV. The second part: experiments with the index



Phenomenon 1 — the rubber and the receiver must stand apart [1758, pp. 321–322]

I hasten now to the second part of the dissertation, in which I have resolved to recount the principal phenomena which I have in some part defined with the index described. The index described taught me—

1) The body exciting electricity by rubbing must be different from, and separated from, the body receiving the electricity, if a notable electricity is to be produced. For if, on one side of the electrifying glass, a man standing on resin touches the body receiving the electricity on the other side of the glass, [whether] the man turning the machine stands on resin or not, scarcely an electricity of a few [degrees] can be produced — a simple [electricity] is excited — and if a compound electricity [is attempted?], no electricity at all is produced by rubbing the electrifying glass on the mass. Otherwise the matter stands when the body receiving the electricity is separated from that which excites the electricity, and is not touched by it. For then the index's thread is quickly raised and a vehement spark arises if the bodies touch one another; nor by such a touch does all the electricity cease, but only [so much falls away that] an electricity of several degrees is observed remaining, which gradually decreases. This happens whether the machine and the man turning the wheel stand on resin — in which case, however, the electricity is excited with more difficulty — or not. The vehemence of the spark here is not increased with the difference of the electricities, as at other times, but rather seems to be greater if the sum of the electricities is greater. Do therefore in this case the particles of electric matter adhering to the body exciting electricity assume a motion directly contrary and opposite to that which the particles of electric matter of the receiving body hold [?]. By the mutual meeting of the particles a vehement and crackling, efficacious spark is [produced]. The same is [shown by the experiment of the cylindrical glass tube?] [Tab. VII, Fig. 2? — the machine text here is disordered by the plate: twin very dry glass cylinders are concerned, the mercury descending through the one and falling into the other, and the thread hung from the rule repelled, with crackling sparks arising; but if the thing is considered more closely, the droplets of quicksilver striking the glass walls by no means touch the quicksilver standing in the funnel [or tube's] bottom; hence at the moment of collision the particles of quicksilver exciting electricity are distant from the particles of quicksilver receiving electricity...] At the same time it is understood [how], by the fall [of the mercury], the particles of derivative electricity are supplied to the particles of original electricity, these are electrified, and the electricity can be communicated from the same to others of derivative electricity attracted to them. For it is by no means to be doubted that corpuscles of original and derivative electricity are sometimes present in the atmosphere in great abundance, and so that electricity can be produced, since in fact in it electricity is communicated with supported masses, as we shall see from what follows.



Phenomenon 2 — Franklin's reading, and the "negative" electricity [1758, pp. 323–325]

2) I found: if someone standing on resin holds in his hand a Musschenbroek jar furnished with an index, its lower part clothed in a metal vessel, and then applies the other [rule — the jar's rod] to [the electrified mass], a vehement spark arises and [the effect propagates?] through the arm; [yet] almost no electricity could afterwards be observed [remaining] in the man. For the index hangs almost idle [from his rule]. Franklin — the discoverer of natural electricity — detected this phenomenon without using an index, and asserted the total extinction of the electricity after the concussion; which I did not observe, but perpetually found a sensible electricity in the rule, the hand being removed from the rule, the jar being placed on bodies of derivative electricity. Franklin here posits a negative electricity and a positive: [saying] the outer surface of the jar enjoys a negative electricity and the inner a positive, and if such contrary virtues are equal and run together, the electricity will be made equal to nothing. Yet the concept of a negative electricity seems to me most difficult, unless it is posited, as in mechanics, that a motion of electric matter made in a contrary direction is a negative motion. Are therefore, on both sides of the jar standing on resin and exercising electric vibrations, the particles of the subtle matter agitated with a contrary motion? And hence, when they touch one another, do they collide with equal speeds and directly opposite directions, and so [make] the vehement [concussion]...?

If indeed on both sides of the Musschenbroek jar ... [the machine text is here disordered by the plate (Tab. VIII, Fig. 5?) and is carried as description: he electrifies a jar from the machine, touches its rule with another rod, and finds the electricity restored in the last mass after an instant's interruption, as the thread shows; and this can be repeated three or even ten times in succession, without motion of the machine and without total extinction of the electricity —]. Is therefore an infinite motion impressed upon the particles of the glass, or upon the particles of the electric matter adhering to them, by one part of the jar's surface, so that it happens only from the other part of the surface? Or is it really granted to a certain matter to pass from an infinite body into a finite body through the jar's walls, affected by electricity?



Phenomenon 3 [numbered 4 in the original] — great masses hold their charge longer [1758, p. 325]

4) I observed that the electricity of a greater mass lasts longer than that of a smaller mass, if they are joined to the electrifying glass with the same metal thread: for at different times the thread returns to the rule. These times, however, have been observed in no ratio of the masses. Then I learned that a certain mass can be applied more often to a greater electrified mass before the whole electricity is extinguished, than to a smaller mass electrified to the same degree; yet the sum of the masses which take away the electricity is not much [greater] than the sum of the masses which take away the electricity of the smaller mass; and these sums are by no means in the ratio of the electrified masses. Is it, because in a greater [mass] the volume of agitated electric matter is greater, that the decrement of electricity is slower? This I purpose to inquire into at last, when bodies of great surface can be employed, whose volumes of agitated electric matter can easily be defined...
