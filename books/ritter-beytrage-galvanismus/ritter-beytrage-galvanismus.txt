DEDICATION

Who he was. Johann Wilhelm Ritter (1776–1810), German chemist, physicist, and philosopher of the Romantic age — the apothecary's apprentice from Silesia who taught himself physics and, before he was twenty-five, had stated the principle of the voltaic pile, decomposed water on his own, and found the invisible rays beyond the violet.

His home and circumstances. Born 16 December 1776 at Samitz near Haynau in Silesia; apprenticed to an apothecary at Liegnitz at fourteen because mathematics alone would not feed him; from 1796 at Jena, studying medicine as his road into physics; no chair, no regular income, ever. In 1804 the Bavarian Academy called him to Munich as an ordinary member of its physical class — the uniform in his portrait — and Munich was his home for the six years he had left.

His death. He married in June 1804 and had four children he could not provide for. He died at Munich on 23 January 1810, aged thirty-three, poor. The old lexicon says a wasting sickness took him; that his years of passing the pile's current through his own eyes, ears, tongue, and hands ruined his health is often repeated and nowhere proven, and this edition does not print it as fact.

Why this work is vital — to prove his logic. In the Beweis of 1798 and these Beyträge of 1800, Ritter argues that galvanism is no trick of two metals but a chemical action — oxidation and reduction locked together — and that the same action accompanies the life process in the animal kingdom. He builds the case the way Galvani taught the century to build cases: vary one thing, watch what survives. The pile, electrolysis, electroplating, and the accumulator all stand downstream of this little book's logic.

His relation to this series. The Boneyard keeps works the world shelved before it finished listening. Ritter's galvanism treatises waited in Fraktur while his Fragmente and Physics as Art got their modern English edition (Holland, 2010); the treatises themselves had, after the research recorded in the notes, no English translation at all. Here is the young physicist's own proof, carried across at last — because he proved his logic, and then the century credited the battery to other names.

— The Editor

THE PROOF



From the Beweis (Weimar, 1798)



First Division — The phenomena occurring in galvanism, and the development of the laws those phenomena follow



§1. A common galvanic experiment [1798, p. 1]

If to one or more muscles of a recently killed animal — for example, to a lower extremity of a frog separated from the trunk, Fig. 1, or to a moist body touching it, as in Fig. 2, e.g. a wet sponge, water, etc. — one applies a piece of silver (5, Fig. 1 or 2), and further applies to the nerve belonging to this extremity, still undisturbed and organically united with it (c, Fig. 1), or else only, as before, to a moist body in contact with it (h, Fig. 2), such as water, moist muscle-flesh, etc., somewhere a piece of zinc (d, Fig. 1, or k, Fig. 2), and now brings the ends of these metal pieces opposite the points of contact into immediate contact with one another, e.g. at l in Fig. 1 [or 2], then at this instant violent contractions will arise in all the muscles into which the branches of the nerve of this extremity — the nerve that was a member of the chain — spread. If, after the contact, one presses the metal pieces firmly together, so that a commotion of the apparatus, made possible by the convulsion of the frog's leg, cannot disturb their rest, one will find that the contractions do not repeat: the organs remain quiet, however long the chain still stays closed. Only when the mutual contact of the metals is lifted will they — if the irritability of the organ still stands at a fairly high degree — fall a second time into convulsion, but weaker than at the closing of the chain. If one changes the order of the metal pieces so that silver comes into connection with the nerve, zinc with the muscle — be it immediately or mediately through moist bodies — and proceeds as before, one will notice the weaker contraction, or at a lower degree of excitability of the organ none at all, at the closing, and the stronger or in general a strong one at the opening of the chain.



§2. Conductors and non-conductors of galvanism [1798, p. 4]

In the preceding experiment, which was a galvanic one, we saw a galvanic chain, and indeed an effective one. Every body, solid or fluid, which interrupts the effectiveness of this or a similar chain when it steps in between any two of its members — thus increasing that chain by one member through itself — is called an ineffective, a disturbing member of the galvanic chain, an isolator [insulator] of galvanism. Examples are fatty oil, [resin?], glass, air, etc. Every body, on the other hand, which, when it steps in between two members of the effective chain, is not wont [utterly] to alter the degree of its effectiveness, except according to circumstances now to heighten it, now to lower it, but only under a few determinate cases — through its relative position to the remaining members of the chain (a circumstance that will be explained more closely below) — wholly to destroy it, wholly to abolish the effectiveness of the chain (which therefore in no case shows itself as an isolator, and seems to be one only in few cases), is called an effective, a conducting member of the galvanic chain, a conductor of galvanism.



§3. Closer determination of the conductors and non-conductors of galvanism [1798, p. 5]

The bodies which have hitherto been found conducting in galvanic chains are also conductors of electricity. Even all bodies hitherto known as isolators of electricity, more or less, are so for galvanism too; only four exceptions have so far been found, namely dry bones, the airless [evacuated] space, the flame of light, and hot glass — all of which conduct electricity fairly well, but do not conduct in the galvanic chain.



§4. The manifold ways in which excitable organs can successfully be subjected to galvanism [1798, p. 6]

Before we go further it is necessary to recall that, in order to produce contraction in excitable muscles by galvanic stimulation, it is not exactly necessary that the muscle to be contracted should itself be in the chain. It is sufficient for this that a piece of nerve (a, Fig. 3), surrounded by substances that do not particularly conduct galvanism, e.g. air etc., stands in connection with the nerve (b) undisturbed and organically united with the muscle (c) that is likewise undisturbed and organically united [with it] and is to be contracted — that this piece of nerve, more or less, itself forms a member of the chain. So, conversely: in order to move a muscle (a, Fig. 4) by the galvanic stimulus, it is also enough if one arms it alone at two different places with the effective metals, etc. — for so one also calls the act by which one brings a galvanic chain-member into connection, mediate or immediate, with an animal part, be it nerve or muscle [— provided the connection with the nerve-fibre bound up with the muscle remains]. The contractions that then follow seem, however, to occur only when this muscle still contains a significant [nerve-branch]; for hitherto no muscle could be brought to contraction by the galvanic stimulus after all significant nerve-branches had first been removed from it as far as possible. The effective stimulation of a muscle when it alone is in the chain may thus well be traced back to a mediate stimulation of its nerve, as has indeed hitherto been assumed and as other experiments make very probable. Here we may also make the remark that, accordingly, it cannot be decided by simple galvanic experiments whether irritability of the nerve can exist without muscle-irritability, or the latter without the former, since for the excitable muscle-fibre there is no other immediate reagent than the excitable nerve-fibre organically bound up with it, and for the latter no other than the still excitable muscle-fibre organically bound up with it.



§5. First condition for the production of effective galvanic chains [1798, p. 7]

We come back to §3. That the animal organs themselves must be conductors of galvanism is self-evident; otherwise we should know nothing of any galvanic experiment. The first condition for the production of an effective galvanic chain is mutual contact of heterogeneous conductors with one another; the proof of this will be seen in [the following sections] where it could first properly be given. Now, that which this mutual contact of heterogeneous conductors contributes to the effectiveness of the chain — that which in any case can be traced back to an activity — was given the name Action. If now, by heterogeneity of the chain-members coming into contact — for with homogeneous ones there is no ground for it — action is determined, and we further see that with the difference of the qualitative difference of those bodies their action too is different in degree, but there must be just as many relative heterogeneities in the galvanic chain as different combinations of bodies differing in chemical mixture, form, temperature, etc. are in general possible with one another, then it is intelligible that those actions can have very many degrees; i.e. an action can now be greater, now smaller than another, and there will be as many possible different magnitudes of it as there are specific heterogeneities of bodies. [The degrees of bodies' action] we can [rarely] determine at once, since our usual [criteria] are grounded only on external relations, whereas here [they are determined] more through inner quality, which we can in the fewest cases perceive immediately and strikingly. Who would believe, for example, that of two pieces of silver completely alike in all external circumstances and inner content, of which one has just been in contact with a piece of tin, zinc, etc., the inner quality [is altered so] that it is indeed no longer to be regarded as wholly homogeneous with the piece of silver not touched by zinc — that they are now really two heterogeneous bodies? And yet this is so, as the so-called galvanising of metals discovered by Messrs [—] and von Humboldt shows.



§6. The usual division of the conductors of galvanism into excitators and conductors; all conductors are both at once [1798, p. 9]

From the foregoing it is clear that in the galvanic chain, everywhere where two heterogeneous substances come into contact, an action [arises], but that this action, according to the nature of those bodies, [may be very different]. Hitherto two classes of substances effective in galvanic experiments were everywhere distinguished. Those substances of galvanism which, when they come mutually into contact in the galvanic chain, particularly contribute to the production of an effective chain were commonly called excitators; [others, which,] in whatever [conductor] they might come into contact [with], nevertheless seemed to occasion little [action], were generally called conductors. To the first class, until not very long ago, were counted only all metals, several ores that contain the "unregulated" [reguline] metal in a reguline or nearly reguline state, plant charcoal, graphite, several carbon-containing minerals, and the [chalk] naturally occurring under various forms. Herr Volta recently — earlier indeed, but especially in Gren's Neues Journal der Physik, vol. [—], no. [—], pp. 115–136 — drew attention to the fact that there are yet many more which, when they enter into combination with these, produce considerable action, but which, unlike those, are not in the solid but in the fluid state. Hitherto one was accustomed only to the former, and it is really very striking that there is no solid conductor of galvanism which does not also, in the meaning of the word taken here, deserve the name of a strong excitator, while there are several fluids which, in whatever combination they may be, show little excitatory power. This may also have been the cause why the thought of [counting] more fluids in this respect did not arise earlier — that, led partly by chance, partly by Herr Volta's observations, I [have been led to] experiments [of my own, and found the law confirmed] that, on the contact of certain heterogeneous conductors in the galvanic chain under determinate circumstances, an action arises — a law that holds not for metals (etc.) alone, as one might have been tempted to believe, but for all different conductors. Meanwhile there especially distinguished themselves, in the property of producing considerable action under certain circumstances, in Volta's experiments and in mine: all caustic-alkali solutions, whether caustic or carbonated; the solutions of the [earths soluble in water]; all acids, oxygenated muriatic acid not excepted; all saline combinations arising from these with the caustic alkalis, earths, and metallic calces, in proportion as they are soluble in water; all sulphur-livers [hepars]; the combination of the caustic alkalis with [sulphur] and metallic calces, in so far as water dissolves it, e.g. arsenic-liver; the soaps; the soluble bodies in the same; all plant extracts, opium and musk extract; gum solutions; plant mucilages; all mucous and gelatinous fluids; blood; milk; the other animal parts and fluids, e.g. saliva; and so forth. All these, though in different degrees, are mostly eminently fitted to produce a considerable action in the chain under suitable contact with the [other members]. [Set against them stand, far weaker, the solutions of] alcohol, and the fluids containing it; the solution of [— in it — OCR uncertain].

That such action in every case arises [only] under the determinate condition of the arrangement of the experiment — with an apparatus such as Herr Volta describes [in the place cited] — but under every other form of the same: [the fluids filled] e.g. into bent glass tubes, the thicker ones brought in layers upon glass plates, etc., one uses them completely as one uses the metals and the [solid conductors].



§7. Remarks on the artificial raising and lowering of irritability [1798, p. 12]

Many a reader will perhaps here hit upon the question whether, in the so remarkable experiments of Herr von Humboldt and others on the so-called raising and lowering of nerve-irritability by caustic-alkali solutions, common and oxygenated muriatic acid, opium and musk extract, etc., the eminent excitatory capacity of these bodies — which the preceding section taught us — may not have had some share in the phenomena obtained. And this is indeed really [likely]. Here, therefore, a fitting place to add a somewhat longer [account] of some examples, in order to call the attention of those who have occupied themselves less with our subject to this circumstance, [while] the better informed [I ask] to follow the following [experiments with patience].

If the vitality of a frog's leg and its nerves, Fig. 5, has just, or for some time, sunk so far that, when one applies a metal piece, e.g. zinc, as armature both to the nerve (a) and to the muscle (b), and now closes the chain at e, no further contraction follows, then dip the nerve (a) into any solution of a caustic alkali, into opium-extract solution, into common or oxygenated liquid muriatic acid, into nitric acid, etc., and now lay this nerve upon a piece of zinc (d), while with the other [zinc] one touches the leg (b) and thereupon the first zinc (d) at e: with the first-named bodies one will have strong contraction at the closing of the chain, weak or none at its opening; with the latter, on the contrary, weak or none at the closing, stronger at the opening. Under such circumstances it was then believed that the irritability of the organ had been raised (compare e.g. von Humboldt's Versuche über die gereizte Muskel- und Nervenfaser, vol. §8. Ineffective galvanic chains without the presence of non-conductors, and their conversion into effective ones by alteration of the relative sequence of the conductors upon one another [1798, p. 21]

We heard in [§5] that heterogeneity of two [conductors] that touch one another immediately in the galvanic chain is the first condition for the possibility of an action in general. We have enumerated the bodies which, when in those chains under otherwise suitable circumstances they mutually touch, mostly especially distinguish themselves by the production of an action of a high degree. But there are cases of galvanic chains where that condition is fulfilled several times in the most complete manner, and nevertheless we find that the animal organs situated in them, be they ever so excitable, give no indication of the presence of an effective [chain] through resulting contraction. We find no isolator, according to the concept of it set up above in §2, in the chain, and yet it is wholly ineffective. An example of such a chain is Fig. 9. (a, b) are the two lower extremities of a very excitable frog lying on a dry glass plate, still organically connected with one another; (d) their prepared sciatic nerves; (e, e) are two completely homogeneous, dry zinc plates, upon which the ends of those nerves opposite the muscles lie; (h, f) are two completely dry pieces of silver of like [alloy and size, whose points each touch a zinc rod opposite the nerve. We now close the chain at g, by bringing the silver pieces into contact with one another — and all is quiet. The same at the opening. The result will be the same when Fig. 9 is transformed into Fig. 13, 14, or 15. We now let the number of the members remain the same, but alter their relative sequence upon one another, so that in Fig. [9] we take the silver away on the right hand and bring it on the left hand to the place of the zinc piece (e), and take this away there and bring it to the place on the right hand that became free through (f). Now the chain finds itself in the position of Fig. 10. We close at g so that we bring (f) into contact with (e) indeed, but do not separate both again the next instant; rather we let them remain in contact — and it is no longer quiet: contractions arise, and indeed, if the organs were very excitable, in both, but with the remarkable difference that they are far more violent in the extremity whose nerve lies upon the zinc plate (e), [and] weaker in the opposite one. At a lower degree of [irritability] the latter will remain quiet, and only the former will twitch. Now we separate (f) from (e) by removing their outer ends from one another, and now, in an accurately arranged experiment, that which twitched before will remain quiet, and the one that was quiet before will alone suffer contractions, though mostly weaker than at the closing of the chain. The result will moreover be the same if, as in Fig. [11], (f) and (e) remain away, or as in Fig. 12 (f) and (e) do not [stand] immediately at the [nerve, but at homogeneous conducting parts, e.g. wet sponge, water, etc., with which the nerve stands in contact,] and one proceeds in the way indicated.



§9. Law of the conditions under which a chain composed wholly of heterogeneous conductors of galvanism is nevertheless ineffective [1798, p. 23]

This [state of things] gives occasion to manifold considerations. In the inactive chains of Fig. 9, 13, 14, and 15 we soon find a striking symmetry of their construction. There are in their periphery two points opposite one another, (g) and (h); if we draw a line through these which touches the chain only in two points — namely in those where it cuts it — we have divided it into two halves, which possibly in magnitude need not be exactly equal, in which, however, we find an equal number of conductors different in visible quality, and these following upon one another quite in the one [half] as in the other, if we start out from one of the cutting-points (g) or (h). The forces standing opposite one another from both sides are mutually equally great: they cancel one another; no action can arise in the chain which would be in a position to make an impression upon the nerves [or] their muscles even at the highest [degree of excitability]. All remains at rest. — It is otherwise with the chains of Fig. 10, 11, [and] 12. Here we can find no two points through which [the chain] cut would fall into two halves in which not only the number of the different qualities, but also the bodies belonging to these, in reversed sequence upon one another (if we start out from any point, e.g. g) would be the same on both sides. Here, consequently, not equal forces stand opposite one another. The sum of force (x) of the one side will cancel, annihilate, of the [sum] standing opposite it from the other side (x + y) just so much as it itself amounts to, i.e. x, and only the remainder, (x + y) − x = y, will be left over, whose magnitude determines the stronger or weaker degree of the action, and with it the magnitude of the impression upon the excitable nerve, and thereby the magnitude of the next product of the action of the stimulus — [the contraction] — standing in proportion with the degree of irritability of the organs, and with it [the consequence following upon it is determined]. We shall therefore, in consequence of this, be able to set up as a law confirming itself everywhere and without exception — for where one believed [an exception], it is only apparent — that in every galvanic chain in which two points can be found through which, [the chain being] cut, it falls into two halves in which equally great forces stand opposite one another from both sides — or, in other words, in which on both sides the determining-grounds of the actions are exactly the same in sequence and in degree — no action of any kind at all can take place. This law holds, however, not for whole galvanic chains alone; it seems also to fit their parts. Instead of several, only one example from experience. In Fig. 16, (e, f, e) = zinc, silver, zinc is to be regarded as one metal piece; for at (α) and (β) the determining-grounds of actions of equal magnitude cancel one another, and only the determining-grounds in (γ) and (δ) come into account. But one sees that they are of equal magnitude and sequence with those in (e) and [the other member], and the whole chain can again be divided by two points (g) and (k) into two halves in which the determining-grounds of the actions to be brought into account [on both sides] are the same; consequently no activity can arise — the chain is therefore ineffective. Not so will it be in Fig. 17: here we must, as in the preceding case, regard (e, f, e) and (f, e, f) as one and one [piece]. The action produced by an (e, f) will therefore equal the one occasioned by an (e, f), and since on the other side none of equal magnitude stands opposed to it, there is surplus of force present, and with it the activity in the [chain] is determined. But we also find in this chain no two points through which, [the chain being] cut, it would give two halves in which, starting out from one of the cutting-points, the actions to be brought into account would be exactly the same. SECOND PANEL — FROM THE *BEYTRÄGE* (JENA, 1800)



Proof that galvanic action, or galvanism, is possible and actual also in inorganic nature — Introduction [1800, p. 132]

The task of our treatise, as its heading already indicates, is: to show that that activity which usage has for long, more or less determinately, designated with the name galvanic action, galvanism, is operative also in inorganic nature. In my "Proof, that a constant galvanism accompanies the process of life in the animal kingdom" (Weimar, 1798), published last year, the presence of the same in the animal world was demonstrated. That it has place in duly ordered systems composed of bodies of determinate quality, taken partly from organic nature, partly from inorganic, became known to us with the discovery of the first galvanic experiment; for what is a chain, consisting of metals etc. and an excitable animal organ, other than a conflict of both worlds? But there still remained the question unsolved: Is the production of this activity bound throughout to organic-animal bodies and their parts; is it possible only among them, or [only through] their presence in chains that have also been formed by other members belonging to inorganic nature; does it, in the last case, spring from those alone, or have all members of the chain an equal share in it; and is it just as possible that it have place in chains formed solely of members from inorganic nature, as is the case with chains formed solely of members of animal origin? — Very probably, with the affirmative answer to the last part of this question, the part preceding it would also be affirmatively solved; and indeed, at the first and therefore often least partial survey of the phenomenon as one is wont to construe it — i.e. of the givenness of its ground of actuality with a [certain] spatial arrangement of a mixture of partly organic-animal, partly inorganic bodies — one does not see how the one class of these bodies should come to a privilege over the other. For if every effectiveness becomes recognisable to the senses only in so far as its product displays itself, sooner or later, through alteration of spatial phenomena; if, further, we are able to estimate ex- and intensity of a force, of an activity, only through the ex- and intensity of its product; if in galvanism this activity, where animal organs are present in the chain, manifests itself through the alterations which these suffer, and which commonly show themselves as contraction of the organs — then the hypothesis that animal parts alone can be the source of this activity loses still more of probability when we perceive — and this in almost every experiment — how this product, and consequently also its ground, can gain quite incomparably higher degrees when animal parts are set in conflict with other, non-animal [parts], as e.g. an excitable nerve with two different metals, with charcoal and caustic-alkali solution, graphite and acids, etc., than when merely animal parts act mutually upon one another; and that even these do so only in so far as it is mediated through a substance assumed in itself as decidedly inorganic, namely water and watery moisture. Several of those who have worked on our subject have felt the importance of this argument strongly enough, be it clearly or darkly, and hardly did anything else lead them, when with such confidence they endeavoured to place the ground of the galvanic phenomena not in the excitable organs but in inorganic nature. (Here belong next all those who made electricity the principle of these phenomena — whither in the end also Creve's opinion, that a decomposition of water, and more or less Ackermann's, that the production of a concentrated, as it were etherised oxygen gas (oxygen-half-gas, life-ether) through the different members of the chain grounds these phenomena, are to be reckoned.) Nevertheless that hypothesis too has had its admirers, and these were precisely such as were acquainted with the compass of galvanic phenomena in a fairly high degree. This prejudice must therefore well, as every prejudice in general, have had its ground, and it will be interesting to seek it out, because this alone can mark out for us the way on which we shall have to contest it.

If we embrace all galvanic phenomena so far, however manifold, with one glance, we notice in them, as what is common to all, alteration of the excitable organs present in the chain (wholly, or only with their nerves) — an alteration which, in parts separated from the organic whole, consists now in contraction, now in modification of their excitability, but in undisturbed organisation of the whole is felt, according to the character of the sense affected, in a manner suitable to it: in the eye e.g. as light, on the tongue as taste, in the wound as pain, etc. From the first moment, therefore, the animality of these parts, in which that alteration first manifested itself, drew all attention to itself; and long accustomed to see in the organic [realm] means and end, cause and effect, originally united, it was easy in this case also to hold the animal organ for the reagent of its own activity, and thus to derive the whole effectiveness of the galvanic chain from the animal parts contained in it. Guided by the law of reciprocal action, pressing itself upon everyone unbidden, one did indeed hold side-effects of these substances upon the inanimate nature bound up with them not to be impossible; and even before an experiment had made them noticeable, one even believed that they must be demonstrable a priori, and that their weakness alone was the cause why they would not become sensible to our senses; one did not therefore in fact exclude inorganic nature from the capacity to react upon the activity present in the galvanic chain; always, however, those organs were yet the source of it, and without them one made oneself little hope of being able to discover a trace of it. For the direct refutation of this opinion there consequently remained, if it was possible at all, no other way than to find in inorganic nature bodies in which a slight alteration of their inner quality — for to this the result of all working and being-worked-upon of space-filling individuals reduces itself — could modify their outer character considerably enough to be sensibly noticed by us; to bring these bodies, further, as members into chains which, apart from the lack of the animal organ, united all the conditions with which, where those are present, galvanic effectiveness is given; and attentively to observe what, sooner or later, might occur in them. One will find in what follows that we have really taken this way, and that it has rewarded us wholly with the success that must correspond to an actual incorrectness of that hypothesis.
