DEDICATION

Who he was. Ferdinand Gotthold Max Eisenstein (1823–1852), the Berlin prodigy whom Gauss championed and Humboldt bankrolled — the boy who at fourteen had outrun his Gymnasium, at seventeen was sitting in Dirichlet's university lectures, and at twenty-one was publishing in Crelle's Journal papers that made the old masters take notice. His name survives in the daily working vocabulary of mathematics — the Eisenstein integers, Eisenstein's criterion for the irreducibility of polynomials, the Eisenstein series of the theory of modular forms — but the memoirs in which he earned those names have waited outside English for nearly one hundred and eighty years.

His home and circumstances. Born in Berlin on 16 April 1823, the son of Johan Konstantin Eisenstein and Helene Pollack, he survived the infant meningitis that killed his brothers and sisters, and grew up a frail, brilliant, musical boy. At fourteen he entered the Friedrich Werder Gymnasium; by fifteen the school had nothing left to teach him, and he went to Euler and Lagrange on his own account. In 1843 he travelled to Dublin and met William Rowan Hamilton, who put Abel's proof of the impossibility of solving the quintic into his hands and so turned him toward research. August Crelle, the founder of the Journal für die reine und angewandte Mathematik, discovered the young man's genius and introduced him to Alexander von Humboldt, who became his patron; Humboldt sent him to Gauss at Göttingen in 1844, and Gauss's enthusiasm — talents, he wrote to Humboldt on 14 April 1846, such as nature bestows on only a few in each century — made the twenty-three-year-old famous overnight. Breslau gave him an honorary doctorate in 1845; he took his post-doctoral degree at Berlin in 1847 and lectured there. In March 1848 the revolutions caught him: dragged from a house during the street fighting of 19 March and held overnight in the citadel at Spandau, he was released the next day, but the mistreatment broke something in his already fragile health, and the whisper that he was a "republican" dried up the money that had kept him alive.

His death. In late July 1852 he suffered a severe hemorrhage; Humboldt raised the funds to send him to Sicily to recover, but the money came too late. He died of pulmonary tuberculosis in Berlin on 11 October 1852, aged twenty-nine, in what the biographers call forlorn solitude — and the eighty-three-year-old Humboldt walked behind his coffin to the grave.

Why this work is vital — to prove his logic. The memoir translated here is the sixth of his "Contributions to the theory of elliptic functions" (1847): the exact investigation of the infinite double products from which elliptic functions are composed as quotients. Watch what he does in these opening sections. He defines the double product, throws out the degenerate cases by a ruthless three-way case analysis on the ratio of his two periods, and then — because no one had ever proved such a thing — stops everything to prove the general convergence lemma for r-fold series that his whole theory needs, counting lattice points in dyadic blocks in an argument that still appears, in essence, in the textbooks. Then he turns the ratio test itself into a fine instrument, squeezing the convergence of his logarithmic series between two bounds that both tend to M(x)/M₀. This is a twenty-four-year-old doing analysis the way Gauss did arithmetic: from the definitions, with nothing taken on trust. The famous irreducibility criterion that bears his name was born the same year, in a letter to Gauss of 18 August 1847 about the division of the lemniscate — Abel's theorem proved by noticing something about coefficients, on the same path Gauss himself had walked in 1797 without publishing. Read these pages and that letter together and you see the same mind at work: find the hidden structure, then prove that it must be so.

His relation to this series. The Boneyard keeps works the world shelved before it finished listening. Eisenstein's 1847 volume — printed with a preface by Gauss himself — gathered the higher arithmetic and the elliptic functions of his four working years; the century absorbed his theorems and forgot to read his proofs. The double-product memoir, which the histories call fundamental but seldom quoted, has, after the research recorded in the notes, no English translation to its name. So here is its opening movement, carried across at last — because he proved his logic, and then the tuberculosis took him at twenty-nine, and the book waited.

— The Editor



A note on this edition

This volume translates, complete, the opening movement — §§1–2 — of one memoir by Gotthold Eisenstein:

- Genaue Untersuchung der unendlichen Doppelproducte, aus welchen die elliptischen Functionen als Quotienten zusammengesetzt sind, und der mit ihnen zusammenhangenden Doppelreihen (als eine neue Begründungsweise der Theorie der elliptischen Functionen, mit besonderer Berücksichtigung ihrer Analogie zu den Kreisfunctionen) [Exact investigation of the infinite double products from which the elliptic functions are composed as quotients, and of the double series connected with them (as a new foundation of the theory of elliptic functions, with particular regard to their analogy with the circular functions)], Beiträge zur Theorie der elliptischen Functionen VI, in Mathematische Abhandlungen, besonders aus dem Gebiete der höhern Arithmetik und der elliptischen Functionen (Berlin: G. Reimer, 1847), vol. pp. 213–230 [= Journal für die reine und angewandte Mathematik 35 (1847), pp. 153–170]. The memoir as a whole runs to vol. pp. 213–334; the remainder (§3 to the end) is mapped in the notes file (NOTES.md), as are the volume's other memoirs.

These two sections are the founding act of the memoir and a complete movement in themselves: §1 defines the infinite double product, excludes the degenerate cases of the period ratio, and proves — as a lemma the theory cannot do without — the convergence of r-fold numerical series; §2 applies the lemma to prove the convergence of the logarithmic series of the product, by a ratio argument of Eisenstein's own devising. The conditionally convergent coefficient series and their rearrangements, announced at the close of §2 as the "third problem," belong to §3 and are not translated here.

The translation was made from the digitised 1847 Reimer edition (Internet Archive item `mathematischeabh00eiseuoft`, the Toronto copy; a second 1847 copy, `10053424bsb`, served as collation witness; file checksums are pinned in NOTES.md). The author died in 1852; the text is public domain. The machine-read text is good in the prose and treacherous in the displays — Fraktur α, β, γ, subscripts and fractions suffer — and the translation therefore brackets what it cannot certify: uncertain readings are bracketed, never silently repaired; the handful of load-bearing displays (the two product forms, the logarithmic series (1.), the r-fold lemma and its constant C, the quotient bounds of §2) were reconstructed from the surrounding argument and are flagged wherever the machine text could not certify them. Page anchors in the form [1847, p. n] refer to the printed pagination of the 1847 volume.

His notation stands. His analytischer Modul M(u) is the modulus (absolute value) of the complex number u; his accented sums ∑′ are sums over restricted index sets, the restriction stated in place; his (α.) and (β.) are his labels for the two systems of coefficients in the lemma. The names by which the century knows the results — the Eisenstein integers, Eisenstein's criterion, the Eisenstein series — are the textbooks' and the historians', and they live in the dedication and the notes, not in his text. It was made without consulting any modern copyrighted translation; no English translation of this memoir was found after research, though its results are restated wherever elliptic functions are taught.

One correction of the record: the brief for this build described its author as Danzig-born. He was not — the biographical sources (MacTutor, the auction-house biography, the encyclopaedia entries) agree he was born in Berlin on 16 April 1823. The dedication prints Berlin.

THE MEMOIR



§1. The double product, its degenerate cases, and the convergence lemma [1847, pp. 213–227]

Just as, with the circular functions, there occur infinite products whose factors are defined by vanishing in succession for all the values of the variable which are terms of an arithmetical progression continued to both sides — that is, for all values of the form αm + β, where m runs through all whole numbers (positive, negative, and zero) from −∞ to ∞ and α, β denote constants — so the elliptic functions are composed of infinite double products, in which the root-values of the individual factors are determined by the terms of an arithmetical progression with double entry, that is, by the terms of a double series whose general term has the form αm + βn + γ, where m and n run simultaneously and independently of one another through all whole values from −∞ to ∞. The infinite products occurring with the circular functions are therefore of the form

[the printed display, reconstructed from the machine-read text: ∏m (1 − x/(αm + β))]

and the infinite double products occurring with the elliptic functions are of the form

[the printed display, reconstructed from the machine-read text: ∏m,ₙ (1 − x/(αm + βn + γ))].

Before I pass to the exact investigation of these latter infinite double products, I wish first to linger a moment over the consideration of the expression αm + βn + γ itself, in which the three constants α, β, γ may in general have any complex values whatever. Put

u = αm + βn + γ.

This expression u may be regarded as a form by which certain values can be represented. The nature of the expression in this respect evidently depends principally on the ratio of the two coefficients α and β, while γ plays only a subordinate role. If α stands to β in a real and rational ratio — that is, if the quotient α/β is equal to a real and rational number — then the expression u represents every value it represents infinitely often; that is, there are then in each case infinitely many associated pairs of whole values m, n for which u takes one and the same value. For suppose, when this case occurs, that α/β expressed in lowest terms = μ/ν, where μ and ν are whole numbers without common divisor, and put α = μd, β = νd; then [the printed computation, garbled in the machine text, shows u = d(μm + νn) + γ], and since here the expression μm + νn represents every whole number, and each infinitely often, the values of the expression u are nothing but the values of d·(whole number) + γ, each written, as it were, infinitely often. In other words, u represents only the terms of a simple arithmetical progression, but each of them infinitely often. In this case the infinite double product would therefore be nothing but an infinite simple product in which each factor had been written infinitely often; the value of the product would thus always be infinitely great, and for this reason the case is to be unconditionally excluded. For the same reasons the case is of course to be excluded in which one of the two coefficients α, β (or even both) is zero. If, secondly, α stands to β in a real but irrational ratio — say β = ωα with ω real but irrational — then the expression u becomes

u = α(m + nω) + γ.

Here, since ω is irrational, m + nω can, by a known theorem, come as close as one wishes to any prescribed real magnitude, and this for infinitely many pairs m, n; u can therefore likewise come arbitrarily close, for infinitely many whole values m, n, to every value of the form αk + γ, where k is arbitrary real. But this consequence is, with regard to the infinite double product, just as inadmissible as that of the first case, and so the second case is to be rejected like the first — unless one were to stipulate that m and n are not to take all whole values, but only such as satisfy some further condition, e.g. that an expression α′m + β′n + γ′ similar to u, or rather its analytical modulus, shall always remain enclosed between quite definite bounds. There remains the third case, when α stands to β in an imaginary ratio — that is, when [the imaginary part of the quotient of the two complex numbers α and β is different from zero; the machine text reads "real part," evidently misread]. This case does not have the difficulties of the other two; for whereas there the number of whole values of m and n for which the analytical modulus of u lies between definite bounds is always infinitely great as soon as it is different from 0 — that is, as soon as any value of the modulus of u at all falls between those bounds — here, on the contrary, this number is always finite and bounded. Let the analytical modulus of a complex number be denoted by the letter M, so that M(p + qi) = √(p² + q²) when p and q are real; let further α = a + a′i, β = b + b′i, γ = c + c′i. Putting am + bn + c = v, which is the real part of u, and a′m + b′n + c′ = v′, which is the coefficient of i in u, one gets M(u) = √(v² + v′²). If now M(u) is to lie between certain bounds, then a fortiori v and v′, apart from sign, must lie between the same bounds; hence v − c and v′ − c′ also lie between quite definite bounds; and since the determinant ab′ − ba′ of the linear system of equations

am + bn = v − c, a′m + b′n = v′ − c′,

by which m and n can be expressed in v − c and v′ − c′, is in our case different from 0, m and n too are enclosed between definite bounds; and since between these there lie only a finite number of whole values, there exist a fortiori only a finite number of pairs of whole values m, n for which M(u) lies between the given bounds. In particular, then, there is only a finite number of combinations m, n which make the analytical modulus of u smaller than any given positive value. We shall therefore henceforth always presuppose the ratio of the two coefficients α and β to be imaginary. These considerations are not new, but it did not seem to me out of place to emphasize them particularly here.

The properties of a product are best investigated by considering the logarithms of the individual factors. The variable x in our product ∏(1 − x/u) is presupposed to be arbitrary complex. The logarithm of 1 − x/u can be developed in a convergent series in powers of x as soon as M(u) > M(x); it is therefore expedient to exclude from the product, for the present, those factors in which M(u) < M(x). The number of factors to be excluded is, by the preceding, finite; and let the product of the rest be denoted by

and consequently the logarithm of the whole product is

(1.) −x∑′ 1/u − (x²/2)∑′ 1/u² − (x³/3)∑′ 1/u³ − ⋯ etc.,

This transformation of the logarithm of the product is legitimate as soon as one can show that the sums forming the coefficients of the series (1.) are convergent, and that the series (1.) itself converges independently of the order of its terms — hence even when all terms are replaced by their analytical moduli. As for the coefficients of the series first: I shall prove that, from the third on inclusive — that is, the coefficients of x³, x⁴, x⁵, in inf. — they not merely converge, but are even wholly independent of the arrangement of the values u, and always take quite definite and ever the same values, in whatever order the terms of the sums are made to follow one another in the addition. There remains, however, a gap here: the question arises whether the converse of a known theorem is correct — whether a series converging independently of the arrangement of its terms always remains convergent when all terms of the series are replaced by their analytical moduli; whether one may therefore necessarily conclude, from the fact that a series passes into a divergent one when the analytical moduli are written in place of the terms, that the series in its original form cannot converge independently of the arrangement of its terms. If all terms of the series are real, then the sum of all positive, as well as the sum of all negative terms, must by itself form a convergent series, and therefore the sum of the absolute values of all terms converges too. But if the terms are complex (imaginary), bring the general term to the form r(cos φ + i sin φ), where r is the modulus of the term: then the two series whose general terms are r cos φ and r sin φ must also converge independently of the arrangement of the terms; and since in them the terms are real, they must keep this property even when for cos φ and sin φ only the absolute value is always put. Since now, apart from sign, |cos φ| < 1, |sin φ| < 1, and therefore cos²φ < |cos φ|, sin²φ < |sin φ|, the series whose general terms are r cos²φ and r sin²φ must a fortiori converge in the same sense, for they arise from the two preceding ones by diminishing the numerical value of all terms. Those series, now composed entirely of positive terms r cos²φ, r sin²φ, give by addition a likewise convergent series whose general term is r(cos²φ + sin²φ) = r — namely equal to the analytical modulus of the general term of the original series — and thus the cited theorem is placed beyond doubt. The strict separation of the series which owe their convergence to a particular arrangement, and which on a change of this arrangement partly become divergent and partly can change their sum, from those which converge wholly independently of the arrangement of the terms and always keep the same sum, in whatever order the terms are made to follow one another — is probably due principally to Dirichlet, who in his admirable memoir on the arithmetical progression enters into very interesting details on this subject.
