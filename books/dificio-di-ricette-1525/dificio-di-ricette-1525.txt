DEDICATION

To the unknown author of the Dificio di ricette — whoever he was.

He never gives his name. In his preface "To my kindly reader" he tells us
only this: that for many years, not without labour of his own, he delighted
in gathering and heaping together diverse and various tried recipes; that he
showed them, confessed, to many friends; and that they prayed and even
constrained him, for the universal benefit, to put them in print — so the
work was divided into three recipe-books. Beyond this voice, nothing is
known of him: not his city, not his trade, not the year of his death. None
is invented here.

His work is vital, and the proof is not praise but print-runs. This little
anonymous pamphlet — first printed at Venice in 1525 — invented the
"book of secrets": 187 micro-recipes running from cures for toothache to
burning a candle underwater, from keeping grapes all year to seeing the
stars at noonday. It was reprinted twenty-eight times in forty years, and
every book of secrets after it walks in its footprints. It has never been
put into English. This edition gives, complete, the First Recipe-Book — "Of
diverse and various virtues" — which is the whole of the 1574 printing's
body text here used.

Where to go next: the French translations of 1539 and 1545, and the Dutch,
for the book's travels; William Eamon's *Science and the Secrets of Nature*
for the world that made it.

[Title page of 1574:]

A NEW WORK, ENTITLED BUILDING OF RECIPES, IN WHICH ARE CONTAINED THREE MOST
USEFUL RECIPE-BOOKS. In the first are treated many and diverse virtues; in
the second is taught the composing of various kinds of sweet and useful
odours; in the third and last are treated certain secret medicinal remedies
needful for healing human bodies, as the table contains. At the sign of the
Queen. 1574.

TO MY KINDLY READER

Having for many years, kind reader — not without labour of mine — delighted
in gathering and heaping together diverse and various sorts of tried
recipes, which will teach you many most beautiful secrets; and having shown
them, confessed, to many friends of mine, who have prayed and even constrained
me that for the universal benefit I should put them in print, so that they,
together with those who delight in such virtues, may have copies of them;
so, to their satisfaction, I have resolved to have my aforesaid little
precept-book printed — from which every man may take much utility and
pleasure. The work shall be divided into three recipe-books. In the first
shall be written many most useful recipes, which shall also serve to give
pleasure, as in the present work you shall see. In the second recipe-book
you shall see written many most worthy secrets concerning the art of sweet
odours. The third and last recipe-book shall treat of certain secrets needful
for the health of human bodies — each of which is worth a treasure; for
certainly, were it not for the secrets of the medicinal art, our life would
be much briefer. And so, live happy, and fare well.

THE FIRST RECIPE-BOOK, OF DIVERSE AND VARIOUS VIRTUES

A remedy against every stink and fetor of the mouth, or bad breath from the
stomach, or other cause. Take rosemary flowers, three [ounces?]; cloves, five
drams [a dram ~ 4 grams]; cinnamon, a dram and a half; nutmeg, half a dram;
musk, a little, at your discretion. Then take honey enough to bind the
above-named composition; and take of it, when you will, as much as a
hazelnut, more or less at your pleasure. This composition is good against
sickness of the stomach, and gives a most delicate and loving breath. And he
that takes of this composition in time of suspected plague — that sweetest
odour will defend him from all sickness and from all corruption of the air.

To know for whose fault it is that conception fails — the man's or the
woman's — when he has long lain with the woman. Take two bowls; in one put
bran [semola], in the other... [the rite: let the man pass water in one and
the woman in the other; leave three days; in whose bowl worms are found, in
him or her lies the fault of the unfruitful seed.]

To make a man or a woman have a good memory. Take the rind(?) of...
[Take] bugloss flowers, borage flowers, and bugloss (?) flowers, two drams
each; then take fine cinnamon and nutmeg, long pepper, half a dram each;
fine musk, ten grains; candied (?)... one ounce; let all these things be
powdered subtly and mixed in manner of a confection with an ounce of rose
syrup; and take every morning as much as a hazelnut, continuing this electuary
for the space of a month — and you shall have a most firm memory.

To make a good voice for singing, or for pleading, or for disputing, or for
reading in church. Take cumin and fennel seed, that is... and cinnamon and
pellitory, each... and use it in the morning. And it is done.

To make a powder that makes the teeth whitest and clean, purges catarrh,
heals toothache, and firms teeth that shake, using the said powder. Take red
corals, four parts; and pellitory cut small and dried on a plate over a most
gentle fire till it may be powdered subtly, one part; mastic and mace, half a
part; and a little fine sugar. Powder all well and mix together; and with
the powder rub the teeth — or have them rubbed by one who... — and you shall
make them whitest. Likewise, when your tooth aches or shakes, rub with the
powder, holding it a little in the mouth, and you shall see marvellous
working.

To make grapes and pomegranates keep the whole year. Take cistern water and
put it in a cauldron, and put in a little honey; as it begins to boil, put
the stalk of the vine in, tied with a thread, till it sweats a little; then
take it out. Do the same to the pomegranates, and they shall keep the whole
year.

To make a powder which, laid on paper and rubbed with the fingers, and then
writing with water, the letter becomes blackest. Take gall-nuts and Roman
vitriol [copperas], as much of one as of the other, and a little writing
varnish; powder subtly in a mortar; then lay the powder on the paper and rub
with the fingers, and then write with clear water — and straightway the
letters shall become blackest. You may carry this powder on a journey and use
it at your needs.

To see the stars at noonday, or at whatever hour you will, by day. Take a
clean basin full of fountain or cistern water, and have a mirror, and put it
in the basin, and set the basin in the sun's rays — and you shall see clearly
the stars of the sky by day.

To know whether one is a virgin — man or woman — that is, whether he or she
has spilt his seed. Take a doubled thread or twine, and measure gaily the
thickness of the throat, and cut away the rest you have over; and with that
measure, single not double, set it atop the head and measure the length of
the head down to the beard. If you see the thread does not reach the beard,
that male or female is a virgin; and if it passes the beard, not a virgin —
for know that as the person is corrupted, the throat thickens and the head
shortens. And if of this you would make trial, measure those you know surely
to be virgins, male or female, from nine years to thirteen — who are surely
virgins — and you shall see the thread cannot reach the beard; then likewise
measure one who is not a virgin, and you shall see the thread passes the
beard by more than two fingers. And this truth you shall find by such trial.

To make a handkerchief burn, and when it has done burning it shall not be
burnt nor have had any harm. Take a handkerchief and wet it very well in
aqua vitae [spirits], then light it with a candle — and it shall burn; and
when done burning you shall have the handkerchief whole.

To take the measure of a man's or woman's foot without measuring the foot —
do thus. Take a doubled thread, and with it stride the head of the middle
finger of the hand, doubled; and take the measure up the palm of the hand to
the wrist of the right hand — and you shall find the foot of the male or
female you measured to be just so great. And of this you may soon make trial.

To make a candle on the table go off with a great crack, and frighten women
and others — and you may do your design, whether to steal the viands or to
kiss your sweetheart. Take a little stick as thick as a goose quill, and
round the little stick make a cannon [tube] of paper; tie the mouth on one
side, then fill the cannon with bombard powder [gunpowder], not too finely
powdered; tie the cannon as sausages are tied — as many ties as you make, so
many bangs it shall make on the table. Then take a wick and put the cannon in
it, as long as two fingers' breadth, pressing the powder round the wick in
the cannon; press the powder in the cannon with an iron, then cast white wax
melted upon the cannon, and contrive to make a candle. Set it burning on the
table and you shall see a marvellous noise and crack — and straightway it
goes out, and you may do every effect of yours as above is said.

To put a hen's egg inside a narrow-necked flask. Take an egg and boil it hard;
and when cooked, put it to soak in strongest vinegar for fifteen or twenty
days; and you shall see the egg with its shell become tender as paste. With
your hand make it long and thin enough to enter the flask; then fill the
flask with fresh water — and the egg shall become round as before within
fifteen days.

To make the hair not turn white. Take bitch's milk and anoint the head with
it, and they shall no more turn white — and it is proved and certain.

To bring back any cloth of wool or silk that has lost its grain [dye] to its
colour. Take quicklime, two ounces; citrus (?) ash, one ounce; clear water,
two pounds; mix all together, and leave it to soak a quarter of an hour —
and it is done. This shall bring back its colour.

To wash scarlet and take away stains. Take white tartar [cream of tartar],
four ounces by weight, and boil in twelve pounds of water till a third is
gone; then strain it. And when you lay it on the cloth, see the water be
lukewarm; and let it soak. And when it is dry, it shall be returned to its
colour.

To freshen camlet [a cloth] so it seems new. Take water and boil it a little
with bran; then let it rest some days till it turns a little sour; and with
this lukewarm water wash the camlet, and it shall be clean.

To turn silk cloth to its colour. Take quicklime, one ounce; citrus (?) ash,
a handful; put in a basin of water and mix together; then set on the fire and
let clear; then wet the stain with a sponge, and wet nothing else — and it is
done.

Also for toothache, that it shall not return. Take twenty betony (?) leaves
and put them in a pipkin with good old wine, and put in a fistful of common
salt; and boil till the leaves are well cooked. Then take from the fire, and
let cool till you may bear it in the mouth; and take a mouthful of the wine,
and hold it on the side where the ache is — and you shall be healed.

To make the hairs fall where you will on the person. Take quicklime from the
kiln, eight parts, and one part orpiment [arsenic sulphide], and mix
together, and make powder; then put in strong lye, and set all on the fire
in a glazed pipkin, and stir till it thickens. To know when it is good and
done, take a goose feather and put it in the mixture: if the feather plucks
[i.e., its barbs loosen], it is done and good; if not, boil again till it
plucks. Then, when you would use the composition, in a stove or warm place
anoint the place where you would have the hairs go with the medicine; and
mark well — when you feel the heat, wash straightway with hot water, and
quickly, lest the flesh peel.

To make a water that hardens and tempers iron so it cuts other iron like
wood. Take earthworms, as many as you please, and make water of them in the
alembic; and make as much more water in the alembic of radish root; and
finally make water of apple root. Then mix the waters together, as much of
one as of the other. When you would use it, temper your weapon or iron in it;
and if you temper it twice, it shall be more effectual.

To make the beard or the hair grow. Take many bees making honey, and dry them
in a... at the fire, and make powder of them, and knead with common oil; and
with the ointment anoint the place many times — and you shall see great
trial.

To make a black paper for writing without ink. Take a lamp with oil in it,
lighted, and set a bowl over it, and leave it till all the oil is consumed;
then take the smoke with a sponge and blacken your paper. Then take a piece
of the paper as great as you will, and lay it on white paper, and write with
a pin's point or other iron upon the black paper — and you shall have your
letters black upon the white paper. And note the black side must lie upon
the white paper.

A way to make a bait to bring a great number of doves to your dovecote. Take
sorghum [forgo], as much as you will, and boil with water; and when it is
nigh cooked, drain it somewhat from the water, and...

To take away every ink stain from books. Take sal ammoniac [ammonium
chloride]... rock alum, each one ounce; sal ammoniac (?), four ounces; distil
all these in the alembic, and with this water wet the written sheets, and the
letter shall go away.

To make a kidskin parchment blue, or green, violet, or black, for writing in
it with gold or silver — and I shall teach you to grind the gold, that with
it you may write. Take a kidskin parchment, thin, and have it well scraped by
the parchment-makers; then put it to soak in clear water, and wash it well,
and break it with your hand. Then have a great hoop, so the parchment may be
stretched in it with string, using your ingenuity. Then, if you would make it
blue, take pitch (?), blue or green or violet, which you shall buy from the
colour-sellers, and soak the pitch of what colour you will the parchment for
the space of a Credo; sponge the parchment with the colour, then let dry, and
give another coat, and then another, till the parchment is well coloured to
your liking. See that it is done, and it shall be of most beautiful colour,
in which you may write with gold or silver. Likewise, if you would make it
black, take ink; if violet, do likewise with brazilwood (?).

To grind fine gold or silver, for writing or for laying with the brush on
whatever work you will. Take as many leaves as you will of beaten gold; then
take a glass cup, smooth within, and wet it with clear water, and lay as many
leaves as you will of gold in it, one upon another. Then with the middle
finger of the hand grind the gold little by little till it is well subtle —
wetting the finger now and then — and take no more space at a time than a
... till it is ground; and so go following, and grind it all little by little.
When it is well subtly ground, put in a little water, and grind, and temper
the gold; then fill the cup with water, and mix with the finger, and let the
gold settle to the bottom for half an hour; then pour away all the water, and
there shall remain at the bottom a subtle heaven [film] of gold. Let it dry,
and cover it that no dust enter. And when you would write on tinted papers
verses or sonnets or orations, take a little knife and scrape some gold, and
put it in a quill, and temper with gum water, and write — and you shall make
most beautiful gold letters written on coloured papers, which shall be a most
brilliant sight. And if you would lay the gold with the brush where you will,
you may do it. And know that if you would burnish it with a tooth upon a
stone, you may do it — for in every way it is most beautiful.

To write gold and silver letters on any paper. Take juniper leaves and make
juice of them; then take filings of gold or silver, and put them in the
juice, and leave three whole days; and with it you may write perfectly what
you will.

To write gold letters, and to paint what shall seem gold without gold. Take
porporina [a gold-coloured pigment] from the colour-sellers; put it in a bowl
and with a little urine grind with the finger little by little; then fill the
bowl with urine or lye, and let settle, and throw away the water, and grind
well with the finger till it is subtlest; likewise fill the bowl with water
and let settle, and wash so many times the water remains clear — and every
time grind with the finger, that the porporina be subtlest. Then put in a
little saffron with gum water, and with it you may write small letters and
ground great letters, and paint where you please — for it shall seem ground
gold of the ducat, and it is a most beautiful secret.

To write silver letters in another way — and likewise you may paint with the
brush where you will, and it shall seem finest silver. Take marcasite of tin,
which he sells that makes pewter; first pound it in a bronze mortar, then
grind as colours are ground; then put in a little bowl and temper with water,
and let go to the bottom, and throw away the black water; and so wash twice.
Then take the marcasite left at the bottom and temper with gum water, and
write or paint where you will, and let dry, and then polish with a tooth or
with porcelain — and it shall seem finest silver.

To write on paper or cloth so it shall not seem written, and then warmed at
the fire the letters become black. Write with juice of lemons or of onions;
and when you would read, set the paper to warm at the fire — and it is done.
Note it must be well warmed.

A general rule for black hair. Those who have black hair by nature are
burnished... [obscure] and to keep it clean you must wash with bran; the bran
is to be put in a clean cauldron on the fire, and stirred so it does not
burn; and while it is hot you must rub the head with it, combing very well
with several combs, till they are cleanest. Also, without bran, you may comb
very well and rub the head with combs, and have diverse bristles of every
sort, and anoint with oil... which makes black and clean the hair, and does
good.

To make the hair most beautiful. Take... wood, and peel the first bark, and
make ash; then take half a flask of aqua vitae [spirits], and make a lye;
then wash the head well and dry in the sun. Meanwhile make... with white
soap melted... in a... till it is liquid as honey; then wet your hand in the
soap and rub well the little hairs; let dry, then anoint four or five times,
and so do every week.

To make the hair grow and come most beautiful. Take olive branches, vine
bark, date stones (?), and make ash of all; then make a lye, and wash the
head — and in a few days your hair shall grow.

To drive away the dandruff that eats the hair. Take a handful of lupins and
put to soak in water, as one does...; then give one boil, then take out; and
of this broth make a lye, and anoint three or four times.

To make the hair black. Take litharge by weight, and as much quicklime, and
knead with hot water; and with this wet your hair, and it shall become black.

To take away hairs where you will, that they never grow again. Take five
eggs, and take the shells, and lime them (?); then put them in the alembic
with the receiver, and with good fire you shall have a water; and with it go
anointing where the hairs are you would take away — and they shall not be
born again.

Not to let hairs be born again, in another way. Take a cat's... and make a
most subtle powder, ground with strongest vinegar; temper and wash the hairy
place — and those hairs shall go, and return no more.

To make a white beard black, and likewise the hairs. Take fig leaves and dry
them and make powder; then have oil of... (?) and mix with this powder, and
anoint the beard: the white shall become black.

To make the face beautiful for ladies. Take pear seed (?), fennel (?),
nettle, marrow of peach stones, and boil together; and with that water wash
where you will.

To make a red for the face, for women. Take red sandalwood, weighed subtly,
and strong vinegar twice distilled; then put of the sandalwood in it as much
as you please, and boil subtly, and put a little powdered rock alum in,
which shall make you a most perfect red. And if you would have it smell
good, pound a little musk in, or civet, or other odour that pleases you, and
it shall be excellent and good.

To make the face beautiful. Take beans and make... and make powder of them,
and temper with lukewarm water and white of egg and ass's milk; and set to
dry, then temper with water, with which wash the face — and you shall make
yourself beautiful and splendid and clean.

To make the face beautiful in another way. Take fresh bean flowers, and make
a water of them in the alembic; and with it wash, and you shall make yourself
beautiful.

To make a beautiful face. Take rosemary flowers and boil with white wine;
and with this liquor wash the face — and also drink of it — and it shall
make the face beautiful, and give good breath.

To make spots go away and make beautiful skin. Take a pinch of rock alum and
grind small; then take a white of egg, new-laid, and while warm put it on
the fire in a glazed pipkin with the rock alum; and leave till you see it has
made a film (?); and mix ever with a little stick. The medicine shall come
hard; and with it anoint the face very well for two or three days — and you
shall slough the skin, and it is tried.

To make a water that whitens the face. Take litharge, sublimate of silver
(?), two soldi's worth (?); put in a flask with strongest white vinegar, and
boil till a third is gone; then let rest, and afterwards... and keep. Take
milk and orange juice, and mix with oil of tartar, and incorporate all, and
use.

To make a water that takes away every spot and blemish from artisans' hands,
and makes the hands whitest; and also the water makes the hands and face
white for those sunburnt. Take lemon juice, and in the juice put a little
common salt; with this juice wash the hands, and let dry a little by itself;
then wash the hands, and you shall see the juice shall gnaw every spot you
have on the hands — and it is good against the itch.

To make warts go away. Take... common oil (?), sugar (?), one dram; ship's
pitch, verdigris, half a dram; incorporate together and make an ointment;
anoint the wart, and you shall heal.

To make bedbugs not trouble you at night. Take the herb... as much as you
will — for in summer all the... are covered with the herb; and put the herb
on the straw under your head... and all the bugs shall gather upon the herb,
taken, that they shall move no more to give you trouble.

To make an ointment to kill bedbugs in the bedstead. Take quicksilver
[mercury] and put it in a bowl, and with a little black soap, and with your
finger... grinding till the quicksilver is incorporated with the soap; then
put in more soap at your discretion, and likewise incorporate together; then
anoint the bedstead with the soap — and with this unction you shall kill the
bugs and their eggs, that none shall be born more.

To have no fleas in your room. Take... (?) and powder it, then boil in a
pipkin or cauldron of water a quarter of an hour; and with that water sprinkle
the room every day for four days — and you shall never feel fleas on the
ground in the room.

Also to make an ointment to kill bugs and lice, in another way. Take black
soap, two drams; sulphur by weight, one dram; aloes by weight, half a dram;
quicksilver deadened with spittle, a dram and a half; make an ointment and
anoint where the bugs are, and the lice on the person — and all shall die.

To kill lice and nits. Take black soap... (?), aloes by weight, half a dram
(?), sulphur by weight one scruple [~1.3g], stavesacre (?) by weight one dram;
mix all together, and with the ointment anoint the place where the lice are —
and all shall die with the nits, and it is proved.

To make common ink for selling — and know that those who make ink for the
shops make it only of rain water with three things. Take rain water strained
from the roof-tiles; and boil in it a... of broken gall-nuts, and let boil a
little; then take out, and take out the galls, and put in gum arabic and
vitriol at your discretion; and let boil. Try it; and when it seems black to
you, take from the fire and strain; and keep the night in the dew, and the
day in the sun. And go selling it; and as it is used up, put water in and
mix — and it shall last a great while.

To make raw ink of cistern water, in another way. Take four pounds of cistern
water, and put in four ounces of gall-nuts pounded like bean grains, and four
(?) ounces of well-pounded gum arabic, and (?) ounces of well-pounded
vitriol; and leave in the sun and the dew for ten days, and see it be mixed
every day; and if it were too thick, add a little vinegar; then take from the
sun, and it shall be done.

To make perfect and finest ink, in another way. Take eight ounces of white
wine, one ounce of gall-nuts broken in three or four pieces, and put to soak
in the wine for a day and a night; then take out the galls, and put in half
an ounce of gum arabic, and a quarter of an ounce of well-pounded or ground
vitriol; and set in the sun, and leave several days. And if it be not black
enough, put in a little more Roman vitriol — and it shall be done. The
goodness of the gall-nut is known when it is veined (?) and heavy, and sounds
(?) within, and is not dusty. The goodness of vitriol is known if it is
sky-blue within and without. The goodness of gum is known if it is clear and
brittle when chewed.

To tan a raw goatskin. Take a goatskin and put it in lukewarm water, and
leave a day; then take out, and put water on the fire, and put in a fistful
of salt and four ounces of rock alum; and warm till it begins to boil. Then
take from the fire, and let stand till it remains lukewarm; and put in a
beaten egg and a little olive oil. Then put the skin in, and stir with the
hand three or four times — and it shall be done.

leave ten days; and put in... four ounces of rock alum; and boil together...
then strain through a thin cloth, and put the strained liquor in a bladder

To soften crystal, so of many pieces you shall make one. Take burnt lead and
crystal, as much of one as of the other, and grind on marble, and mix
together; and put in a crucible, and melt, and cast what you will — and you
may make other things that could not be melted.

To make a horse that cannot stale, stale straightway. Take clear water and
make it lukewarm, and give it to drink to the horse — and he shall stale
straightway.

To chase flies from a place. Take a green bough, or a cloth of white linen,
and anoint with juice of pimpernel (?) — or of an herb called... — all the
flies shall draw to the bough or cloth, and as many as go upon it shall all
die. And change the bough or cloth at the end of six days.

To find a wick that shall never be consumed, neither in the lamp nor even in
the fire — and of this... you may make trial. Take... (?) wicks, which are
sold by the colour-sellers, and put them in the lamp that never burns out.

A perfect thing for gluing everything you will. Take dried eel skins without
... and put to soak in water for two days and two nights; then pound or beat
them with a stick very well; then boil in so much water as barely covers
them, and keep a temperate fire, till it cooks a little, stirring and
tempering; then strain, and put in a metal vessel, and let stand in the shade
till it becomes hard. And with this you may glue what you will.

For one that pisses in bed sleeping. Take nettle seed, as much as two
spoonfuls, and make powder; mastic (?), as much as two great beans, and make
most subtle powder. Then take a lump of dough as great as a fist, and knead
the powders with the dough; then make a thin cake and bake in the oven. Then
make three parts of it, and every morning take one part, and finish these
three, then another — and so do, that every day he have one, till the term of
fifteen days.

To know if must [new wine] has water in it. Take a pear and put it in the
must: if the pear goes to the bottom, know there is no water; but if it
swims above, there shall be water. And likewise... an egg (?).

To make a glue that neither fire nor water spoils. Take powdered quicklime,
temper with linseed (?) oil, and glue; then set to dry in the shade, and put
then in the fire — and it shall stand as if it were iron.

To make an excellent varnish of clear water, to lay with the brush, and it
dries straightway, and is odoriferous, to lay on paper, on woods, in every
place. Take five ounces of aqua vitae [spirits], and one ounce of... (?);
pound the... between two papers, or in a mortar — I do not say to powder it
wholly, but only broken in pieces; then take a glass vial, and put in half
the water — two and a half ounces; then put in the... so broken, and mix
very well. Then set to rest for a day and a night; then take the water and

To make a little candle or torch of snow, that shall burn. Take a wick of
many threads, as long as you please, thicker than if it were of wax; and wet
the wick many times in... (?) that is well hot and melted. Then, when the
wick is done, take snow and put round it as thick as you would make the
candle; and when done, let it rest the night in the dew, and the day put it

To make a candle burn under water. Take half a pound of wax, two ounces of
sulphur, two ounces of quicklime, one ounce of pitch (?), one ounce of
turpentine; incorporate all together, and make a candle, and light it — and
it shall burn as well in water as out.

sulphur; and have a piece of linen cloth, and roll the wick round in it, and
... of sulphur; then cover with wax, and make a candle, and light it — and
never shall it be blown out by breath.

To make a little candle of ice, that shall burn. Take a wax candle, and hang
it by the wick with a thread where the ice-water drips from the roofs, or in
another place, so the drop falls on the wick; and there shall form a covering
of ice as thick as you will upon the wax. Then put it in the candlestick on
the table, and light by the wick — for though it be iced, the heat shall make
the water run, and it shall burn; and you shall make marvel he that sits at
the table.

To make a ring jump about the house. Have made a great ring of latten
(?), hollow; and put in saltpetre, live sulphur, quicksilver — as much of one
as of the other; and stop it so nothing may come out. And set it near the

To make cooked flesh seem raw. Take hare's blood, or sucking pig's, and cook

powder upon it, and leave a little so — and it shall seem raw.

To make cooked meat seem all wormy. Take lute strings, and cut them small
like little worms; and when the meat is in the dish, put the strings so cut
upon it, and cover. When the strings feel the heat, they shall come to soften
and go writhing, that they shall seem live worms.

To make clusters of grapes appear in a room. Take a little flask, and fill it

stop so nothing may enter within; and leave so till the moon (?) is ripe.
Take this oil and burn it with a wick in a room where there is no other light
— and it shall all seem clusters of grapes.

mixed with the matter; and burn where there is no other light — and you shall
see a marvellous and beautiful thing.

PRIMACY NOTE

None found after research. No English translation of the Dificio di ricette
has been found. French translations of 1539 and 1545, and a Dutch
translation, are recorded; this is a fresh rendering from the Italian of the
1574 Venice printing. No modern rendering has been used or borrowed from.

Caveats, stated plainly:
- This edition gives the complete First Recipe-Book ("Of diverse and various
  virtues") — the whole of the 1574 printing's opening section, from the
  preface "To my kindly reader" through the household confections.
- The Second and Third Recipe-Books (perfumery; medicinal remedies) are
  present in the scan used but are not translated here; they await a later
  hand.
- The author is anonymous; the preface's account of his gathering is given
  as he gives it. Nothing of his life or death is asserted.
- Quantities are as printed; historical units and obscure ingredients carry
  brief bracketed glosses and are never silently modernized.
- Recipes are given as the 1574 text gives them; nothing is vouched for as
  safe or effectual. The greater part, the author says, he tried himself;
  the rest he leaves the reader to try.
