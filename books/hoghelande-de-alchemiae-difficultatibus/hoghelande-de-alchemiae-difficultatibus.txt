DEDICATION

**Who he was.** Theobald van Hoghelande calls himself, on his title page, of Middelburg — in Zeeland — and nothing more is securely known of him. He is a genuine mystery man of Rudolfine Europe: two books bearing his name appeared in the 1590s, and then silence. The great bibliographer John Ferguson noted a possible shared identity with one Ewald Vogel — as if "Hoghelande" might be a borrowed name — but the puzzle is unsolved [UNVERIFIED: the Vogel hypothesis is Ferguson's note, not an established fact].

**His circumstances and death.** No record of his life or death survives. The book itself is dated at Cologne, 23 December 1593 (given as the year 1594, by the Christmas reckoning), and dedicated to Ernst, Archbishop-Elector of Cologne. That is the whole of his documented biography: a man who stepped out of the dark long enough to warn the student, and stepped back in.

**Why his work is vital — his logic proved, not praised.** Hoghelande's logic is proved the hard way: by failure, confessed in print. He is not a theorist describing the art from above but a workman writing up his own wreckage — the mercury-and-gold amalgams sealed in glass on the athanor, the colors that rose and lied, the illness that followed, the suspicion that his quicksilver came from the wrong mine, the fruitless journeys after better mercury through Hungary, Transylvania, Poland, and Bohemia back to Cologne, six years gone. From this he draws the student's rule, and it is all negative, all caution: learn Latin first, for the books are in Latin and cannot be faithfully carried into another tongue; be free, leisured, and secret; spend warily, for the art devours money and the poor must abandon it; distrust the books — most are false, and even the true ones lie by transposition; trust long experience over reading, for "book opens book" only after the hands have learned; and count the devil's illusions, which detain men about trifles, among the *primary* difficulties of the art. He proves his honesty by printing his failures. That is the vital thing — not that he found the stone, but *how* he taught the student to survive not finding it.

**His relation to this rescue.** The Boneyard exists for works left in the in-between, and this little manual has waited in Latin since 1594 — the honest student's guide from inside the chymical underworld of Rudolfine Europe, written by a man who may not even have owned his own name. It is rescued here as Part the First whole: what the true student of chymistry must know, and what he must flee.

**Where a reader goes next.** John Ferguson's *Bibliotheca Chemica* for the Hoghelande identity puzzle; the *Turba Philosophorum*, whose dark sayings his Second Part expounds (not translated here); and, shelved in this same row, Michael Maier's *Examen Fucorum* (1617) — the companion fraud-buster, written a generation later, which quotes this very "Hohenlandius" on the murdered Italian monk.

— *The Editor*

# THE DIFFICULTIES OF ALCHEMY

*by Theobald van Hoghelande of Middelburg*

*IN WHICH IS TAUGHT WHAT THE TRUE STUDENT OF CHYMISTRY, ASPIRING TO PERFECTION, MUST KNOW AND WHAT HE MUST SHUN; AND MANY OBSCURE AND DIFFICULT PROPOSITIONS OF THE PHILOSOPHERS ARE EXPLAINED*

*Cologne: Henricus Falckenburg, 1594*

## TO THE MOST ILLUSTRIOUS AND MOST REVEREND PRINCE AND LORD, LORD ERNST, ARCHBISHOP OF COLOGNE, ELECTOR OF THE HOLY ROMAN EMPIRE, BISHOP OF LIEGE, DUKE OF BAVARIA AND OF BOUILLON, ETC., HIS MOST CLEMENT LORD,

*wishes health.*

Since, as Cicero says, there are three kinds of things worth seeking, most illustrious Prince and most reverend Prelate — one which draws us by its own force and dignity, another which is sought for the fruit and profit it brings, a third compounded of both, which by its own force and dignity draws us and, the more it is sought, brings also some profit — alchemy may rightly be counted of the third kind. For if we regard its dignity, what is more worthy than the science in which almost all of nature and the whole universe is beheld as in a clear mirror, holding itself as a lesser world, in which are the four elements and the fifth nature, which they call heaven? If those who have brought to light in learned monuments the natures of living things, of trees and herbs, of metals and stones, have usurped to themselves the name of philosophers, shall not they be called by a truer word philosophers — nay, wise men — who have penetrated not only nature but the workshop of nature herself, and her inmost shrines, with most sagacious and most subtle wit; who have beheld not only the general but the special causes of nature's effects, and not only the accidental but the essential? Then, since everything excellent is more to be sought for its own sake than for another's and by accident, as they say, we declare this science before all others, both theoretic and practic — theology alone excepted, which looks to the contemplation of divine things and our salvation — to be sought for its own sake. For the other sciences are not learned for themselves, but the inquiry of truth brings no relish(?) — they are learned for a stranger's sake, namely for getting gold and silver; but this is known for its own sake, because in it the inward thing is gold and silver, and not the outward, and at once the inquiry of truth. Moreover it is most excellent by reason of its subject, to which all things obey and which bears all things up. Besides, if we look at its figured and metaphorical modes of handing down, we shall pronounce this science divine rather than human, since it cannot be come to except by the grace of God alone, enlightening. Add that in it shine the mysteries of the Christian faith. For if we consider the matter of the art, we shall see a certain likeness of the most holy and undivided Trinity — sparingly be it spoken, for it is not lawful to compare created things with the Creator: one is the doctrine, one was, one is the matter, though tripartite, and it returns to unity. By this science enlightened, the wise have foreseen the day of judgment and the resurrection of the dead, in which each soul shall be joined to its own body and never more be parted. In it is depicted a certain image of the incarnation of the Son of God our Saviour, who, fallen from heaven into the womb of the Blessed Virgin Mary, was made flesh, and suffered and died for us, and after the resurrection returned into heaven with the body taken up on earth. I pass over other arcana, which are shown only to the sons and heirs of the science, since the writings of the philosophers are incredible to those who understand them not.

Who then shall sufficiently admire these great miracles of Nature? Who shall contemn the science which may in some sort be called the fount and origin of all natural philosophy, and from which the knowledge of almost all things is drawn? Further, if we look at the profit of the art and the mildness of its manners — since, according to the philosophers, it sharpens and ennobles man's wit, and conciliates memory, prudence, and honest boldness, and frees the body from diseases and from poverty — who would not judge it worth seeking and searching out with all his strength? And rightly, indeed — were it not that, by reason of the greatest difficulties with which it is beset on every side, and its most uncertain end, it seems to be wholly abdicated and postponed to other things.

But what clear fame was ever gotten without labor and peril? What merchant ever became notably rich who committed nothing to fortune? As I judge this art to be commended to learned and philosophical men, leisured and rich in any case, so I judge it to be fled in every way by the unlearned and those oppressed by the mass of business cares, and men of middling fortune — nay, by all others who are borne to it rather by the desire of making money than of knowing. For unless they be fit by nature for attaining this science, they will frustrate their pains. Which Alphidius seems to assert — not one of the meaner professors of this science — in these words: "Son, I have written thee a book; if thou understandest its words, thou wilt find this art — if yet thou be of those who shall find it."

Wherefore, most illustrious and most reverend Prelate, since by a natural inclination of mind (which is, as it were, proper to the most ample house of Bavaria) Your Highness has ever cultivated this science, loved it, and with the utmost zeal taken care to have it searched out — nay, has published at your expense, for the phrase of Paracelsus(?), the works and colleges and gymnasia in divers cities, founded and richly endowed for the poor — first I pray God Almighty that He prosper your vows with happy success, to His glory and the consolation of your subjects. Then I humbly beg and beseech that you receive with cheerful brow these first-fruits of my studies, and this little work, such as it is, which I dedicate and devote to your Name, against the calumnies of detractors — which perhaps will seem small to some, but if they look within will be seen to have more of right(?) than the brow promises, especially if they consider how much more arduous and difficult it is to speak of the most abstruse mysteries of Nature, understood by so very few, than of vulgar things. As the Philosopher also says, in the tenth book *On Animals*, we are held by a greater desire to know a little of excellent and sublime things, though we know it topically(?), than to know much with certainty of less noble things. May God keep Your Highness safe and sound for very many years. Given at Cologne, 23 December, in the year of man's salvation 1594.

*Of Your Illustrious and Reverend Highness the most devoted client,*
*Theob. de Hoghelande*

## THE AUTHOR'S POEM ON ALCHEMY

Reader, I promise thee no hopes of Croesus, nor golden gifts;
I seek no man with painted words, but speak true things.
Chymistry, mindful of herself, with avenging(?) care pursues
the unjust: for she brings not gain but loss, and ruin of the thing,
and haste — too much — and never prepares(?)...
[The poem continues through its arc: the frauds who cloak themselves in her name; the miseries she inflicts on the unworthy — the fumes and vapors of quicksilver, coal, and sulphur that punish the body; those who dare with foul hand to violate Nature, the sacred parent, and deserve the...; the learned and pious, on the contrary, she embraces willingly, cherishes, shows open doors, leads by the hand into the sanctuary, displays the treasure laid up, and bids them take as much as they will, as the first pleasing fruits of their labors, which God and fate(?) grant to the deserving: if He does not let the foolish go unharmed in body and estate, nor the powerful rejoicing(?)... what the good man has earned, what chance might harm...]

*By study, labor, and patience.*

## TO THE BENEVOLENT READER, THE AUTHOR WISHES HEALTH

When I weighed with myself, candid reader, in how brief a moment the affairs of mortals turn — how he who is happy even to envy is presently dashed by adverse fortune, and he who is well today is borne off tomorrow — I could not refrain from publishing this little work, though not yet absolved in all its parts, but hasty and like an abortive birth: fearing lest, hindered by business, or weighed down by diseases, or forestalled by death, I should be by no means able to do it afterwards.

For to me this science has hitherto brought no profit — devoted to its study, in which I confess I have spent the better part of my life: first induced by the words of certain authors, especially Count Bernard, then confirmed by false(?) experiences. Now I labor to be as useful as possible to others, and I desire that all into whose hands this little treatise of ours shall come may be more prudent in choosing their course of life, nor knowingly dismiss themselves whence they see there is no exit. Although I never, from love of riches or ambition — which I can truly testify of myself — but only allured by the dignity of the art and its marvelous virtues, pursued it, and that I might have whence to search out Nature's arcana, to which I was borne by almost my whole natural inclination. Nor was the study alien to my manners, who by nature was quiet, hated forensic business and turmoils, loved tranquillity and solitude — especially since my means were rich enough that no more was to be wished for living a private life, had not by a certain fate, and our sins so deserving, the public turmoil of Belgium with my and many others' calamity been disturbed.

But if anyone wonders why at the very beginning of the book I strive to assert the truth of the art, when I ought rather to have called it into doubt, to deter others — since that seems to have been my purpose — let him know that my love of truth is greater than that I could or ought to hide it or wrap it in silence. And perhaps the same has befallen me, though in a disparate matter, as that Roman censor who, when he had held an oration before the rostra persuading the people that each should marry wives, was not ashamed to confess that there is something sweeter in bachelorhood. Then my mind was to show the difficulty of the art. Far be it from me to strive to obscure God's works and Nature's mighty powers, or — which is worse — to calumniate them, like those who, as if for themselves and their conscience, would call the science's witnesses(?)... For however much they may seem to the unskilled to have learned something, yet prudently(?) brought forth they show the weakness of their judgment — like that which befell Phormio the Greek rhetorician, who, when in the presence of Hannibal the great Carthaginian leader he declaimed on the officer's duty and the military art, and the rest of King Antiochus's satraps applauded and praised him in turn, being asked what he thought of him, Hannibal answered: he had seen many senseless old men, but none who doted more than Phormio.

But if anyone complains that I sometimes use words unheard and unusual, let him know that each art and science has its own fitting formulas and modes of speech. Since, then, I treat of Alchemy, the most arcane of natures, it was necessary to use even such words as the matter itself seemed to require. Elegance, indeed, and the charms which the schools of rhetoricians and orators require, I have not hunted after, nor does the matter(?) much exact it of me — about which I was not greatly solicitous, since it was more my care to inquire into the thing than into words, which under humble speech is often more august than under exquisite and over-elegant. Nor in this can I wholly degenerate from the writers of this science, by reading whom, made accustomed, I am sometimes compelled to imitate their modes of speech and not sufficiently Latin phrase. Wherefore, reader, whatever this is that I give, take in good part, enjoy, and farewell — until, with more leisure, I shall perhaps some time have polished these same things more accurately.

# PART THE FIRST

## PROOEMIUM

That the noblest part of natural philosophy is Alchemy — in which are treated the principles, causes, properties, passions, and transmutations of metals — no one will call into doubt. Add transmutation, lest I seem to speak only of that common part of physics which treats of prime matter and form, of the place, duration, color, taste, smell of generation, and the like: I mean the part which teaches how one metal may be turned into another. That this is possible needs no demonstration, since experience gives it faith. Take the Pontic earth, calcine it, and make — even by artifice — ... [a passage of examples follows, badly mangled in the scan's reading: quicksilver mixed with sulphur; witnesses beyond Gaul, England, Germany, Bohemia, and Belgium; quicksilver driven out by fire returning silver or gold, to be put to every assay]. Cardan tells that an apothecary of Tarvisium sold quicksilver, in subtleness, turned into gold. Agrippa says thy spirit (?) ... [mangled] ... to purify imperfect metals. In the addition to the ... Ioannes Andreas (?) of his time there was at Rome Arnald of Villanova, and he made certain golden rods which he permitted ... [mangled]. But what marvel? Metals in themselves ... [mangled: the argument is that metals differ only in accidents — place and decoction — not in matter and essence]. So marble ... [mangled] ... of animals among rocks and stones, and of natural waters flowing spontaneously, and of the sealing(?) virtue of the stone. But what stone is proved ... [mangled]. I shall bring forward only a certain relation: Albertus Magnus tells that in his time, in the Danish(?) city of Lubeck, there was found a great tree, and in it gold dust which, together with the tree's branch, had been turned into stone. And the Emperor Frederick, when he had heard of a ... to whom all things are turned into stone, caused a sealed coffer to be ... when half had been sunk in a fountain ... the other half ... near Mount Hecla [mangled]. And Georgius Agricola, on the nature of those things which flow spontaneously from the earth, where there are more examples of these things; and in the same(?) book, on the nature of fossils.

But that the transmutation of metals has been admitted by some, the testimony and consent of most famous men convinces; though that it can be done with profit and increase, they deny(?). To these I think answer must be made in this way. If in games, where the contest is of ... or fame or esteem is at stake, two or three witnesses are lightly(?) to be believed as if divine(?) ... how much more in the chymical art, which is proved true by reasons, similitudes, examples, and witnesses not three or four but almost innumerable. For we have the writings of most learned and most upright men — St. Thomas Aquinas, Hermes Trismegistus, Raymond Lull, Ioannes de Rupescissa, Marsilio Ficino, Arnald of Villanova (whom Ioannes Andreas calls the theologian, the supreme physician, and the alchemist), Rhases besides, and Avicenna, and many others — who, being greater than all exception, testify to the truth of the art. Why should faith be withdrawn from men so renowned, and belief given rather to certain others alone, who with perverse judgment cavil at the art as vain and adulterate — and say it was handed down enigmatic and obscure for no other cause than that the wits of all men might be exercised? Far be it from us to think that those venerable philosophers of ancient authority deliberately committed anything false to memory — they who ever labored with unwearied neck in inquiring out the truth, and ... to posterity ... [mangled] ... hard and sublime ... to be handed down. Add also the consent of divers nations: for not of the Latins only, but of the Greeks, Egyptians, Arabs, Chaldeans, Gauls, Germans, Spaniards, and English, concerning the metamorphosis of metals, ... daily(?) ... among men ... who all, as with one mouth (though with dissonant tongues), proclaim the art most true — than which, after the divine ... nothing ... was more lofty or sublime [mangled]. There are, perhaps, in this ... those who contradict ... who, when the very ... chymist ... boasts himself to have gotten the science. But of the wise and cautious ... Abuses ... [mangled]. For ... he operates with undue matter ... What error, then, marvel if they be frustrated of the fruit? Wherefore ... Avicenna ... [mangled: most seekers are plainly blind, not understanding even common healthy sense, much less metaphors and allegories; though learned and perspicacious, they either neglect them, or cannot expend the price through avarice, or cannot through poverty, or cannot get an exemplar, or are kept from pilgrimage in the places where they dwell — as those who have gotten books and God's grace remember Solomon's saying ...]. Nor are there lacking causes why the wise do not manifest themselves. But if those things are to be denied which rarely or never happen, all faith, as much sacred as profane, will be minished. Who would believe the rain of sulphur and flame from heaven upon Sodom and Gomorrah? Who the strength of Samson, and other almost incredible things, whose examples are told among fables? Who, running through the Annals of Cornelius Tacitus, would not burst into laughter when he reads that the burning of Cologne was restrained not by water but by vinegar, the coverings torn from the body? Does he not seem to tell fables? And yet we can take and believe all that. But in metals, the crasser matter — which, if at some time it be rightly separated and kept in its faithfulness beyond all false supposition — might generate. Who yields not to the consent of so many men and so many nations will confess himself conquered, not even by experience itself, but will ascribe more to the Devil and the trifles of future magicians than to God and Nature's power in effecting transmutation.

Someone — a noble man and not unlettered — in familiar talk, when he disparaged the chymical art as vain, was asked whether he believed it possible. He denied it, before him who gave proof of the art itself by experiment. But what is to be done with such perverse men, who admire only what they know, nor admit their fault — through slowness or through neglect of what they read — laying the blame on the philosophers and the art? But what marvel if all men despise what they are ignorant of? What is more rash than that the unskilled should pass judgment on a thing? As Cato(?) not vainly exclaimed: "To the unskilled man never anything well"; "who thinks nothing right but what he himself has made."

To these is joined another kind of men: those, namely, who, cheated once or twice by some gilded(?) ... desiring riches ... condemn the art rather than the impostor — dogs in nature, who, when they lack the thing, dash against the stone that was thrown, omitting him who threw it. But if anyone look more deeply into the cause of calumny, he cannot marvel enough at the arrogance and tyranny of many wits, who are moved by this sole name — that the art is various and not plain, as may appear from the best chymical authors handed down — as if they themselves were not takers(?) of so many mysteries, which are revealed to very few, and at times appointed by God; since otherwise, with most obscure force of wit, they could weigh and penetrate them: as if God had given all things to them, and nothing lay hid in the nature of things beyond what they know, and nothing could be done besides what they do. There are also those who, led by a certain desire of glory, disparage the art and openly tear it with contumelies, that they may seem to know a little more, and not to be infamous with the crowd. With the same kind of disease they assert that to labor foolishly and solidly, searching out Nature's arcana, is nothing — hoping their plants may thus be in esteem. Nor is it to be marveled at if St. Thomas, Lull, and others excelling in doctrine and wit neglected to defend the art, since men's doubt turned to profit rather than to loss for them and theirs. For by this reason God providently provides for the security of the workmen legitimately, and teaches them to take no mediocre profit from their enemies. Nor is it to be thought that words or reasons failed them; but perchance they could not, unless Nature's arcana were published — which, if they were made vulgar, not so certain would the mathematical axiom be as the demonstration of this opinion. For almost all the arguments brought against the art proceed from nowhere else than from ignorance of those things which are in the art. So of substantial forms, of the proportion of mixture, of the vessel of Nature and of art, many speak, and disputing of unknowns conclude unknowns. Wherefore, omitting all these, I proceed to the proposition and scope of my undertaking.

## WHAT THE PHILOSOPHERS' STONE IS

The intention of the philosophers of this art is: by a certain mineral virtue, from the purest seed of gold and silver, artificially — yet by Nature's benefit and help — to produce, in the form of a most subtle powder, white or red, which may propagate itself to infinity, and have the power of penetrating quicksilver and the other vile and impure metals almost in a moment, and of cleansing them from all impurities, and of bringing them to the state of the more perfect — namely gold and silver; and of restoring the infirm human body, and recalling it to its true temperament, or preserving the sound, in health, unto the term prefixed by God. This powder, endowed with such and so great virtue, the Arabs call Elixir; the Latins call it Medicine and Stone. But I would warn the younger that the name of Stone spreads wide and is used diversely. For sometimes the rude matter of the art, which Nature supplies to us, is called Stone. Whence Arnald and Lull: "Although our stone naturally contains in itself ..." [mangled]. Of disputing of unknowns ... [badly mangled marginalia and text here; the sense resumes below].

From the stone comes quicksilver; from it water is extracted, which is called quicksilver. Likewise the principles constituting the Elixir are called stones, which are three in number: namely quicksilver, sulphur, and tincture — or Body, Spirit, and Soul; or Earth, Water, and Fire — and from the first two results the fourth, which is Air. For by these names the wise have endowed this art. Of quicksilver is to be understood that of Avicenna: "The third degree of the work is the reduction of the stone into natural earth" — which Arnald expounds: "The third regimen is to lead the humid water over the dry earth." Of sulphur, this of Arnald: "Fire comminutes the grossness of the stone by calcination." Of tincture, this of Marsilio Ficino: "What the philosophers seek as stone is a spirit invisible and impalpable: it is tincture, and a tinging spirit, which another visible and palpable spirit has hidden in its inmost bowels." The stone is also called the philosophers' matter, perfectly cleansed from all superfluous filth, and sublimely elevated, now fit to receive form. The exalted stone must be ground upon marble. The stone is also called the first part of the whole work, which is occupied about the division of the elements and their depuration. Whence Lull: "The generation both of the stone and [of the elixir] is and is completed by reason of similitude and subtlety of parts among themselves." That I may sum up in one word: in whatever degree of the work ... [mangled] ... stones are called; nay, to other things wholly extraneous to the philosophical work the appellation of stone is transferred. These things I wished to premise in this place, as foundations of things to be said, without whose knowledge the following can scarcely be understood, since I must use the voice of "stone" oftener.

But why the perfect powder or Elixir, or its matter, is called Stone, I find chiefly these reasons among the philosophers, whose monuments have come into my hands.

**First reason** (from the author of *De Universali*, ch. 10): The philosophers call "stone" everything from which the elements can be separated by artifice; for when separation is made from them, through their conjunction in this mastery there comes to be a certain substance after the manner of stones, which through the coming of the humid with the dry is generated.

**Second:** The philosophers' compound is called Stone because the things of which it is compounded have similitude to stones, and from them is created the blessed stone. And Arnald: "The stone [is so called] in similitude and touch, not in nature." And it is called stone, or a certain compound. The author of the *Speculum*: "This matter I call stone, which long retains whiteness, and also, having taken redness, through long time stands hard, and returns to the specimen of strong mixture, until by continued decoction it perfects itself." And in the *Turba*: "The philosophers do not follow(?) with the first ... until it become stone." Haly: "There are stones which melt and run without evaporation." A fourth reason Rhases renders, if I remember well: that the philosophers' stone is called everything which does not evaporate from fire.

A fifth could perhaps also be given, because of the lime which the philosophers require in their work, since lime is for the most part fixed stone. Whence in the *Allegories of the Wise*: "Lime is stone incombustible in fire, and therefore their lime is body mixed with spirit." Or because all metals are generated in stones almost, and the Elixir must necessarily be made from metals, if metals are to be converted. Or because of the mineral salt — whence some: "The salt of metals is the philosophers' stone." But Lull calls salt "stone" because salt is likened to a stone abiding in its seat. But there may lurk another hidden cause why this medicine is called stone, which the philosophers conceal — though the first, by the consent of the most, is the likelier.

This philosophers' stone, then — or Elixir, or most subtle powder — not because it is by its nature impalpable and invisible, but because, as a spirit, it most swiftly pervades and penetrates bodies, both metallic and human ... [a mangled passage follows, on men of every kind and condition — noble, ignoble, learned, unlearned, rich, poor — panting after it; the text here defeats certain reading].

## THAT THE ART IS NOT EASY, AGAINST THE OPINION OF MANY

But a certain opinion of many has invaded me: that the philosophers' work is altogether easy — to prepare light works, and by fortune and chance to hit it, if the will of inquiring be not lacking. For it is the gift of God; He lavishes it on whom He will; and so to me and to those like me the art may be open no less than to others more learned. Whence what is more shameless than for rude and unskilled men to prattle of the philosophers' arcanum, and boldly promise themselves that fortune which not even the most learned men and most watchful could hope for — unless from God's mercy alone? This error is most to be detested. It draws its origin partly from the writings of certain philosophers, partly also from the deceits of false swearers. For in the *Turba* of the philosophers the work is called women's work and children's play. And Rosinus, citing Democritus: "Our work is light," he says, "needs moderate expense." In the *Mirror* ascribed to Albertus Magnus: "I intend to write the true and infallible art." And the vulgar verse: "This art is dear, brief, apt(?), light, and rare." Also Count Bernard of Treves, who is handled today in the hands of all, and who might seem with a certain zeal of charity to have written his book on the metamorphosis of metals — lest it cost the more skilled — he himself handed down the science, asserting the art to be so easy that, if it were shown in act or in practice itself, it would scarcely find faith.

When I was yet a young man, acting(?) at Artois, I remember a gardener ... [here follows, badly mangled in the reading, a gardener's tale: daily ... with herbs ... in a month ... by chance ... watered ... the same place ... against(?) lead it flowed down and was turned into gold, by the undoubted virtue of a certain herb ... which afterwards, the cinders removed ...]. It is a fable [Hoghelande's verdict]. Would that those who prate of such things had in some part betrayed Nature's arcana, or judged them — rather they have rendered them to the impostures of most false men. They should have thought that from man is generated man, from lion lion, from plant plant — that everything has innate in itself its like ... [mangled] ... how a plant is generated ... Neither were there lacking, nor are there lacking today, those who seek the so great arcanum of the philosophers both in animals and in vegetables, and try whatever is obvious to them — which they have found either in books ill understood, or composed by the fraud and avarice of impostors — hoping by chance to find what the philosophers with utmost labor and zeal in common seek, and deem to be obtained from God by prayers and perils.

Some bring salamanders alive in oil, diligently shut up, to be concocted and burned — vowing they will get gold thence, whence rather they ought to fear death — since they have heard that it succeeded happily with others, or dreaming that the philosophers call "stone" what is perfected by fire, by the name of salamander; when yet the naming is twofold. For the similitude is drawn from the salamander, which the ancients believed to live in fire: in which the body, spotted with variety of colors, has the likeness of the stone, which is changed from color to color, and sometimes shows more colors at one time.

But that I may in some part reply to the above alleged, I say: ... [the Latin here is corrupt in the reading; the argument is:] the philosophers call the art easy only simply(?) — [the "women's work and children's play" follows upon the due disposition of the matter; as the priest says(?): "I will now demonstrate to you the disposition of white lead," after which nothing follows but women's work and children's play]. But how great the difficulty is in separating, purifying, and conjoining the elements of the stone, the books of the philosophers and experience faithfully interpret. "The stone known, and the operation, is nothing else ..." [mangled]. And certainly to the faithful and expert they grant the stone to be made light ... [mangled]. Why does Bernard assert the art to be easy? We may presume him to speak of the latter part of the work, as above ... in the *Turba*: "first, with the whole ... the common difficulty" ... or very sparingly; but his parable embraces only so much decoction. Or he could call it easy to himself, after so many expenses ill bestowed and forty years' labors — in the space of two years afterwards, from the reading of books, he got the science by divine favor, which for so many years he had tried in vain before — yet it had not been so easy to him as he could afterwards relate, seeing that, before he wrote to others, he confessed that he had [but] moved his hand to the work.

But the art can be called light in respect of other operative arts, which require many and various instruments, and bodily exercise and supreme labor; since this requires one matter alone, one vessel, one furnace, with fitting fire. Then through the whole life it is exercised ... [mangled] ... once perfectly found ... the philosopher needs it no more, since the worker lives long and ... [mangled]. But who would dare deny that the philosophers put forth such things — as many others — to trip(?) and to deceive the prudent? But I would that those who think this art easy would recall to mind that adage: "Difficult are the things which are beautiful." Likewise that of Robert of Chester(?): "In those things in which God has placed great virtue, in the same He has placed great difficulty." Would that they knew that the philosophers' stone is called not of rustics or of the unskilled — to which by its name alone anyone not unskilled could be allured. Nay, consult the truth — Bottus(?) the Ferrarese — and he will answer to this question of the art: the truth and experience, stronger than the inquisition of all other things, more difficult and more occult, and of longer time. Which, since it is most true, it behooves each to examine himself, and show himself the judge of his virtues and his goods, before he tries to enter this science, and diligently weigh whether he be such as the philosophers require the disciple to be — which are many indeed, and far otherwise than the vulgar opinion ... [mangled] ... what the region promises ... [mangled] ... than the apparent species, from the true ... are false. Whence Geber: "We allure only the prudent to the art." The same: "We shall hand down the art in speech which it will not befall the prudent to be ignorant of" ... [mangled] ... Morienus: "Provide that expedite things be applied by the craftsman." ... [mangled: what is not gotten through natural wit, let it be helped]. Hence: "We have hidden this thing ... so that it cannot be apprehended."

## WHAT THE DISCIPLE MUST BRING

Above all, let him be skilled in the Latin tongue: for how otherwise shall he understand the things which for the greatest part are written and published in Latin speech? For no one thinks chymical books can with such facility or truth be carried into another tongue as the books of other sciences. In natural philosophy and others ... [mangled]. Whence Arnald: ... let him suspend number(?) from the work. Likewise the foundation of this work is the patience of delay. Let the craftsman be his own master and free. Arnald: three things are to be inquired: the subtle wit of the craftsman, the operation of the hands, and the free power of choice. Let him be leisured, nor occupied with other cares or business, but intent on the work alone — for this art requires the whole man. Whence Geber: "There are many curious, detained by divers cares and solicitudes of this world, mixing themselves in every business, from whom this science flees." Likewise let him be tenacious of secrecy, lest he draw peril on himself and give occasion to others of evil doing. Whence in the *Allegories*: "Conceal this work from all, as the word in the tongue, as fire in thine eye. Speak not to thyself, lest the wind bear thy words to another, and they turn to thy loss. Behold, I have made thee beware."

And it behooves the craftsman to track out this science with subtle wit, and cautiously and prudently to use it, in place and time, in his necessities: for he who carries treasure publicly is plundered. And Aristotle: "Divine providence help thee to conceal the purpose." From the beginning ... [mangled: against the works of the sophists]. Geber: ... to the sophistical ... to find ... the goal of the sophistical work, but to the completion of the true alone be intent. For perchance God, from vengeance of the sophistical work, would deny thee the art, and cruelly thrust thee into the byway of error, and from error into perpetual infelicity and misery. Let him not spend his money beforehand in hope of future things, lest perchance, not finding it, he be left in misery — or lest perchance, when he has now approximated to the end by superfluous inquisition of this mastery, his expenses be consumed, and he be compelled to leave the true end for poverty. Nor let him be avaricious ... in his studies and labors ... [mangled]. Let him be wary in knowing and preventing the diabolical illusions, which he mingles with chymical operations, that, holding the workers about vain and useless things, he may detain them from the works of Nature — as happened to a certain friend of mine, as I shall tell below. Let him be sufficiently rich: for it is not enough to be witty, learned, industrious ... if pressing poverty compels him to postpone [the art] ... to be seen by nobles, and to be secure of friends. Whence Lull: "This art requires riches, wisdom, and books." And the Philosopher: "It is impossible for the needy to philosophize." Although to those seeking at vile price the philosophers ... can come to the art. For Arnald says: ... the medicines of this art do not exceed fifty silver pieces. And Alias: "The expenses of our art do not exceed the price of two gold florins in its first purchase" — yet it necessarily befalls the worker to err often, nor is it learned except by error, as is said in the *Turba*. And Alphidius: "He who errs not arrives not"; and "he who corrupts not amends not" ... [mangled]. Let him not be made sad ... [but] begin ... and be able to repeat the work, as is needful. For thus the author of *De perfecto magisterio*: "When thou canst not be secure of the experience of this thing, I persuade thee ... [mangled]."

## OF BOOKS — AND OF FALSE BOOKS

Let the craftsman have a store of books. It is the opinion that the philosophers handed down the science sparingly and by parts. ... [The Latin of this chapter is much mangled in the reading; the following renders what could be recovered, in order:]

The philosophers teach the science scattered and by parts. Hence it commonly happens that tyros of this art take false and adulterate books, empty of all admiration(?) and doctrine, for true and good ones — as(?) ... [mangled]. I know excellent and prudent men in ... of whom one bought at great price the manuscript *Practica Philippi de Rouillasto Pedemontani*, not yet printed; the other [bought] a certain Io. Salinarius's(?) French [book] *On the Preparation of Salt* — each thought the true doctrine and practice of the art was contained in his booklet, nor did they desire anything but that some artist be given them, by whose work they might use [it] toward drawing so great a treasure from the books, since they, occupied with other business, could not or knew not how to apply their hands to the work. Who, though friendly warned by me that those booklets contained nothing of truth — that they should spare their money and make no expenses — yet afterwards, held back by false hope, did not obey sound counsel, but gave themselves to be deceived by others ... [mangled] ... the student ought to give years' spaces to reading authors ... with readings(?) and transpositions. But he who bows his back to reading our books, and studiously ... applies his mind, nor is implicated in vain cogitations ... as Solomon for wisdom, for riches ... shall reign without end(?), until he die. And the author of *De perfecto magisterio*: "I pray thee, son, that thou search the philosophers' books in ... reading; for he who desists from reading books will be prompt in preparing things" ... [mangled] ... one may ... in practice ... another's mind in the theory of the thing is to be ... For he will come to operation safer and quicker, in whose mind the images of operations manifoldly result.

And Richardus Anglicus: "Study removes ignorance, and reduces the human intellect to true science and knowledge of every thing. Therefore all lovers of this art's benefit must insist on studies, and draw truth from books, not from feigned fables and faulty works; since this art is not truly found except after the term of study, and knowledge of the philosophers' sayings." The same, in short, affirm all the wise of this art — so that I am superfluous in bringing authorities. After some years' studies, however happy, long experience is still desired; for the foundation of the art is experiment. Whence Geber: "One must come to the inward by working, searching, and experiencing." The same: "Let the craftsman exercise himself, until by studying and experiencing, with insistence of labor, he come to knowledge." And Lull: "Woe to you, doctrine, sons(?)" ... ## PRIMACY NOTE

No complete English translation of Hoghelande's *De alchemiae difficultatibus* (1594) — in whole or of its First Part — was found after research. The work is treated in bibliographies (Ferguson's *Bibliotheca Chemica*) and named in histories of alchemy, but no English rendering is known to the series. This is stated as a research finding — "none found after research" — never as a claim of being first, oldest, or only. Note also: Adam McLean's 2025 print-on-demand English of Maier's *Arcana Arcanissima* Book II (a different work, shelved in this same row) is in copyright and was not used or consulted.
