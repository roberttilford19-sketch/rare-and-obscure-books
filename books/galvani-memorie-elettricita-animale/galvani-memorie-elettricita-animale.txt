DEDICATION

Who he was. Luigi Galvani was a physician and anatomist of Bologna. He took his degree in medicine and philosophy at the University of Bologna on 15 July 1759, having studied under Jacopo Bartolomeo Beccari and Domenico Galeazzi. He rose slowly through the university he never left: honorary lecturer, then paid lecturer (22 June 1768); curator and demonstrator of the anatomical museum (1766); adjunct to Galeazzi in anatomy (12 December 1775); and, on 26 February 1782, professor of obstetric arts at the Institute of Sciences. His early work was solid, unglamorous anatomy — the structure and diseases of bones (1762), the kidneys and ureters of birds (1767), the ear of birds. Then, from late 1780, he began the experiments on frogs that made his name a verb.

His home and circumstances. Galvani lived his whole life in Bologna. He married Lucia Galeazzi, the daughter of his teacher Domenico; she died in 1790, after some thirty years of marriage. In his last decade he turned, by his own account and by the testimony of those who knew him, more and more to religion; after Lucia's death he had joined the Third Order of the Franciscans. He was, the old biographies agree, a man who shrank from controversy and left the public fighting largely to his nephew Giovanni Aldini.

His death. In April 1798 the Cisalpine Republic — Napoleon's client state in northern Italy — required every professor to swear allegiance to the new order. Galvani refused, on religious and political grounds, and on 20 April 1798 he gave up his chair rather than take the oath. He was stripped of his offices and his salary, and withdrew to the house of his brother Giacomo — the family house, the house where he had been born. His friends obtained an exemption and his appointment as professor emeritus on the strength of his fame, but the decree came too late. He died at Bologna on 4 December 1798, aged sixty-one, in what the Dictionary of Scientific Biography calls poverty and sorrow, and was buried after a modest funeral in the church of the Corpus Domini, beside Lucia.

Why this work is vital — to prove his logic. The Memorie sulla elettricità animale of 1797, addressed to the great naturalist Lazzaro Spallanzani of Pavia, was Galvani's last published word in the quarrel with Alessandro Volta. Volta had argued that the frog's kick came from the metals — that dissimilar metals, merely by touching, unbalance the common electric fluid and sting the nerve into moving the muscle. If Volta was right, there was no animal electricity at all; the frog was only a very sensitive detector of other people's electricity. Galvani's answer, built experiment by experiment in these memoirs, is a chain of exclusions. Contractions without any metal. Contractions without any stimulus — the nerve itself never touched, only a strip of the animal's own flesh let fall upon another strip. Contractions with nerve laid upon nerve, so that no difference of substance can be blamed. What survives every exclusion is his conclusion: there is a circuit of electricity intrinsic to the animal, naturally out of balance in it, and the muscle is its reservoir — his famous comparison is to a Leyden jar, charged within, waiting for the arc to let it discharge. History's verdict, delivered decades later, was that both men held a piece of the truth: Volta's contact electricity built the battery, and Galvani's animal electricity — mocked, shelved, then measured at last by Emil du Bois-Reymond in the 1840s — became electrophysiology. But the logic is Galvani's, and it is proved here the way he proved everything: not by assertion, but by closing, one at a time, every other door the fact could have come through. Read him doing it. That is the vital thing — not that he was right, but how he made sure.

His relation to this series. The Boneyard exists for works that were left in the in-between — unfinished, untranslated, or written by people the world stopped listening to before they were done. Galvani wrote these memoirs in 1797, a widower already marked for ruin, one year from losing his chair, his income, and his life for refusing an oath. They are a last testament: a man with nothing left to gain, still doing the experiments properly. No English edition of them has been found after the research recorded in this edition's notes. So here they are, resurrected and carried across — because he proved his logic, and then the world filed him under somebody else's name for the battery.

— The Editor

THE MEMOIRS ON ANIMAL ELECTRICITY

Memoir the First

[1797, p. 1]

I read with the greatest pleasure, in the last letter with which you honoured me, the experiments made by you on the respiration of bats, and transmitted to your celebrated friend Monsieur Senebier; and since you wish to have my opinion of them, whatever it may be worth, I will tell you with all frankness that they seem to me very fine, and that they open a vast field of new knowledge — not only concerning the lethargy into which bats and other similar warm-blooded animals fall, but concerning the most important operation of respiration itself, which, although in our days it is placed in much greater light than in past times, is nevertheless still wrapped in much darkness and obscurity. As for the explanation you have ingeniously given of the phenomena of so easily falling into lethargy and of awakening in these animals, I am of the opinion that you have hit the mark; it seems to me precisely that everything is to be attributed partly to the free will of the animal, and partly to a particular structure of the organs of respiration. I therefore urge you to continue these fine experiments, and to fill, in order to be still more certain of the matter, the role of anatomist as well. Here, in brief, is my opinion, such as it is.

I pass now to beg of you a new favour, and it is this: that you will give me your impartial judgment concerning certain reflections of mine, and certain new attempts on that electricity which I have called — following not a few illustrious philosophers — animal. Since you have deigned to oblige me in this many times before, both by letter and by word of mouth, I dare hope that you will do so now as well; indeed, I would almost say that I may now expect it of your kindness as of right, in a kind of exchange for the liberty with which I have given you my opinion above. Do it, then, also for our friendship, and for the advantage of animal physics, which you have so much enriched and illustrated.

My considerations and my experiments turn particularly upon the opinion of the most celebrated Signor Volta concerning animal electricity; and in disputing that opinion, the abundance of the matter has carried me further than I intended, so that for love of clarity and order I have thought to divide this writing of mine into several memoirs, all of which I address to you, and submit to your fine discernment. You know the learning and the penetrating intellect of this illustrious philosopher; you know the many most beautiful and most delicate experiments attempted by him; and you know also how these tend to destroy animal electricity of this kind altogether, and to establish the animal machine, in respect of electricity, as nothing more than any simple moist body whatever — wholly the opposite of what I have believed, and have striven to demonstrate with the labours and travails of many years.

With new considerations and new attempts I have sought to sustain this endowment of the animal machine against the strong and most ingenious objections of the aforesaid author; but I do not know whether I have succeeded so far. In such doubt, allow me, then, to set briefly before you, at a single glance, the weighty arguments and the most beautiful experiments which Signor Volta, in many published dissertations, has proposed in favour of his opinion.

He admits, then, in his first published memoirs, that the principle which, by means of the metallic conducting arc and the metallic armatures invented by me, is put into action and excites the contractions and motions of the muscles, is really the electric fluid — as I judged, and published first. But in this he then differs from me: that where I conjectured it a particular fluid, proper to the animal, he, on the contrary, believes it the one common and universal fluid diffused among bodies, which, set circulating by the metals, if it encounters some nerve, strikes and irritates it, and thus gives it the most efficacious stimulus of any hitherto known, exciting the nervous force, by which the muscle contracts. Now since, by the demonstrated laws of the equilibrium of electricity, such a fluid is not set in motion — still less in a circle — except when it is out of balance, he, in order to establish this imbalance, does not admit a particular machine in the animal, as I suppose, but has recourse to the difference of the metals, which unbalance it; and this difference he places first in the diverse nature and substance of the metals used for such experiments, then in their diverse polish, size, and so on, and finally in their different placement upon the parts of the animal. From which you may clearly see that we both agree on the existence of electricity in animals, and on the disequilibrium, or imbalance, of the same; but we then differ as to the character it shows, and as to the cause of that disequilibrium.

He holds the electricity to be the same as that common to all bodies; I, particular and proper to the animal. He places the cause of the imbalance in the contrivances employed, and particularly in the difference of the metals; I, in the animal machine. He makes that cause accidental and extrinsic; I, natural and internal. He, in short, attributes everything to the metals, nothing to the animal; I, everything to the animal, nothing to the metals — so far, that is, as the mere imbalance is concerned.

Now allow me to examine the matter part by part; and first, beginning with the nature of the electricity. How can the electricity that we observe in animals put to the test be called pure and simple common electricity, if characters so different from it are manifested in it? When does common electricity, however weak, not act at some small distance? When does it not traverse a stratum of air, or of the vacuum of the pneumatic machine? When does it not pass through a continuous arc, if that arc is formed of a single wire of metal, or of a few pieces joined together in the manner of a chain? And yet my experiments, and those made also by you on the electricity of the torpedo — an electricity that has so much in common with the one we are treating — clearly demonstrate the existence of these characters in animal electricity, which are wanting in the common kind. Moreover, common electricity set out of balance — is it not restored at once to equilibrium, and does it not cease to give sign of itself, once the bodies are immersed in water, or placed in long contact with conducting substances? And yet nothing of the sort happens in animal electricity: for although the animals with their armatures are placed under water, and consequently room is first given to the imbalance caused by them (as Signor Volta opines), and then to the restoration of equilibrium through the long stay in the water, nevertheless, upon application of the arc, the contractions arise again and again. Finally, why does animal electricity not attract light bodies in the manner in which they are attracted by the common kind, however minimal that may be? Perhaps I shall be answered that the degree of animal electricity is an infinitesimal in respect of any minimal and sensible degree of the common kind set out of balance in other bodies; but to believe this, one must do oneself great violence, when one reflects how it can ever happen that, for example in the leg of an ox, the armatures, however large one may suppose them, should unbalance only a minimal part of the electricity, incapable of giving signs of itself — and yet this same minimal part should be able to excite in that same leg most violent contractions and motions.

Furthermore, if this electricity is common, why — adding to the animals, or to the metals, or to the arcs applied to them, some of that same electricity, in as great a quantity as one wishes; or stripping these bodies of the electricity they contained; or otherwise artificially unbalancing it — why is there never obtained the least difference of effect in the vivacity and readiness of the contractions, as was demonstrated in the anonymous little work entitled On the Use and Activity of the Conducting Arcs? From all this I seem to myself not to err if I conclude two things. The first: that the electricity in question is not absolutely the simple common electricity, such as is found in other bodies, but rather that same electricity modified and combined with some animal principle, by which it acquires the aforesaid characters, and others still its own. The second: that such electricity is much more similar to that of the gymnotus, or Surinam eel, and of the torpedo, than to the common kind — as I noted in my Commentary De viribus electricitatis — since it has, according to the observations of so many learned men, and most particularly of yourself, all the characters set out a little above, wholly dissimilar from those of the common kind. And if in these animals it rises so far as to give the shock, this signifies nothing else than a greater quantity of it accumulated, and perhaps modified in them, by means of a particular machine. Hence one might compare this electricity to the stormy and lightning-bearing electricity, and that of other animals to the calm electricity of the atmosphere — as Signor Volta himself most elegantly wrote in that dissertation of his in which he honoured this discovery of mine with so gracious a comparison, and accompanied it with praises it does not deserve, which he then all at once took back in other later memoirs of his.

But let us pass to speaking of Signor Volta's other opinion, concerning the cause of the imbalance, which he places in the metals. Every time that I have demonstrated — as I believe I have done — that the electricity found by me has much resemblance to that of the torpedo, and that the circle it makes in animals put to the test is likewise similar, I should think I had done enough to demonstrate and convince anyone that the imbalance does not depend on the metals, but on the animal. For in the torpedo no one can deny the circle of the electricity in the one simple animal, naturally so constituted, without any exterior metal; and since, according to the known laws of electricity, no circle is made without imbalance, who will be able to deny that this imbalance arises from the particular organization of these animals? And who, consequently, by force of analogy, will refuse to grant the same in the other animals, in which I, and so many others, have artificially shown this circle? But of the torpedo — from which I draw arguments in confirmation of my opinion on animal electricity — I shall speak more diffusely in another memoir.

For the present, however, I do not wish to rest my reasons on the argument from analogy alone; and since Signor Volta makes use of experiments to demonstrate the truth of his theory and the falsity of mine, it is right that I keep to the same path. He courteously grants me, then, that whenever the muscular motions obtained without any metal — reported in the little work lately come to light — were constant and violent, like those excited by metals, and did not depend on any other extrinsic cause, and particularly on a stimulus which the nerves suffer, in his view, in coming into contact with the muscles (strengthened also by the acceleration of motion induced in the nerves by the attraction of moisture) — he grants me, I said, that then such motions would depend on the electricity naturally unbalanced in the animal, and that the consequences deduced by the author of the anonymous booklet in favour of the animal machine would be just, and would stand. [1797, p. 6]

Let us therefore set ourselves to examine both of these two most interesting points. And beginning with the first: I can assure you that not a few times, as he asserts, but in many, very many experiments, I have succeeded in obtaining such motions, almost always — so that out of a hundred times, in only a few has the effect been wanting. And these experiments have been tried publicly in our Institute of Sciences, in the presence of many learned scholars and other persons well versed in this kind of thing, nor did they ever go awry. For greater confirmation of the truth, I have arranged for various illustrious friends of mine to lend their attention, and their assistance, to these same experiments — among whom I think it enough to name the most learned Secretary of our Academy, Sebastiano Canterzani, to whom I have never failed to show every experiment of mine, and to communicate every conjecture of mine before publishing it — although before every other I could name you, who, as in every kind of science, so in the difficult art of experimenting, are an excellent and solemn master, and whom my good fortune willed that I should have not only as witness, but as approving judge, of this very experiment. It seems, therefore, that from the constancy of the outcome — where the experiment is executed with the due cautions — every doubt is removed. And I flatter myself that Signor Volta, accurate philosopher that he is, will not cast doubt on the truth of a fact, and of an experiment, merely because sometimes it does not succeed; otherwise no fact deduced from experiment could ever be admitted as true in physics. He knows better than I that the minute circumstances which concur in the good outcome of an experiment are so many, and so often unknown, that it is altogether too easy for some one of them to be wanting, though every diligence be used; and that therefore the phenomenon, ever constant in itself, is yet sometimes desired in vain.

He adds to me further that such motions are not violent, nor ever comparable to those excited by metals. I answer that they are nevertheless such as to set the whole leg of the frog in motion, and to make it rise not a little; and that if they are not large and strong to the degree of those induced by metals, this still does not prove that they cannot be had without metals. The presence of the effect is what decides the presence of the cause; the quantity of the effect decides, rather, its particular intensity and vigour. And indeed, if we let ourselves be led by the mere quantity of the effect in establishing the cause, how often would we not go wrong? A clear example, and most apt to our case, is furnished by the Leyden jar. If, from having a very strong discharge when that jar is armed with metal and a metallic conducting arc is applied to it, and on the contrary a most feeble and almost null discharge when it is not armed and an arc of green or wetted wood is adapted to it, anyone were to draw the consequence that the metals are the cause of the imbalance of the electricity and of the phenomenon — and not rather the diverse charge of the surfaces, and the construction and nature of the jar itself — how far would he not go from the truth? For all physicists agree that metals, attracting by their nature, and better conducting, the electric fluid already unbalanced in the jar, do indeed increase its force, but do not themselves produce the imbalance. The same, I imagine, happens in our case: the metals strengthen the force of the animal electricity, naturally unbalanced; they do not unbalance it.

But let us pass to the second objection, which is as much subtler and more ingenious than the first as it is stronger and more seductive — to such a degree that without new experiments it could perhaps not be wholly destroyed. And here allow me, for the greater clarity of what I am about to set forth, to place the experiment in question again before your eyes.

[1797, p. 8] The frog being prepared in the usual way, the sciatic nerves are cut as close as possible to the vertebral canal; then the frog is taken and held in the air by one of the thighs, either with the naked hand or by means of some insulating body — which comes to the same thing. The sciatic nerve of the free, hanging thigh is then applied upon that little portion of cut bone which in frogs corresponds to the innominate bone of quadrupeds, using all the cautions set out in the anonymous little work, and taking particular care that the aforesaid little bone remain free and clean of every muscular part. Things being thus arranged, one seeks to make the nerve, resting upon the little bone as upon a point of support, hang free, and at some distance from the lateral muscular part of its thigh. The aforesaid nerve, so placed and sustained, will then form a kind of arc, of which one extremity is already naturally applied and inserted within the muscles, while the other waits to come into contact with the external part. Let the thigh by which the animal is held be bent, then, little by little and with the utmost slowness, until the extremity of the nerve comes to a soft and most light contact with one of its muscles — better, with one of the external lateral muscles. Behold: at the moment of contact, that is, of the total application of the nervous arc — without the interposition of any metal — most sensible contractions are excited, of no mediocre force and vigour, in all the muscles of the corresponding leg, and it moves, now more, now less, according to the varying state of the animal subjected to the experiment.

This fact seems to me really convincing; but Signor Volta strives to take away all its force, having recourse, as I have said, to the action of the stimulus, which he holds to be the author of the contractions that arise; and, to make his opinion prevail the more, he brings into the field even that little acceleration of motion which the nerve acquires, in his view by reason of the attracting moisture of the muscle — by which acceleration of motion the force of the stimulus is increased. Moreover, he cleverly confounds the contractions born of the application of this nervous arc with those tremors and starts which arise spontaneously in prepared animals, and which are produced by the violence done to them in the act of preparing them; and to make the action of the stimulus count for still more, he says that he has never observed such motions except when the nerves and muscles enjoyed a full vigour of vitality, such that every mechanical stimulus, every knock and compression, sufficed to excite them.

Now, beginning from this last point of objection: how can he maintain this, when, on the contrary, in order that the experiment succeed with clarity and certainty, it is necessary to wait until the aforesaid spontaneous motions are totally extinct? Moreover — since such motions [from the arc] succeed almost always in the same animal, not a few times only, but ten and twenty times and more (as I have assured myself by repeating the experiments in these last days) — how can he assert that the full vigour of vitality is necessary in the animals, when, on the contrary, for the happy outcome of the thing it is required that the frogs not be taken freshly from their marshy dwellings, vigorous and strong, but that they rather be kept long in suitable reservoirs, and suffer long fasts before the experiment, so that they remain drained, weakened, and are seen prepared nearly deprived of all blood?

Here, then — if I am not mistaken — is the cause of the different outcome of the most celebrated Signor Volta's experiments from that of mine. He has tried robust, well-fed frogs, and perhaps in the summer season, a time in which prepared frogs quickly dry out, and out of water quickly die, being unable to suffer long fasts without succumbing. The same has indeed happened to me, experimenting at such times.

But let us come to the objection of the stimulus, which is the strongest. Beginning with the acceleration of the nerve's motion, and the attraction of that nerve when it is at the greatest nearness to the corresponding muscles — phenomena which it has seemed to me also that I have observed in the aforesaid experiment — why could these not more aptly be referred to electrical attraction, rather than to the attraction of moisture, which is the common property of bodies? But leaving aside this reasonable doubt, and returning to the pretended stimulus which the nerve receives in falling upon the muscle: to make it clear that the indicated contractions do not depend on this, it suffices to apply, at the place where the nerve falls upon the thigh, some little insulating body, however rough and hard. At the contact of this with the nerve, no contraction will ever occur; and yet who is not forced to recognize in such a body a more efficacious stimulant than the soft substance of the muscles? Will its hardness perhaps not suffice to supply that force of stimulus which, according to Volta, is born of the acceleration of motion in the nerve, and which is here wanting for defect of moisture? Very well: let this insulating body be moistened as much as one wishes — provided, however, that the moisture does not reach as far as the muscles beneath — and let the experiment then be renewed; and it will be seen that, notwithstanding the added moisture, the outcome is the same. Will the moisture used perhaps be blamed as inefficacious, because it is not the same as that of the muscle? Well then, let that very moisture be used, but with the cautions indicated: none the less, the trial does not succeed. Perhaps, to defend his cause, he will accuse the diversity of this body's stimulus from that of the muscles? Let an experiment be made, then, in which the stimulus applied to the nerve is always the same, and the difference lies only in the continuation or interruption of the arc formed partly by the nerve, partly by that very substance which ought to serve as stimulus according to Signor Volta's view.

[1797, p. 10] The experiment is the following. Upon the little insulating body, applied as has been said to the lateral part of the thigh, let there be placed a little piece of the frog's flesh — of the muscles of the lower belly, for example. Let this be diligently and exactly isolated on the said body, in such a manner that neither of itself, nor by any moisture, has it the least communication with the muscles beneath. Let the nerve then be brought in the usual way into contact with that isolated little piece of flesh: no contraction — the experiment being made with all exactness — will ever be awakened. Let it then be brought about that this little portion of flesh, stretched out, communicates with the muscles beneath; and, the experiment renewed, let the nerve be brought into contact with that little piece of flesh in the same place: behold, the contractions and motions of the leg immediately appear. If the phenomenon were born of stimulus — since the acceleration of motion of the nerve, and the body upon which it strikes, are totally the same in the one case as in the other — the stimulus also ought to be the same, and consequently the contractions ought equally to occur in both cases. This not being verified, it seems demonstrated that such contractions do not absolutely depend on the mechanical stimulus of the nerve. Now who does not see clearly that when the flesh laid upon the insulating body is isolated, the arc formed by the nerve is interrupted, and that on the contrary it is continued when the flesh communicates with the muscles of the thigh? Or rather, who does not see that the arc is not applied to the muscle, in the first case, with both extremities, but with one only — that by which it naturally inserts itself into its muscle — and with both in the second, by means of the added little piece of flesh; and that therefore the natural circle of the electricity can be completed in this case, and not otherwise in that?

But who is there, however little versed in physical matters, who does not know that such a circle is not formed except where the electricity is unbalanced? It must therefore be confessed that it is unbalanced naturally in the animal, by force of a particular machine; and that from this imbalance, and not otherwise from mechanical stimulus, the above-reported contractions are born. I hope therefore to have demonstrated as much as Signor Volta required of me, in order that these consequences should follow well; and I hope also that he will not now take back what he had before courteously granted me, once the contractions were constant and every suspicion of stimulus removed.

[1797, p. 11] What I have striven to demonstrate with the experiments narrated, the most celebrated Signor Valli has also done in his letter on animal electricity — to whose fine understanding, and to whose most beautiful experiments, I owe not only very much, but animal electricity itself singularly owes much as well; since the contractions obtained by him in so many manners without the interposition of any metal, or of charcoal, but only by means of arcs formed either of parts of the animal itself, or of those who worked in the experiment, take away — in his judgement — all motive force, and all magic virtue, from the metals, and at the same time make animal electricity cease to be a problem. I hold it for certain that to the force of these experiments Signor Volta — a just thinker, as he is — will oppose nothing, and will abandon every suspicion of stimulus applied to the nerves.

But if, nonetheless, anyone not so just a thinker as he should wish obstinately to retain his opinion of the stimulus — on the reflection that however the experiment is set up, the body employed in it must at last touch the nerve, and from this contact some stimulus may always arise — I could make such a one reflect, as I warned a little above, that every reasonable suspicion of stimulus is taken away by the constancy with which the contractions succeed when the piece of flesh placed on the insulating body communicates with the flesh beneath, and are wanting when it has no such communication, although the stimulus is always, as everyone sees, absolutely the same. But nevertheless, to dissipate even these shadows and these fears, let the experiment be changed in the manner I am about to indicate.

Let a frog be held suspended by the fingers by one of the legs, and let the prepared nerve of the other be applied upon a little strip of tendon, or of flesh, of that same animal, which is placed upon an insulating plane, such as glass. Let the said strip be well moistened, and the extremity of the nerve well adapted to a portion of it. Then let another similar strip be laid over the muscles of the corresponding thigh, at about the place on which the nerve would fall if the experiment were made in the usual manner. Let this second strip have two extremities, one broad, the other narrow; the first is entrusted to the thigh in the manner already said, and the other extremity remains hanging and free. Things being so prepared, let this free extremity be raised with a very thin cylinder of glass, then let it be allowed to fall — not upon the nerve, but upon the first tendinous or fleshy strip stretched on the glass, in which the nerve lies. Let the point on which the free extremity of the strip falls be at some distance from the point where the nerve rests. At the moment of contact of the one strip with the other, the contractions will arise, where the animal has moreover all the conditions already so many times set out. And what renders the experiment more certain, and sometimes more pleasing, is to observe that the strip on which the nerve lies has some raised point of its own, especially at the extremity on which the strip we left free is allowed to fall; for in such circumstances the contractions succeed not only more happily and more promptly, but sometimes so frequently that the leg on which the experiment falls presents a kind of animal electric carillon, similar to that which I indicated in my little work. It is a clear thing that in this experiment neither the portion of strip on which the nerve lies, nor that which touches the muscle, is moved at all: only the extremities of those strips are moved, and come into mutual contact with one another. It would therefore be vain to seek in this case, and to claim, an irritation made either to the nerve or to the muscle; and it would likewise be a pure chimera to refer the arisen contractions to this irritation.

The experiment is certainly delicate, because the animal must be in that state of strength and size in which it can conveniently adapt itself to the trial, and the muscular contractions can arise very promptly, even after no small interval of time from the not-brief preparation. It is further required that the said strips be well moistened, and of a certain smallness and thinness, and well adapted to the animal. Where, however, the said necessary cautions are observed, the phenomenon does not fail to appear. I have seen it many and many times, and with me Signor Doctor Giulio Cesare Cingari, Professor of Practical Medicine; Doctor Francesco Sacchetti, Professor of Astronomy — men of singular learning and of finest judgment, who have always been my companions in all these experiences of mine; as also Signor Doctor Camillo Galvani, my dearest nephew, author of the far from commonplace little work on the Phosphori published at Bologna [1794], to whose singular dexterity and diligence I owe in good part the outcome of all my experiments.

But to overcome even the difficulty this experiment brings with it — indeed, to render it easier and more decisive — instead of the strips already mentioned, let there be detached and wholly separated from a frog the muscle of the leg which corresponds to the gastrocnemius of man, and forms the part that might vulgarly be called polpastreilo; then let this muscle be cut lengthwise and divided into two portions. Let the prepared animal then be stretched upon an insulating plane; under the one thigh, toward its upper part, let one of the said portions be placed, with its broader extremity; then upon the other portion, likewise at its broader part, let the extremity of the corresponding nerve be laid over. Let the said portions be at such a distance from one another that, raising one of them, or bringing it near with the usual cylinder of glass, it comes into contact with the other, and especially toward the free extremity. Care must moreover be taken to place the two parts of the muscle so that in the falling, or the approaching, they touch one another with their smooth and moist external part, or the one with that part and the other with the internal. Things being thus arranged, let the experiment be tried; rare will it be that the desired effect is wanting, and that prompt contractions are not seen. Among the very many frogs that I have tried, hardly has there been one or another that denied me the expected motions. I have seen them more than a few times not only in putting the said extremities into contact with one another, but also in removing them from it — which is the same as to say not only at the completion of the interrupted arc, but also at the dividing and interrupting of that which was continued: phenomena which correspond precisely to those reported in the little work cited many times, and which belong as much to the artificial arc as to the natural.

But since it might occur to some fastidious, and perhaps too severe, judge that the motion made in raising the aforesaid parts could still communicate some irritation, now to the nerve, now to the muscle, let the experiment therefore be changed in the manner I am about to set out, so as to remove every such suspicion — a suspicion, moreover, wholly vain and insubstantial, the two parts of the cut muscle on which the nerve and the thigh lie remaining always immobile. Instead, then, of making one of the extremities of the said pieces of muscle fall, or approach into contact with the other, let them rather be separated somewhat from one another, so that the muscular arc is interrupted; let both be left immobile on the said plane; then let a third piece of muscle be applied to the aforesaid extremities, in such way, however, that, raised by means of a little cylinder of glass, it is let fall upon one of them to complete the arc. It will be seen, where the frog is well adapted to the experiment, that at the moment of contact the usual contractions are born. The same I have also happened to observe, making use of a piece of a frog's skin folded back, provided this is adapted to the aforesaid muscular extremities with its inner moist surface.

Everyone therefore here sees the arc formed of pure animal substances, and every uncertainty and doubt of the experiment removed, and every suspicion of mechanical stimulus; and the circle of the electricity, depending on the sole machine of the animal put to the proof, is proved at the same time. But if, even on this, some doubt should remain to anyone, I flatter myself that you will be able to clear it wholly from his mind, whenever you will — as I hope — have the goodness to tell him of that experiment which I had the honour to make not long ago under your eyes, and which you deigned to approve as decisive. You will be able to assure him that no contraction is excited, as you saw, when — holding the prepared animal between the fingers in the manner proposed, so that the hanging nerve should fall upon the corresponding thigh — one bends the animal instead in such a way that the nerve comes into contact no longer with that thigh, but with the thigh of another frog prepared in the same manner, and held by another person, or resting upon any plane whatever. You will be able to point out that in such an experiment there is no contraction, although it is made so that the nerve comes into contact with the same muscle, and in the same place, in which it would come if the nerve were made to fall upon its own corresponding thigh. In making such an experiment, however, it is necessary — as I showed you — to observe diligently that the thigh of this second frog does not touch that of the first, nor communicate with it by any unnoticed moisture whatever, or other conducting body; for then the contractions are born, as if the nerve touched its own corresponding thigh. This fact, confirmed by your authority, will banish all suspicions of stimulus, and will put in full light the circle that is contested.

Now it is that I hope, from the kindness no less than from the accurate judgment of Signor Volta, and from his honesty, for full assent to the consequences deduced by me — indeed, for his favour toward animal electricity and its natural imbalance. This will be glory to me, and to animal electricity advantage and splendour — most of all if, having recognized it for true, he should also be willing to grant it his studies and his labours, and to enrich it with those most beautiful and most ingenious experiments that are proper to him.





Memoir the Second — first movement

[The Second Memoir answers Volta's second line of defence: that even without metals, the heterogeneity — the dissimilarity — of nerve and muscle might itself unbalance the common electricity, as the dissimilarity of two metals was said to do. This translation gives the memoir's first movement entire, through the experiment of nerve laid upon nerve and the conclusion Galvani drew from it. The remainder of the memoir is mapped in NOTES.md.]

[1797, p. 18] From all the experiments which I have had the honour to report to you in the preceding Memoir, it clearly appears that a long and constant series of muscular contractions, in all respects similar to those obtained by means of metals, is had without metals, by means of one simple arc of animal substance alone; and that consequently these do not depend on either of the two supposed causes — that of stimulus, namely, and that of metallic force. But here Signor Volta, and his followers, might say that although these contractions recognize neither of those two causes, a doubt may nevertheless arise whether the heterogeneity between nerve and muscle has a part in them — to which virtue of heterogeneity he finally refers all the force of the imbalance of the metals; so that if the said contractions are not born of the metals, they would nevertheless be born of a similar force, to which he attributes the whole effect of the contractions.

But to anyone who objected in this way, I would willingly ask whether this heterogeneity between nerve and muscle suffices by itself to excite the contractions, or whether it further requires that the electric fluid make a circle in the animal. If the first is affirmed, I would ask the reason why, in the case of a nerve of a prepared animal placed in light contact with a little piece of isolated muscle, the contractions are not produced; and why likewise, if this nerve is made to fall softly not upon the muscles of the thigh of the same animal, as we said, but upon those of the thigh of another animal detached from the first, they are not had in this case either. But if it is then said that the heterogeneity further requires the indicated circle — who does not see that with different words each of us is saying the same thing? For a heterogeneity which, in order to operate, requires a circle must finally be reduced to a particular and determinate structure of parts, which naturally keeps the electric fluid in a given imbalance — as I conjectured from the beginning. Since, therefore, every doubt concerning the force of the heterogeneity which the animal's nerves and muscles possess in giving the contractions seems removed, let us pass now to speak more closely — [here the memoir turns to the heterogeneity of the metallic armatures themselves] — of the experiments below.

[The omitted passage of the Second Memoir — on the armatures — argues, with a long series of trials, that contractions arise with armatures and arcs of a single homogeneous metal, of charcoal, of wet paper, of fresh muscle, of fruit and of egg-white; that they traverse whole chains of persons and the very ground; and that the heterogeneity of metals, where it acts at all, acts by increasing the force of the animal electricity, not by creating its imbalance — much as the metal coatings of a Leyden jar strengthen a discharge they do not cause. Its substance is summarized, and its experiments catalogued, in NOTES.md. We resume at the experiment with which Galvani closes the question of heterogeneity altogether.]

But to destroy even this shadow of doubt entirely, I made the following experiment. I prepared the animal in the usual manner; I then cut the one sciatic nerve, and the other, close to its exit from the vertebral canal; then I divided and separated the one leg from the other, in such a way that each of them remained with only its own corresponding nerve. Afterwards I bent the nerve of one leg in the manner of a little arc; then, having raised with the usual little cylinder of glass the nerve of the other leg, I let it fall upon this nervous arc — taking care that the nerve in its fall should touch the other, bent into an arc, in two points, and that the little mouth of the cut nerve should form one of the two points. I saw the leg move whose nerve I made to fall upon the nerve of the other; sometimes I saw both move; and the experiment succeeded, the legs being totally isolated, and having no commerce with one another except by the touching of the nerves.

Now what heterogeneity can here be called to the aid of the arisen contractions, when the nerves alone come into contact with one another? Will recourse perhaps be had to the stimulus which the nerves suffer in the fall of the one upon the other? But why then, striking one of those same nerves upon a much harder and rougher arc made of an insulating material — formed of sulphur, or of glass — are no contractions obtained? And yet in this case the stimulus born of the blow ought to be much greater. It seems to me, therefore, that it can be established that there is another series of contractions, which are obtained without a stimulus, without a metal, and without the least suspicion of heterogeneity; produced, therefore, by a circle of an electricity intrinsic to the animal, and naturally unbalanced in it.



Memoir the Third — the theory in seven propositions

[The Third Memoir undertakes to display the circle of the electricity case by case, by means of plates. Galvani prefaces the demonstrations with his general theory, stated in seven numbered propositions. Those propositions are translated entire; the plate-by-plate demonstrations that follow them belong to the untranslated remainder mapped in NOTES.md.]

[1797, p. 38] It will not be enough, in my belief, for the easy and clear understanding of the phenomena of animal electricity, to have proved generally that all the muscular motions hitherto excited depend on a particular circle of that electricity, made in the muscle by means of the nerve and of the arc; it seemed necessary to place that same circle before the eyes, and to demonstrate it in each particular case. This is what I have undertaken to do in the present memoir by means of some plates, and of new experiments — all of which I have the honour to address to you, most learned friend, and to submit to your fine discernment.

But first of all, to place the matter in the greatest possible clarity, it is necessary to recall to mind what is proposed in the Commentary, and in the anonymous little work, and what new attempts have since openly revealed and demonstrated to me concerning this same circle:

I. That the electricity which induces the muscular contractions is already gathered, particularly and cumulatively, in the muscle — that is, in the muscular fibre — but in a different state, of positive and of negative, in a state of imbalance, whatever the cause of it may be. Perhaps this could be the particular structure and intimate fabric of that fibre; or the double and different substance that composes it — gummy the one, as observation shows; nervous the other, as the sense it indubitably enjoys indicates; or else whatever other particular mechanism, still unknown.

II. That by grace of this imbalance the indicated circle of the electricity is made in that muscle.

III. That such a circle consists in the electricity departing from the muscle, and returning to it with every effort, by inviolable law — not otherwise than happens in the Leyden jar, or in the magic square.

IV. That such a circle is completed in the following manner: the electricity departs from the muscle by way of the nerve; hastens to the place on that nerve to which it is recalled by the force of the armature, or of the arc; issues from the nerve at that same place, drawn out by those same forces; enters the arc, and by means of it returns at last, with all its power, to the muscle from which it departed.

V. That by reason of this tendency, and of this effort which the electricity employs to return to the muscle, it always chooses the shortest road to reach it — a property which no one can deny is the same that is observed in the electricity gathered in the Leyden jar, or in the magic square.

VI. That the nerves, as clearly appears from what we have set out, are the natural and particular conductors of the said electricity — as those which, with their extremities, insinuate themselves into the intimate fabric and substance of the muscular fibre, where the said electricity seems to be gathered and accumulated.

VII. Finally, that they naturally exercise this office with their intimate medullary part and substance; and hence it is that, this being divided and interrupted by means of a ligature, or in another manner, the circle of the electricity is generally suspended — although in the tied or otherwise damaged nerves the membranes remain, and there is always the moisture, which could conduct the electricity out of the muscle.

[Here the Third Memoir proceeds to its demonstrations by plates. End of the translated portion. The remainder of the 1797 volume is mapped in NOTES.md.]
