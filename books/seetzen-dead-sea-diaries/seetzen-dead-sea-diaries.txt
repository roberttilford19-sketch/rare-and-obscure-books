From Mount Hermon to the Belka. Tour to Amman.



13 March 1806 (Thursday)

Our road today led us through a narrow rocky valley. We found terraces, a grotto church, olive and fig trees. Above lay hornstone and flints. On this height a cold wind blew, though otherwise the weather was fine.

Khirbet el-Basha remained on the left in a basin valley on the Mojet Dennanir; it is probably Pella. On the right lay Deir el-Nassara, which is destroyed; further on Khirbet el-Hommar and Ard Hommar. Then came Khirbet Dabuk, and to the left of the road Yajuz, where there are still ruins and a considerable spring.

(Ghreiszan lies between two waters, the Zerka and the Mojet Chreiszan, about two to three hours southeast of Jerash (Dscherräsch), where one is said still to find old ruins, but wholly without inhabitants.)

On the right lies Abdun, which is likewise laid waste. In the valley we met tents of Arabs, camels, and two flocks of sheep. Afterwards we came to a brook in the valley, in which there were many frogs.

We saw a destroyed bridge of three arches, ends of columns, also a fine square little building with a massive cupola and with corner pilasters of the Corinthian order. Also a ruined great church, of which much masonry still stands, and the half tower built of ashlar. Besides we saw remains of a great building, which was fine and adorned with several strong columns of limestone. Here the brook was walled in. Further on we saw a ruined fine temple with Corinthian ornament. Several columns still stand besides. The end of the Ghor was westward. A splendid amphitheatre, whose chord measured forty paces, also still stood. The stepped seats consisted of three tiers; above the second are little chambers. At the very top is a door in the middle, and below on the arena there are likewise two great doors to vaults. Everything is built of whitish marble. Before it there still stands a row of eight Corinthian columns with their entablature but without pedestal. I counted forty-two seats one above another [sic! — 1854 ed.]. The upper part is a bare vaulted chamber, a kind of alcove on both sides with a pretty niche. All the seats are vaulted beneath, and before the theatre there stand four more columns without entablature. Beside the amphitheatre there still stands another destroyed stately building, which was perhaps a church.

On the sides of this narrow valley one sees many entrances to grottoes and vaults. On the northwest side of the valley, on the mountain, one likewise still sees many ruins, but above all some toppled enormous columns of the Corinthian order. This columned building forms a rotunda and must have been very stately. The red-and-white variegated marble is uncommonly fine and durable. Of the city wall one still sees some traces, and one sees from them that its circuit was considerable.

The ground round about is mostly dry, stony, and gravelly, and the water is only four to five paces broad. The upper part of the city was, exactly like Abila, surrounded by a deep valley. Above there still stood a mosque (?).

Amman lies six hours east of es-Salt. From Amman to the Derb el-Hajj (the pilgrim road) is four hours; to Hossban northward half a day's journey.

Before six tents sheep and camels grazed. We were entertained with coffee and fresh bread.

On the mountain toward Amman I noticed several wild pear trees; one is also said still to meet with some pomegranate trees, as proof of former cultivation. Compare on Amman: Jeremiah ch. 49; Amos ch. 1; Ezekiel ch. 25.

From the mountains eastward of es-Salt I saw a small patch of the Dead Sea.



14 March (Friday)

On the left [the following eight lines are in the servant's hand — 1854 ed.] of the road [to es-Salt] they showed me Khirbet Szir, which lies two hours southwest of Amman, and where there are said to be columns, etc. Another place, es-Szir, lies at the spring Mojet es-Szir, which discharges into the Dead Sea. Either Szir or Szar must be the old Jazer, because the position agrees with that on the map. At the spring of the Mojet Szir, then, one would have to seek the Waters of Jazer. On this I must still gather more precise reports.

Khirbet er-Rabbahhidt is beside Dabuk, which we saw yesterday. These three places all lie close together.

My men hunted a gazelle here, but did not get it. There are also wild swine here. The small prickly shrub, like our heath, is called Bullan here.

By Karrak is the Zerka Ma'in, a water like the Zerka.

A strong cold piercing wind blew.

The peasants grazed here along the way for edible herbs.

Araq el-Emir (Örrak el Emir) lies southeastward of Salt half a day's journey, where there are many caves. Between Salt and Karrak is Jebel Attarus (Dschibbal Attarüs), from which Moses saw the Promised Land.

Karrak no longer lies in the Belka, and the river Zerka Ma'in makes the boundary; the land of the Mujib (Ard Müdscheb) is also between them.

Between Karrak and esh-Sharah (Scharäh) is the Wadi el-Hasa (Wuädy Hassa), where there is always flowing water, which discharges into the Dead Sea.

The Mojet Schoaib also runs into the Jordan, and there are mills on it.

In es-Salt there are sixty to seventy Christian houses, and three times as many Mohammedan. There are two priests here and a church. The inhabitants enjoy a rare happy constitution, in that they have to pay no taxes at all. There are several sheikhs in the castle, but they are sheikhs only insofar as they entertain strangers and guests. They have not the least authority over the rest, nor do they levy taxes from them. Only when distinguished guests come does each contribute a little toward the entertainment, and besides there is a guest fund (Mensil) in which account is kept of all expenses, which are summed after a year and the payment distributed, amounting to about three to four piastres per house. They pay neither poll tax nor land tax, etc. About twenty years ago one of the Arab family of the Adwan raised himself to ruling sheikh. But Ibrahim Pasha of Damascus, father of the present pasha Abdallah, besieged the citadel, and on his demand received the head of this sheikh, whereupon he withdrew, and since then es-Salt is without a head. The customary laws of the Bedouin obtain here — blood revenge — and fugitives who flee to them are protected at peril of their own lives and are not given up.

Many vines are grown here, and wine, brandy, raisins, and dibs (grape syrup) are made from them. The raisins and grapes are carried to Jerusalem, etc., and are uncommonly good. Most are white, very long, and of excellent taste. Besides grapes there are also olive trees, figs, and some mulberry trees; the latter only for eating, but one sees from them that their cultivation would thrive well here. Gulcha is a fragrant plant.

There are seven rosary-turners from Bethlehem here. There are also a couple of gun-stock makers, a couple of smiths, a merchant from Nazareth, and a silversmith from Jerusalem. The priest of Ajlun (Edschlun) was here, who comes from Jerusalem. The priest makes only wine.



16 March (Sunday)

Today I went to the church here, which was full, but in which so little attention prevailed that people quarrelled, and I believed myself far rather in a penny theatre than in a house of God — especially as a prompter shouted at the singers. In front sat the men, and behind them the women.

There is now a dearth of everything here, for there is neither dibs, nor oil, nor raisins. But the bread is white and excellent.

The children of Israel are said to have gone through the Wadi es-Schoaib to the Jordan, which is still now the usual road from here to the ford.

The greatest difficulties oppose my departure for Karrak.

There is neither a physician nor a judge here. The inhabitants are extraordinarily hospitable and make it a point of honour to entertain a stranger. The dishes are indeed very simple, but the season of Lent is partly to blame. This evening we dined with the foremost sheikh of the Christians on rice with oil. He waited on his guests himself, handed them water to drink and to wash their hands, and ate only after them. "Sheikh!" said I, "I am now your guest; I beg you to see to it that I can depart tomorrow." This request of a guest carried much weight, and he told me I need have no concern on that account.

The Christians can field about one hundred and twenty, the Mohammedans three hundred men fit to bear arms. They live together in the best harmony, and no Mohammedan may dare to insult a Christian.

This evening there was sheet lightning, and a heavy hailstorm fell.



17 March (Monday)

The large nose rings are called Chadam, the small ones Scheddra. Here in es-Salt, at Jerusalem, Bethlehem, Nablus, etc., people use only salt from the Dead Sea, which the peasants prepare and which is said to be very good.

At Aleppo and Damascus the wooden spoon is called Malaka; here it is Gaschuka or Szulbapheh.

Gegab is the bushy tree with the red bark, which at Aleppo is used almost only for burning.

On Jebel Ajlun (Dschibbal Edschlun) there stands here and there among the bushes a kind of pine with two paired long needles, like the pines, but without edible kernels. They call them Lisbab there.

Bread has various names: Chubbs, Rgif, Mhhide, Mröckadeh, Melleh, Urrs, Aeisch, Schräke.

In this season, when the Jordan is at its greatest, one crosses it by taking a skin under each arm, while a swimmer holds one fast and pulls one across. This is said not to be very uncomfortable. In summer laden asses cross it very easily, and the water comes to men only a little above the knees. So no miracle was needed for the Israelites to cross it.



18 March (Tuesday)

Today we made a contract with a man to lead us to Karrak. But since the morrow, Wednesday, is always held here an unlucky day to begin a journey, Thursday was fixed for our departure. We had been lied to several times.

Noble Arabian breeds of horses [the following seven lines are in the servant's hand — 1854 ed.]: 1) Szackläuwih dschedrany the stallion; Szacklauwieh dschedranieh the mare. 2) Muanaky the stallion; Muanakieh the mare. 3) Khheiän the stallion; Khhele the mare. 4) Abejän the stallion; Abeje the mare. 5) Dschelf the stallion; Dschilphe the mare.

These five races descend from five mares which Mohammed rode. They often have only a religious value, and among the others there are very many that are far better. The religious value is very great among the Mohammedans. These five all have their written pedigree. Besides these there are many others which likewise have their pedigree. Hödsche is the name of this paper which contains the pedigree. The noble ones all come from Nejd.

The Christians here also pay from each house one to four piastres to the Patriarch at Jerusalem, which are collected yearly by the bishop who comes here.

Today I took a walk through es-Salt, which stretches round the mountain, which is joined by a ridge to the other mountains. In the angle of the valley on the south side, quite below at the foot of the rocky castle hill, is a spring which comes out of a grotto. From the interior of the castle an underground stair is said to lead to this spring, which has several hundred steps, but whose water is used only in the most urgent cases. In the rock sides there are many grottoes. Beside the spring is an old building, which was either a church or a mosque. On a square tower beside it is an Arabic inscription.



19 March (Wednesday)

Today the weather was very fine. Heaven grant that we depart at last tomorrow!

The Mohammedan inhabitants here are allied by intermarriage with the Bedouin Adwan. Saltpetre is gathered here, and powder is made for their own use.

By the Zerka Ma'in are said to lie Diban, Medaba, and Ma'in (?). The last is also said to have been a considerable town. But no man knew Livias, Pella, Capitolias, etc.

The people bury their money in the bare earth. I saw at the silversmith's many Ottoman coins, which were therefore covered with green rust.



20 March (Thursday)

The weather was incomparably fine. Yesterday we visited the rosary-turners, who work in a grotto. They work chiefly boxwood, but sometimes also Önnäb and carob wood, which are both harder to work than the former. Önnäb yields reddish corals. The man makes daily ten to sixty rosaries, each of seventy corals. The chief instrument is the chisel with three points, of which the middle one is longer than those on either side. One must look carefully that the two side points are of equal length; otherwise the turning does not go well. It is flat and set in a round handle, which at the other end has a thin iron axle, which one sticks into the stock, and then one turns the chisel with the bow.

The turner holds the little wooden board with his left hand to the chisel and turns until the middle point has bored through the wood; then he begins anew, until at last the whole side is bored in this way. Then he turns the little board over and bores the other side, for which the existing holes always show him the place, and so one coral after another drops out, which he lets fall into his hand. The work goes very quickly. These corals then need only to be smoothed and freed of the projecting rim. This is done at Bethlehem. One pays for seventy corals only two paras, for the string two paras, and for the cross two paras. They work three to four months here and then return to Bethlehem. The rosaries for the Franks likewise contain seventy, but after every ten corals a red-dyed coral for the Our Father. For Frankish clergymen one hundred and fifty corals are necessary, which then cost about double. Smaller corals than the usual ones also cost a little more. Costly rosaries are also made of mother-of-pearl and of Indian seeds, Ddm and Kürrma. They work constantly for Terra Santa; for the other monasteries extremely little. Models of the Holy Sepulchre, of the church at Bethlehem, etc., are also made.

For some days now work has been going on in the vineyards. Jerusalem is called: 1) Auraslim among the Christians who read holy books; otherwise not in common life. 2) Bet el mockdes among the Mohammedans at Damascus. 3) Kodds among Christians and Mohammedans in the country. 4) ur— among the Anazeh and Beni Szahhar, Szirhan el Dimky.

The [from here to 23 March in the servant's hand — 1854 ed.] local Schemmds (his name on the poem) is an Arabian bard. I received from him a poem which he had made on Bonaparte's invasion of Egypt and Syria. He sang off another poem, which was by him, and which contained an invitation to the Christians of this region to repair quickly to Bonaparte. But it was written very figuratively, so that the Mohammedans could not understand the sense. His side trade is making Kassäid (odes) on the Arabian sheikhs. He then betakes himself to one of them and sings his poem with accompaniment of the Rbabe (rebec) in an assembly. According to the generosity and wealth of the sheikh, the latter then gives him several piastres, an Abbäje (cloak), Benisch, sheep, ass, or indeed even a horse or camel; the last, however, is rarer. He is poor, and this poetry is at bottom a kind of beggary. The Arabs, especially the Bedouin, are accustomed from youth to hear such poems, and since rhyme is uncommonly rich in the Arabic language, it is easy for them to make rhymes, for it seems that they are often more rhymed prose than real poems. The Bedouin sets forth everything in rhymes; if he has a quarrel with someone, he depicts his displeasure and the baseness of his enemy, and vice versa, in verses. Among them too there is a great multitude who occupy themselves with making poems of praise and thereby receive gifts from their own or other sheikhs. An excellent poet was the chief sheikh of the great tribe of the Anazeh, Kindn Teijar, who died several years ago. Among the Arabs of the Belka too the sheikh of the Adwan tribe, Diab ibn Adwan, distinguished himself; he still lives. It is a pity that only very seldom does anyone take the trouble to write these poems down; they are propagated for a time only orally and are soon displaced by new ones and brought into oblivion. Every Arabian peasant and every Bedouin usually knows several Kasside by heart. With Ibn Adwan there is a smith Uhmud, whose poems are likewise excellent. It was he who asked for the smith's daughter in es-Salt, and on whom Bulos made his Kasszide. Among the Arabs Schararat there is a woman named Szaida, who makes excellent Kassziden. (The Schararat are eastward of Kothrany to Tbuk.) Mahhmud el Mady, sheikh of the Arabs Eissa, is a poet in Hauran. These named are famous among all the tribes. They take no money. El Bik ibn el Duehhy still lives. He is likewise excellent, and a small sheikh of the Arab Wuld Aly Anazeh.

In the church here I saw a folio volume, a church book with Greek and Arabic title and Arabic text. It was printed at Aleppo in 1711.



Journey to Karrak.



21 March (Friday)

After much vain waiting, we at last got forward today. An armed Christian named Hassan brought us off. Chalil and a man from Acre went on foot with us. We drew southward up a considerably high and steep mountain and left Wady Schoaib on the right. We came past Khirbet Szük and Deir Nasaara, which are laid waste. About two hours from es-Salt we still met several peasants from there, who were ploughing several small fields in a valley, beside which was a small spring. Half an hour further we came to a laid-waste village Mickchlih, where it began to rain a little. The road led always over hill and dale, where limestone rocks often formed perpendicular walls. In many places the mountains were grown with trees and bushes, and the open places spring had covered with a green garment. Further on the country became quite open and the bushes ceased. The soil was excellent, but only here and there did one see a small patch cultivated, and that by Arabs Adwan, a small troop of whom also met us. Afterwards we came upon a fine green plain, where there were several ponds. Since Szar lies close by, and westward of it the Nahhr Szir or Sir rises and runs westward into the Jordan, I suspect these ponds may perhaps be the Waters of Jazer. For of a real small lake no man here could tell me anything, however much I inquired after it. But this plateau is somewhat depressed, and perhaps in antiquity it was more filled with water.

After about four and a half hours from es-Salt we came to a dozen tents of the Arabs Beni Szahhar, where we stayed the night. Soon after us came several peasants from es-Salt and several Arabs, who likewise passed the night here. A small thunderstorm came up. Two fires were kindled in the tent, and two circles formed. On account of the Fast we were entertained with nothing but coffee and bread, otherwise a lamb would have been slaughtered. The Arabs ate camel's milk, which was somewhat thick, had the appearance of cream, and made us feel our Fast heavily, so inviting was its aspect.



22 March (Saturday)

In the morning a thick fog prevailed, and after sunrise the mountains began to steam. We moved on at sunrise, as we had a strong march before us today. We came over good soil, which however lay wholly without cultivation, and was mostly grown with the prickly little Bullan, which gave the country the appearance of a heath.

After half an hour we came to Khirbet Naur, where the Wady Naur flows with water. On the left of the road they showed me a hill, in which in a grotto old drawings are said to be.

About three-quarters of an hour further we reached the ruins of el-Eale, probably Elealeh on the map, which however is wrongly drawn southwest of Heshbon. It lies on a hill on a plateau, which further on is hilly and wavy.

A quarter of an hour behind it we came to Husban (Hüsbän), which lies on a high hill consisting almost wholly of bare limestone. Except for some toppled columns one finds nothing considerable here. On the left of the road was further a walled-in pond, beside which we met some cattle with several Arabian herdsmen. Westward of Husban, in its neighbourhood, rises the Nahhr Husban, which discharges into the Jordan. On this little stream there are said to be several mills. ("Thine eyes are like the fishpools in Heshbon, by the gate of Bath-rabbim" — Song of Solomon 7:4.)

Just behind it we met a troop of ten Arabs, who were partly armed with long lances. Hassan knew several of them, and therefore they let us pass unhindered. The soil in this region is uncommonly good, but it lies wholly without cultivation and serves only the small sheep, goat, cattle, and camel herds of the Arabs for pasture. The open wavy and hilly country here became an almost boundless plain. On the left of the road, about an hour and a half distant, appeared the ruins of Szaltul(?) on a hill. There are everywhere very frequent moles here, as the numerous hills prove. From the presence of a certain small plant the Jews know that truffles are met with here.

After an hour on this plain we came to three laid-waste places, which lie close together on a rocky little height, and are all called el-Kpher.

Half an hour further we reached the ruins of the former town of Madaba (Mädaba), which, like Husban, lies on a rocky hill. Outside the former city wall, westward, I still found the remains of a stately building, of which, besides several walls, two columns of marble with their entablature still stood, which seemed very old. The one was of the Ionic, the other seemed most to agree with the Corinthian order. Under and beside this building were many grottoes hewn in the rock. Here at the foot of the hill one still sees a great pond walled with ashlar, but now dry.

Half an hour further lay the ruined village et-Tueme, and just after there appeared, westward of the road about an hour distant, the considerable ruins of Ma'in (Mae in), which is without doubt the old Meon on the map, from which westward rises the little stream Zerka Ma'in, which runs into the Dead Sea. If my gathered reports are right, which I learned on the spot, the drawing is wrong; for behind it must lie Jebel Attarus, Nebo of Moses. No one knew Livias; because it is new?

In several places I noticed that the road we took was paved, which came to me quite unexpectedly. Hassan assured me this was in old times the road of the Hajj. Probably nearer information on it will be found in Arabian works. The following day I saw several more traces of it. The flat limestone was still full of flints.

To obtain a night's lodging we sought Arabian tents. We at last found five in a small lonely valley, completely hidden. They belonged to the Arabs Beni Szahhar. Unfortunately there was no water, and in the region there was none. In such cases they drink the milk of camels and sheep, and can in this way do without water, since they often take no other food. We too would gladly have been content with it, had not the Fast hindered us. Since also there was no bread-corn, and flour without water could not have been prepared, we had to break up again without having enjoyed anything in the least and without being able to pass the night there, to seek other Arabs. The country here was very mountainous and rocky. We passed a ruined village named Libb, and just after we descried in a small rocky valley twenty-two tents, inhabited by the Arabs el-Hattabije. Since Hassan was acquainted with them, we stayed there overnight, having made seven to eight hours today. The tribe to which these Arabs belonged was thirty to forty years ago far stronger than now, and is said then to have counted some twelve hundred men fit to bear arms. Through all kinds of misfortunes, sicknesses, etc., their number so diminished that they are now not able to put half that on foot. Their chief wealth consists in sheep and goats. They have few camels. They till a little field by Madaba, whither they go with their tents at harvest. The sheep and goats are driven in the evening between the tents and guarded by dogs. The camels are partly hobbled before the open part of the tents, one leg being bound fast to the haunch, so that the camel can then only hobble on three feet and with difficulty.



23 March (Sunday)

This morning it was cold, and we broke up at sunrise, and after a quarter of an hour came to six tents. The bad road led over rocks. A quarter of an hour further, on the left, we again met a couple of tents on the mountain.

The white-flowering broom shrub, like snow, grew here in great quantity, also Kelch, seven to eight feet high.

After an hour and a quarter we reached the brook Mojet el-Wale, where a laid-waste place of the same name. Its bed is very rocky and quite densely grown with oleanders; there was also a destroyed mill on it. It flowed westward. After two and a quarter hours we reached Diban (Dibän), which lies on a mountain crest, and has a fine plateau round it. One sees only a few wells hewn in the rock by the road, and many traces of masonry.

We met six Arabian tents of the Arabs Beni Szahhar, where we drank coffee. At last we came into the very deep valley of the Mujib (Müdscheb), in which toward the west there is flowing water. I noticed here a strong stratum of flints between limestone beds. Below in the valley was a bridge of one arch built of ashlar, but unusable. Here it was very hot. In the valley of the Arnon we were plundered by the Beni Ammr. Beyond the river, on the mountain, were knolls of basalt on limestone beds. We met five tents of the Beni Hamide near Karrak. Near by lay Mhatta and er-Rihha.

[From here to Schaubak (p. 413, line 2) in the servant's hand — 1854 ed.] Arabian nomads enrich a country very little; for if one compares the number of livestock of an agricultural people of four square miles with that of one inhabited by nomads, one will find not only far more cattle in the villages, but besides obtain the richer products of the soil as the capital of the country. Despite his heavier labour, the husbandman is in his by-hours more active than the nomad, whom one never meets working in the field, as one still often does our shepherds and herdsmen.



24 March (Monday)

Early we went on again. The high plateau still continued, which we had crossed on the other side of the Mujib. In many places it was very little grassy, and an extraordinary quantity of wormwood grew on it. Yet the soil seemed excellent for grain, though no patch was cultivated. After a quarter of an hour we came upon a small herd of cattle, sheep, and goats. Great patches had the appearance of our heath fields, because of the wormwood and other small shrubs.

We came past a ruined place Bellue, from which westward, three-quarters of an hour distant, on a high hill, lay the ruined place Schihhin. Half an hour further lay the ruined Kreha, and again half an hour further Hhmeimat (Ghmeimat), from which westward half an hour distant lies Bet el-Kerm. Up to here the stones on the fields were basalt, which also showed itself as rock in some watercourses.

At Hhmeimat we found twelve tents pitched in a circle, inhabited by Greek Christians from Karrak, who like the Bedouin dwell under tents. We were well received here, and they seemed glad to see us a couple of hours with them and to be able to entertain us with bread dipped in oil and coffee. They call themselves Kaddadiin. Eastward of Hhmeimat there appeared still at some distance another destroyed place. From these ruined places lying on our road one sees how strongly inhabited this region was before. Further on there again appeared on the wide fields small flocks of sheep and goats. Afterwards came again limestone soil, and the basalt ceased.

About an hour from the former place we came to the ruins of Robba, the old Ar Rabbath, which by their extent prove that they are the remains of a considerable town. Robba lies on and on the sides of an oblong hill, still on the wide plain that was called Moab. Only eastward at a considerable distance does one see a range of low mountains. Besides a multitude of foundations of buildings and many ashlar stones, one still sees here several fallen-in vaults, remains of a very few houses, several ends of columns, etc. The most remarkable are the ruins of an ancient splendid building, which was either a palace or a temple. The vaults beneath it have fallen in. Some masonry still stands, and of the former peristyle two columns of the Corinthian order, which seem to be without pedestals (below they stand in rubbish). This building is of yellowish-white marble. On the south side of it one sees two walled-in, now dry ponds, and further on several cisterns hewn in the rock in the shape of wells. On this same side one sees the ruins of a place called Jarud, which was perhaps a suburb of Robba. How terribly is this residence of ancient kings and its land laid waste! In Robba we also met several Christians.

About three-quarters of an hour further we reached the inconsiderable ruins of Kraphilla. Here, and already just behind Robba, cultivation again appeared here and there — namely wheat fields. The wheat stood well, and was about three to four inches long.

Now the ground became more mountainous again, and some time after we descried Kirrak lying on a mountain, round about surrounded by still higher mountains. We had first to cross an uncommonly deep valley, on whose sides lie the ruined villages Duehereh and Szweinije. This northern valley is extraordinarily deep, and the side of the Karrak mountain very steep and rocky. On the right, on the higher mountain side, appeared many green fields. There were several grottoes there, further strata of flint between crumbly white limestone, and blocks of black flint of extraordinary size. The path up the mountain is very toilsome and steep, though it goes zigzag. Karrak shows itself from afar almost like es-Salt, especially as regards the castle. But the houses are above on the crest of the steep mountain.

Moab was always a very sheep-rich land; compare 2 Kings 3:4; Isaiah 16:2, 25:10–12; Jeremiah 48:3; Ezekiel 25:4; Amos 1.

("An envoy, whom he (Mohammed the Prophet) wished to send to the king of Bossra (in Hauran?), was murdered in the Syrian town of Muta — without doubt the town of Mote now among the shattered towns here — which stood under Greek overlordship. To avenge this murder he sent 3,000 men under the command of Seid to conquer the town. This host did win a battle, but could not achieve its aim upon Muta, because it was too well defended." — Introduction to Augusti's Little Koran, p. 35.)

Schaubak is said to be as far southward of Kirrak as es-Salt is of Karrak — that is, three days' journeys.

There are here several hot wells: 1) in the Wady Beni Hamide, four hours westward of Karrak, four hours from Robba, in the deep valley, five hours from the Dead Sea; 2) in the Mojet/Wady el-Hossa, seven hours southward of here and nine hours from the Dead Sea; 3) at the Zerka Ma'in, three hours westward of Ma'in and eight hours from the Dead Sea.

From here one sees el-Quds (el Kodds) and Bethlehem in clear weather and with good eyes. The Dead Sea is a day's journey, or seven hours, from here.

On the east side there are chiefly three places where salt is gathered. It forms strong crusts there, so that one rides on horseback upon them, and when it is hollow it sounds like ice in the wind. It is very white and almost like rock salt. In the pure salt I noticed no bitter taste. The Karrakers fetch it in company, because of the Arabs. The salt is said sometimes to form small hills, room-high, and a Karraker loaded a mule with one piece.

From the height by the ruined mosque one sees a part of the Dead Sea and the mountains of Jerusalem. This is a pretty view. One could be in Jerusalem in two days if one could cross the sea. Toward the south end one crosses the sea in summer in five and a half to six hours. The water comes to the mid-shins, but the feet hurt underneath from the salt, so that it is very toilsome. If one wants to go through, one steers by certain marks on either shore.

Kothrany is seven hours and Hassa nine hours from here. Kothrany is therefore most probably Castra Arnonensia, because the Mujib seems to come from there. Kothrany is eight to nine hours from Belka or Dobba, and is said to be five to six hours eastward of Ledschun. Petra is said to lie a day's journey southward of Karrak.



25 March (Tuesday)

This morning rain showers fell. The people here are uncommonly hospitable and quarrel in a friendly way over who shall entertain the other; this strife is said sometimes to have serious consequences. The bread here does not seem so well-tasting as in es-Salt. This morning the young priest also entertained us with olives and much coffee. The priest was instructed by the bishop at Jerusalem, and the Schemmds too.

The Arabs of Hebron pass most safely among the Arabs round the Dead Sea. There are no fish in this sea. Last year the bishop drew a cast net for some hours beside it, but caught nothing. He also visited the baths mentioned, in company with fifteen to sixteen armed men.

In Szaphia peasants are now settled, who live in clay houses. From there to Hebron there are now no inhabited villages. All is destroyed. The castle here is uninhabited and ruined; the former walls of the town too are almost wholly destroyed, and only a couple of towers still stand. There are here eighty Christian and double as many Mohammedan houses. But since in each several families live together, the Christians are said to be able to field four hundred and the Mohammedans about seven hundred armed men, for the married kinsmen stay together, and each manages with his family in a small room.

The hot bath at the Zerka Ma'in in the region of Attarus seems to be the ancient Kallirrhoe, and perhaps one must seek the ancient Machaerus on Attarus.

There is here a pond only for the cattle. The inhabitants drink the water of draw-wells, which is good. The wells here are countless.

The territory of Karrak goes from the Mujib to Hössa; then follows Dschebal, and then Scharifa. Dschebal [from here to 26 March in the servant's hand — 1854 ed.] is a day's journey and two to three hours, Tophila twelve hours, and Schaubak about twenty-four hours (like es-Salt) from here.

On Dschebal and Scharah there are said to be many springs only on the west side, which after a short course lose themselves again and do not reach the Dead Sea.

Here there is a great lack of wood, and in its stead they burn chiefly a small shrubby plant, called Gneda or Chtela, whose leaves are fragrant.

The inhabitants here are without laws, and therefore it is all the more to their honour that they are still as good as they are. A man of about forty assured me he could remember only one case of an inhabitant having killed another in the heat by a blow. In the feuds with the Arabs, however, they are very bloodthirsty, and they did not cease murdering all Arabs of the formerly mighty tribe of the Beni Hamide that came in their way — child and greybeard, etc. — until this tribe was wholly weakened, when the Christians took possession of their ground round Robba. They wished Christians here, especially Russians, in order to be able to slaughter the Mohammedans!



26 March (Wednesday)

Today the weather was rainy.



27 March (Thursday)

Today is fine weather, but somewhat cloudy.

All the inhabitants wear great handschars, often mounted with silver; otherwise almost all wear the Bedouin cloth.

Many olive trees, figs, lemons, many grapes (only for eating), few pomegranates, and some prickly pears make up the products of the land. Wheat, barley, lentils, and white durra are sown. From Hebron are brought hither clothes, abbayes, cotton linen, tobacco (the last also from Jerusalem). From Damascus come with the Hajj from Kothrany clothes, abbayes, women's silk headbands, boots, corals, haberdashery — e.g., steels, knives, scissors, etc. — silk thread. The pasha advances money to merchants to buy barley here, which they bring to Kothrany. The Christians here also rob, like the Mohammedans. The mountains seen round Karrak are the property of the inhabitants. The more distant regions belong to Arabs of the tribes Ammr, Beni Hamide, Szalit, and if the inhabitants want to till fields there, they must first come to terms with them, namely by usually giving the fourth part of the yield. Kerszenne is also grown for the camels.

Besides Karrak [from here six lines on the Hömmar (p. 417) in the servant's hand — 1854 ed.] there are in the land of Karrak, which stretches from the Mujib to Wady Hössa, the following three inhabited villages: Katribba, Chansireh, and Errdk. They lie westward of Karrak, and their inhabitants are all Mohammedans.

Besides these the following destroyed places were named to me, of which many were towns in antiquity: Tinnib, Rakin, Betin, Haddade, Uedry, Schinnar, Robba, Hodscha, et-Der, Ledschun, Serraa, Batir, Kraphilla, Schihhin, Kothrany, Dad Ras, Mote, Mohhna, Muddin, Zemra, Eisdr, Hebesch, Beddn, Subbiiba, Teil, Schahhara, Hhemeimat, Balde, Bet Allah, Jarud, Schbeba, Mudschdelen; and besides these a great number of other laid-waste villages. One sees from this that in antiquity this land was uncommonly populous and flourishing, and that only devastating wars could produce the present desolation. Still: Bteijir, Korriet el-Grilb, Smakije, Sphards, Wudscheffrus, er-Ras, Wasurrpha, Waphakueh, Ossra, el-Nackas, Um Hammar, Nihhel, et-Tur, Dsjiphar, Abbur, Kussr Ubba, Em Hey, el-Bottra, el-Mreiga, Szul, Etbaa, Beddan, el-Kinnar, Ras el-Gijjeta, Walubere, el-Hhdeb, Khurrbet el-Phuttian (this place is near Ledschun), Ris el-Mudschar, Turren, Daud, Aijita, Tradschme, el-Chursieh, Mger, Ey, Bet Lidschi, Massar, Ashur, Esmekieh, Grab el-Kara. Ancient towns in Moab were: Nebo, Kiriathaim, Madmen, Horonaim, Luhith, Camos, Dibon, Aroer, Holon, Jahsa, Mephaath, Bet Dibkthaim, Bet Gamul, Bet Meon, Baal Meon, Kirioth, Boszra, Kirheres, Jazer, Sibma, Heshbon, el-Eale, the Waters of Nimrin, Ar, Kir, Baith, Medba, Zoar, Eglaim, Born Elim, Dimon (the waters of), Kir Hareseth, Sela, Bet Jesimoth (Jeremiah 48:33: "Joy and gladness is taken from the fruitful field and from the land of Moab; and I will cause wine to fail from the winepresses: the treader shall tread no more with shouting").

Waters in Karrak: 1) Wady Wale comes from Ard Ramadan, namely from Chan es-Szib, and discharges into the Dead Sea, after it has united with Wady Heddan, which is southward of it and far shorter. 2) Wady Hassa or Hössa rises from several springs westward by Kalat el-Hassa and flows alone into the sea, in whose neighbourhood its banks are said to be uncommonly high and steep. 3) Wady Karrak rises from several springs at the foot of the Karrak mountain, drives four mills, and runs after seven hours into the sea. 4) Wady Mujib rises three to four hours above the place where we crossed it, eastward. Above its source lies an old once-strong place Ledschun (Castra Arnonensia?). It flows into the sea. 5) Zerka Ma'in flows alone into the sea and rises westward of Ma'in. 6) Wady Husban flows alone into the sea.

The Hömmar (asphalt) springs from rocks that are round the sea, and indeed under the surface of the water, as swimmers themselves have felt and seen. One usually finds pieces on the shore in many places, but only small ones; large pieces are rare. Some years ago a piece drifted to this shore with which the Arabs loaded fifty to sixty camels, and carried it to Jerusalem. They pay nothing for it.

Yesterday came several inhabitants of Hebron, who return thither, either to the ford of the Jordan or to Szaphia.

In the brook below at the western foot of the mountain of Karrak there are many fish and oleanders. The mountain consists mostly of strata of flints of several colours and contains often traces of shellfish.

I returned through a gate which, winding, was hewn with a long vault through the rock.



28 March (Friday)

[The following twenty-seven lines in the servant's hand — 1854 ed.] Yesterday Jüszef went some hours from here into the region of the Christian Haddadiin, to receive back our stolen things.

Here one eats a wild plant named Kahhmum, raw and cooked, with burgul or rice; it has a sour taste.

In Dschebal there are the following inhabited places: Eime, Taphile, Schaubak, es-Szunne, es-Szille, es-Szunnefha, el-Bzera; Schaubak is said to consist only of a castle, like es-Salt, without other houses outside, and is inhabited only by Mohammedans. Dschebal is said to be three days' journeys long, from Hössa to Scharah, and to be, like Scharah, just as rich in places as the land of Karrak.

Juszef told me that once on Scharah, at Daher el-Akabeh, a whole caravan froze to death.

Of Edom several places of Holy Scripture treat: Amos 1:12: "But I will send a fire upon Teman, which shall devour the palaces of Bozrah (in Edom)." Jeremiah 49:16 and 22: "Because thou (Edom) dwellest in the clefts of the rock, and holdest the height of the hill, etc. — Behold, he shall come up and fly as the eagle, and spread his wings over Bozrah." Isaiah 63:1: "Who is this that cometh from Edom, with dyed garments from Bozrah?" Isaiah 34:6: "The sword of the Lord is filled with blood, it is made fat with fatness, and with the blood of lambs and goats, with the fat of the kidneys of rams: for the Lord hath a sacrifice in Bozrah, and a great slaughter in the land of Edom."

Es-Scharah is said to be a little town inhabited on the mountains, and indeed the only one. Mohammedans dwell there, peasants Ennaimat. There are figs, grapes, oil, and pomegranates there. Is this Scharah perhaps Zoara on Paulus's map? Or Zerah in 2 Chronicles ch. 14, v. 9? Or the mountains or Mount Seir (Ezekiel 36:3 ff.)? This Seir is named as a part of Edom. Its towns are mentioned there (Zerah may also be the name of a prince at that place.)

At Öddr in Kirrak there are said still to be four columns.

The Arabs have enormous coffee mortars. Here grain is sometimes kept ten years long in wells under the earth. Some of it gets a somewhat musty taste from it; but I tasted bread of six-year-old wheat and could notice no bad taste. The wells are partly outside the houses beside the rock wall which formed the lower part of the city wall.

Today it was sensibly cold and windy; toward evening thick clouds covered the sky.

Since most inhabitants of both religions live outside in tents, the place is now fairly empty of people.



29 March (Saturday)

In the past night it rained.

The ford of the Karrakers in the Jordan is one hundred and thirty-four fathoms broad, and is said to be almost a day's journey from that of the Salt people. The water is deeper there than in the Salt ford, but seems almost to stand still, whereas the other has a strong current. One binds a skin fast on the back, and a swimming Bedouin takes in his mouth a cord which is fastened to the arm. In summer the water goes only to the knee.

For a couple of days the leaves of the fig trees below in the valley have been breaking out. This valley is very lovely: on both sides, on the high steep mountains, green fields; in the middle ground the sea, and behind it the mountains of Jerusalem.

Kadid is a small prickly shrub with pods and long thorns and pinnate leaves; it is eaten by camels, cattle, and sheep, but also used for firing.

Juszef came back again today. He had received back one part, but with the other part of the things one of the Beni Hamide had gone off.

All Damascene hucksters have under each tribe an Achu (brother), to whom they give something fixed (Chue), for which he is bound to procure for him what his tribe has stolen. He makes a cross with the dagger, places himself in the middle, and says: "By God, this is my brother!"

In the water here there are crabs, which the Arabs here call Abu Snep; they fear them and do not eat them. It is the crab kind.



30 March (Sunday)

Today the weather is warm and uncommonly fine. After church we visited the old fortress, which is indeed wholly ruined, but very worth seeing. It is built on the highest part of the mountain, on craggy rocks, and has two high strong walls with great vaults under the earth one above another. The buildings seem to be from the times of the Greek Christians. Here there is still a ruined Greek church with half-effaced paintings on the lime walls. Before the castle gate stands a column of polished Egyptian granite. Now the church and the basement serve for sheep and goat stables, for no one lives upon it.

On the east and southeast side of Karrak, on the other side of the deep valley, there is a great multitude of grottoes in the rocks. The view from the castle toward the Dead Sea is very pleasant. My companions, the priest, etc., saw Jerusalem, which lies due westward of here; Bethlehem they did not see, and Hebron one cannot see, because it lies deep. Jerusalem showed itself white. Ain Dschiddi (Ein Gedi) too one sees from here on the west side of the sea. Khirbet Szammara too one sees on this east side in the neighbourhood of Karrak, westward.

On [the following ten lines in the servant's hand — 1854 ed.] the west side of the Dead Sea are Arabs Gauiroy(?), who grow the Nil plant (indigo), which they call Nil or Nuile. They also grow the seed, and sell it to Damietta, whence it is carried to Szaeid for sowing. It is called Bisre. The seed in Szaeid is said not to be good.

The seed of the Nil is put into very large sacks of hair, which are called Schuill, into which goes one Grara. It is sold there by a measure called Wuebe, or also by Kaddahh, Rubbea, and Kele.

The Dead Sea is five hours from here.

The Jews' pitch (asphalt) is said to spring under the water from a great cave, to set on the rocks, and afterwards to be broken off in great pieces by the waves.

The inhabitants here have no camels, only cattle, asses, sheep, horses, mules, and goats. Always part of the inhabitants is at home, and part under tents the whole year through. They can field about four hundred Christian and six hundred Mohammedan men fit to bear arms; there are here about seventy to eighty Christian and one hundred and twenty Mohammedan houses. There are four water mills here, which are shared between Christians and Mohammedans. Westward are five to six springs.

On the Gör plain on the east side of the sea sulphur is also found.

The Beni Ammr intermarry reciprocally with the Mohammedans in Karrak.

The inhabitants of Ma'in always come hither and buy wheat and barley, and bring it, because of the Hajj, to Maan. Maan is three days' journeys, or twenty-four hours, from here.

All the inhabitants in Karrak have a gun and a long handschar, both of damascened work; others are not saleable here. On the castle I saw still a swivel gun of copper, and two others Wahaby(?) had by chance received from here.

For four days the Dscherdeh(?) has been in Meserib.

The coloured reed grows one to two hours from the warm baths in Wady Hamad; it is thin and serves for writing. The warm springs there come out of rocks and are hot, as at Tiberias. The warm springs in Wady Hössa are less hot. Those in Wady Zerka Ma'in are said also to be very hot.

East and southeastward of here there are said also to be sometimes wild asses, ostriches, and wild oxen. At Dschaffar Szeljar, three hours southward of here, are two columns of green colour, and the sheikh's grave consists of white marble and an iron railing (778). Beside it there is said to be in a rock an invisible bell, which lets itself be heard once a year.

In the gardens onions, cress, and Hölbe (fenugreek) are sown.

In [the following eighteen lines in the servant's hand — 1854 ed.] el-Gör there grows on the east side of the Dead Sea a tree named Aöschar, which bears a fruit like pomegranates, enclosing a silk-glossy cotton. This cotton is spun in Karrak into coarse threads, which are used for gun priming. Into fine threads it cannot be spun. From the root of this tree, which is the size of a fig tree, one obtains by incisions a milk, which is given to women who bear no children. It is called Lebben Aöschar. I first believed this tree might perhaps be Gossypium arboreum L.; but since its wool can be used like common cotton, it must be another tree. I therefore conjecture it to be a kind of Asclepias gigantea. For although the species of it are met with only in India, one might perhaps also seek them round the Dead Sea, because the heat there is extraordinarily great, and I found there a wholly new vegetation and a new climate. These I take for the apples of Sodom. The tree Sakum also grows by this tree, and the bishop at Jerusalem takes the seed from Karrak and prepares oil from it in Jerusalem. Handal (colocynth) also grows there, and the Arabs use it as a purgative.

Here too dung is burned for fuel.

The chief sheikh of the Anazeh Arabs was called Kinan Feijar. Some inhabitants wear only bare soles under their feet.



31 March (Monday)

Today was fine weather. Tomorrow, hopefully, we go on. I pay for a mule to Jerusalem twenty-five piastres, and about two piastres for barley. From es-Salt to here I likewise paid for a horse and an ass twenty-five piastres. We must provide ourselves with bread and a waterskin.

The inhabitants of es-Salt are wealthier, more civilized, and more hospitable than the Karrakers. In the Fast we ate neither egg dishes, nor milk dishes (cheese, butter, lebben, etc.), nor meat. There were also no fish, because the Arabs do not eat them and because they are also forbidden.

On Bonaparte many occasional poems and songs circulate, but extremely few written ones.

Here I bought of the natural sulphur, which is of a pale yellow colour, and is found from Moctaa to Szaphia in el-Gor on the east side of the Dead Sea. It is used for powder, against the mange of camels, for sulphur matches, and snakes are banished by its smell.

I [the following twenty-two lines in the servant's hand — 1854 ed.] saw here today a horn of the Wual (Capra ibex L.), which has half rings, and of which the grips of handschars are made. It was heavy and blackish. It is said to be sometimes four feet long and arm-thick. It is found in Belka, Karrak, Dschebal, and Scharah. I shall seek to obtain a horn of it.

Here one often pays for a girl as wife a thousand piastres, and other gifts besides — handschar, gun, etc.

A common dish is a soup of lentils and some peeled barley, into which much bread is broken until it grows thick, when it is eaten with the fingers.

The Ribas (rhubarb) grows eastward of Kothrany in very great quantity, and is brought hither by the Bedouin under the name Kahhmun. The stalk, which is a foot high, is eaten raw. To make a roob (syrup) of it is not understood here.

The sea salt is found round the whole sea, and indeed at such places as are so low that the water in winter and spring, when it rises, passes over them, whereupon afterwards, without any doing, a hand-broad strong firm crust is formed, almost like rock salt. One loaded a mule with one slab of it.

Several Arabs Schararat came, who brought several camel loads of Ribas hither, for which they took wheat in exchange.

Today there appeared a kind of high smoke-haze (Heerrauch), and it was fairly warm.

The priest here gave me a letter to the Greek bishop Miszail, bishop of Karrak.

We ate a dish: lentil soup with wheat flour, made into a soft dough, and oil poured over. This is already something unusual.

In the houses of Karrak there is not the high upper room, but just as in tents, in the middle the round fireplace.

The [from here to the end in the servant's hand — 1854 ed.] Damascene hucksters sometimes use certain stupefying grains to catch the fish in the pond of Mserib.

When I slept in the open I always covered at night not only the body but also the face and the whole head; this I found very good.

Hadschar Musa (Moses' stone) from the northwest shore of the Dead Sea is a black stinking bituminous shale with traces of petrifactions.

Is the tree Sakum an Amyris Opobalsamum L. or Guilandina Moringa L.?

On the region by the Dead Sea treat some places of the Bible: 1) "He (Amaziah, king in Judah) slew of Edom in the Valley of Salt ten thousand, and took the town Sela by war, and called it Joktheel unto this day" — 2 Kings 14:7.

The Valley of Salt is most probably the Valley of Salt at the south end of the Dead Sea, and Sela the place Szille in Dschebal.

2) That the Jordan in old times was greatest at harvest in spring is also seen from Jesus Sirach ch. 24, v. 36.

3) "Which forsook the righteous (?) — 'desolate land still smokes, a witness to wickedness, with trees bearing unripe fruit, and the salt pillar standing as a memorial of the unbelieving soul'" (Wisdom 10:7).

4) "And Abishai, the son of Zeruiah, slew of the Edomites in the Valley of Salt eighteen thousand" (1 Chronicles 19:12 [18:12]).

5) "The lot of the tribe of the children of Judah according to their families was the border of Edom, at the wilderness of Zin, toward the south, at the uttermost part of the south country, so that their south border was from the shore of the Salt Sea, that is, from the tongue that looketh southward" (Joshua 15:1–2).

It would be interesting to know the height of the surface of the Dead Sea above the Mediterranean.

Which water evaporates most strongly, salt or sweet?

"One sees there (round the Dead Sea) some ruins of the five sunken cities, and their accursed ashes still bring forth fruits which seem good to eat, but which, when one barely touches them, fall into dust. Thus one is convinced of this terrible event not only by faith, but even by one's own eyes" (Josephus, Jewish War). But Josephus was a bad physicist, for he saw the Sabbatic river, the wonderful plant in the valley of Bara, etc.

At Karrak one finds a whitish fine-grained sandstone, which yields very good whetstones, which are brought to Hebron, etc.

In es-Salt there were three Mdaphea or hostels for travelling Mohammedans and one for Christians; here there is not a single one. This probably comes from few travellers coming hither.



Departure from Karrak to Jerusalem round the southern end of the Dead Sea.



1 April (Tuesday)

Today there was again a thick haze, like a high smoke-haze, and a whirling wind.

The sleeves of the women's shirts here are very long, so that their point reaches to the ground.

Toward evening, two hours before sunset, the haze was very strong.



2 April (Wednesday)

Half an hour before daybreak we travelled off. There was a strong and so extremely biting cold wind that I had to dismount a couple of times to warm myself again by walking. Further on we passed several fine fields, on and by the mountains. But these were rarities today.

After about two and a half hours we reached a small valley with high perpendicular rocks, like walls, as though made by art. Below at the foot of this rock wall lies the small village Errdk with Mohammedan inhabitants. Here are several small clear springs. There are olive trees, and on the mountain sides grain fields. There are also several pomegranate trees here. On the mountain behind one enjoys a view over a considerable part of the Dead Sea. Here I found many thousand-legs, black, shining, and thick as a quill. But I had no time to count the feet of this Julus.

After three-quarters of an hour we reached the small village Chansireh, which lies on a high mountain, where olive trees, figs, and pomegranates stood. From here we again had a view toward the Dead Sea. Further on grew much Kolly (the coral-like Salicornia). There were also many snails with shells here.

Our road went along sandstone and monstrous rock walls of it, which we descended for three hours on a fearful stair to the Ghor es-Saphia (Gör es Szäphiä). The mountain formed two perpendicular tiers. In the sandstone there are white kidneys and often pretty wavy bands and stripes.

An hour and a half after sunset we at last arrived at our station, a flat sandy dry bed of a forest brook, which was very broad and even, at the foot of the steep fearful wild sandstone mountains, on which were ibexes, of which we saw one. Here grew much Sziddr, a kind of thorn shrub.

Karaahhy is the name of the mouth of the Wady Hössa, which is only a quarter of an hour westward of us. We were now more southerly than the southern end of the Dead Sea. In the valley of Wady el-Hössa there is said to be a hot spring, but less hot than in Wady Hamad and at the Zerka Ma'in. It is seven hours southward of Karrak and nine hours from the Dead Sea. Here is the boundary of Edom, or Petraean Arabia.



3 April (Thursday)

This morning we travelled off before sunrise. We came first over a stone field and then over sand. Thereupon we passed the Karahhy, or the lower end of the Wady Hössa. It is somewhat richer in water than the Mujib or Arnon, and forms three to four channels. It comes from the southeast and is the river of Segor or Zoara on Paulus's map. Plants and climate here are wholly different. By the river stood many bushes (among them Thuja aphylla L.?) and grasses; afterwards, somewhat distant from the Dead Sea, one comes into a wide splendid plain of excellent loam, but so salted that no trace of vegetation is visible. In the watercourses one sees thick salt crusts, like ice. This is without doubt the Valley of Salt in the Bible.

We drew across it westward about three hours long, to a wholly naked, utterly plantless, torn mountain, which contains strong strata of rock salt and makes the Dead Sea so salty. It is full of holes, clefts, cracks, jagged points, outwashings, and its colours are whitish, peach-blossom red, and earthy. Everything is loam and crumbly rock. Never did I see so utterly shattered a mountain. Many places are perpendicular, overhanging. This mountain stretches from north to south, is two to two and a half hours long, and is a little separated from the other mountains on the west side. At its foot are many gullies, in which are salt crusts, also blocks of rock salt.

We now drew always northward beside it, at some distance from the Dead Sea, which was now on the right hand, and whose shore here is very low. At the end of the salt mountain, about half an hour northward of it, is the ford in the sea, and just behind it the western shore receded considerably, and the sea grew broader. Just before the ford, at the foot of the salt mountain, was an artificial heap of stones of about twenty feet in height and of considerable circumference. One of our Arabs climbed it. Is this perhaps the so-called Salt Pillar?

Afterwards we rode always along the shore, where all is scree, and the mountains beside us wholly naked, torn, unfruitful, and steep-sided. There is only a narrow sloping space between them and the sea. To gather seaweeds and saltwater shells I dismounted and followed the shore for a time. But to my astonishment I found no trace of them, for single shells that I saw were snails and land snails. Of Fucus, etc., there was no trace. On the shore lay tree branches and roots from the Jordan. This sea is by far not so beautiful as the Sea of Tiberias. One could make the unfruitful salt plain arable by ditches.

The seawater had an extraordinary salt taste, like a ready brine.

About two o'clock we turned left into a hollow way with high steep naked marl walls; here we halted to eat and to drink water, which is in a spring in the hollow way about ten minutes higher up. (The brook at Thamara on Paulus's map was not there, but I saw its dry bed, as it seemed to me.) On the north side of this deep ravine one sees on the mountain a destroyed Greek monastery.

Further on it went along a high steep rock mountain, as we left the shore of the sea. From some places, in climbing up, one overlooks the greatest part of the sea, and here I discovered a considerable island in it, which was hitherto still unknown. This island deserved to be investigated. I took roughly the outline of the part which I could see. The part of the sea on the east side of the island I did not see, but my companions assured me it was an island.

The moon soon after rose in splendour over the fields of Moab.

On the shore, on the stones, were incrustations such as one finds in the graduation works of saltworks.

The Valley of Salt is without doubt a part of the wilderness of Zin by Edom (Numbers 34:3; Joshua 15:1).

On the southeast side of the sea dwell the Arabs Gau—(?); there too grows the Nil plant.

The first day we made nine hours, the second seven hours, and the third likewise seven hours of road. Today we stayed on the mountain by some rocks. In the night we suffered no water, only in the evening.



4 April (Friday)

Today we met many black flints and very unfruitful naked gravelly and stony mountains without cultivation. We rested at a place where there was a grotto. The Mohammedans and Greeks of Bethlehem ate meat; we, however, only bread.

On our left we saw Arabs. A wild swine sprang up. I also found on the mountains gazelle dung, which smelt like musk.

Afterwards we came upon more fertile, grassier, and flatter mountains, but likewise wholly without cultivation; and toward evening we came upon fourteen tents of the Arabs Schahalin, where all the Christians ate meat, we but dry bread with coffee.

The Arabs now demanded more Rdaffa or money from us for their escort.



5 April (Saturday)

Today we suffered lack of bread and were therefore hungry. At noon we travelled on, and sought wild herbs to eat. Juszef grazed the whole afternoon. We at last met two clusters of tents of twelve and sixteen tents; they had only cows, sheep, goats, asses, but few camels. Mountains and valleys are all as before. Two hours before sunset we met with some water in a small rocky hollow; the surface of the water was one and a half square feet, full of tadpoles and foul-smelling. This was quickly taken to mix the flour, and the dough baked in thick cakes in the ashes of gathered dry shrubs. An hour after sunset we camped on a mountain, made a fire, and baked bread. Unfortunately there was no water, for the wells near by were dry, which had not been expected.



6 April (Sunday)

Last evening we rested only a couple of hours and then continued our journey in the bright moonshine by night. We had still six to seven hours to Jerusalem. The lack of water was very oppressive for us; at last, however, one found water in a well, whereupon loud joy arose; but unfortunately it was too little to fill the lowered skin. Luckily there was further on more water in a well. A very cold damp wind blew, which brought a small shower of rain. Our people stood in fear of a band of Arabs, whom we had still to pass before day.

Before Bethlehem the ground was very rocky, but round Bethlehem very well cultivated. It lies on the steep edge of a rocky mountain of bluish limestone, as on the Lebanon, and looks well. The mountain sides have terraces, many olive trees, vineyards with low towers with loopholes, also several fields. In short, the whole pleases. We merely passed through, as we were going straight to Jerusalem. The road thither is also pleasant; everything is cultivated, and the grain stood well. We saw on the left, on a mountain slope, the village Bot Schala, various pilgrimage stations and places; on the right, halfway, a monastery Deir Mar Elias (Der Mär Elias); and came at last, in rain and wind, to Jerusalem, which one first descries when one is near it. It lies on a considerable steep-sided mountain, has good walls, and looks well from outside. The region is well cultivated; the Mount of Olives especially looks very well. The monasteries and churches too are stately.

We went to the Terra Santa monastery, where a couple of Mohammedans barred our entrance. We were led to the dragoman, who referred me to the Greek monastery, against which I protested. I was taken for a Bedouin. At last I was let into the monastery, and after many inquiries made, they convinced themselves that I was really the owner of the passport, etc. The procurator generale showed himself very obliging, and a room was assigned to me.

I had used the mule alternately with Juszef.



7 April (Monday)

Today the procurator paid me five hundred piastres.



10 April (Thursday)

Today was rainy weather.

Here and at Bethlehem many Catholic Christians speak Italian; some also understand a little Spanish.

The wonderful smoke of the Dead Sea, of which Büsching speaks, is very natural. The Arabs in the neighbourhood namely burn charcoal, soda, and sometimes lime.


* * *



Tuma's journey from Karrak to Jerusalem.

Printed p. 432, closing the diary.

1st day. At noon off, and at el Assr (afternoon prayer) to Robba, and stayed a half hour and a half from it, westward with the Beni Hamide. Plain.

2nd day. At noon onward, always among Arabs; at sunset to el-Hije, the name of a region where there is a small spring of running water. Mountains.

3rd day. Mountainous road; passed a small brook, where they rested. Afterwards on a plateau, where no water; station.

4th day. Many mountains. Stayed about half an hour from the sea and two hours from the Jordan. A sheep was slaughtered. Near by a spring, which flowed into the sea.

5th day. In the morning on; two hours to the ford of the Jordan; stayed four to five hours there; slaughtered sheep. Forty to fifty sheep and goats, with a Hamide Arab, drowned. Tuma crossed this time on a horse, led by an Arab; the horse walked, and the water reached over half its body. Before, the Jordan had been much higher. They drew on from there, and stayed an hour northward of Ribha.

At Maggreb (sunset prayer) they received news that robbers had murdered someone, and that they were in danger of being attacked by them. Therefore they broke up at once and drew on through two-thirds of the night; one-third they slept.

6th day. Immediately after they drew on, and two to two and a half hours after sunrise were at Jerusalem.
