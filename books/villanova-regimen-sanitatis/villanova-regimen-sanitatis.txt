DEDICATION

**Who he was.** Arnold of Villanova (c. 1240–1311) — Catalan physician, theologian, and prophet; professor of medicine at Montpellier, and physician to kings of Aragon and to popes: Boniface VIII, Benedict XI, Clement V.

**His circumstances and death.** The masters of Paris condemned his prophetic writings about the year 1300, and he spent his last decade as physician and diplomat to King James II of Aragon. He died in 1311, in a shipwreck off Genoa, while on a diplomatic mission for the king.

**Why his work is vital — his logic proved, not praised.** The Regimen is his most practical and most humane book: short sections of dietetic advice written for one man — King Jaume II — but built to serve any reader. Its logic is proved in the structure itself: health is kept by the six non-naturals — air, food and drink, sleep and waking, motion and rest, retention and expulsion, the accidents of the soul — before it is ever restored by medicine. The physician's first work is to teach the patient how to live: what air to choose, when to exercise, how to eat, what to drink, how to sleep, how to govern anger and make room for joy. No theory without a rule; no rule without a reason. That is the whole of it, and it has not aged.

**His relation to this rescue.** The physician-prophet whom Paris condemned and the sea took has waited seven hundred years for an English rendering of his most humane book — none found after research. The Boneyard rescues it here as the source gives it: the king's regimen, which became every reader's.

# THE REGIMEN OF HEALTH FOR THE KING OF ARAGON

*by Arnold of Villanova*

## PROLOGUE: ON THE CHOICE OF AIR

[The works of the mind — whether in understanding or in judging — are performed more clearly and keenly in pure air.] By this consideration of preservation, the king's prudence is not a little exalted: that he provide well for the public good by procuring clearness of understanding — for native industry shows itself not in the mind alone but in the whole counsel. From it flows the influence of government over the whole kingdom; toward procuring what good, as far as it is possible for him, the king himself is bound by the duty of his own majesty. For it is shown not only by native teaching but by daily experience that impurity and thickness of air dulls the wit, blunts apprehension, darkens judgment, makes thoughts foolish, and twists the affections.

Therefore, for dwelling long in one place, let him choose where the air is purer in substance, and temperate in its qualities, as the seasons require. In summer let pure air be sought, and the surroundings of the dwelling declining to moderate cold; and contrariwise in winter. But where the air is pure, there spring or autumn exists in temperate qualities — for it often happens that where the air is pure it is shaken by the blowing of winds. Then the thin body is easily harmed by sudden changes, since the wind lightly penetrates to its inward parts; and when it blows as through a channel, it is expedient in windy places to be covered with thick clothing. For the choleric and the sanguine, clothing of linen or silk lined with cotton is fitting. Such clothing does not inflame the blood nor make it boil, and it protects the parts around from the winds' assault.

In the chamber set apart chiefly for lying and sleeping, take care that the air not be shut in, and that it have free breathing-holes — not like certain vaulted houses, which have few and small breathing-holes — nor rheumatic ones, like cellars whose floor is not paved. For in such dwellings the air is too thick and moist, and those lying, especially sleeping, in them gather much in the head and chest; it makes them dull, sluggish, and drowsy, constricts the heart, hardens the breath by making breathing difficult, and takes away or obscures the voice.

Let care be taken in every house that the air be thinned and purified with fire — made mostly of vines [vine-cuttings] that smoke but make a clear flame. Let the room be strewn and hung about with green things; let only the floor be watered; and in summer let the window be shut through which air may more freely enter. If it must be strewn with green plants, take care they not be watery, like coarse rush — but good soft herbs: myrtle, willow, or the like. Such a house may in winter be fumigated with juniper wood, or cypress, or aloes wood, or incense; in summer with sandalwood, or dried myrtle leaves, or its twigs, or dried roses — with which also, if there is plenty, the floor may be strewn, or small bags filled and hung about near the bed's pillow.

When cold compels the use of the fire's benefit, take care not to come too near it, nor to sit long at it — especially after food, and with the face toward it. For it impoverishes thin bodies of spirit and hinders their digestion; it dulls the sight, sharpens the blood, and causes dizziness. But with the back turned to the fire, let its benefit be taken moderately. If it is before the eyes, and especially near, it is fitting to put something before the eyes close by.

## CHAPTER II: ON NATURAL EXERCISE

If the superfluities have been perfectly expelled in the past — whether gross superfluities, like those of the intestines and bladder, pass from some bodies without exercise, or subtle ones which are driven out through the pores of the skin (which for the most part gather in the hollows of the limbs and between skin and flesh, and are seldom drawn from a healthy body except by strong effort, since a man seldom sweats, and it is most rare that anyone lives or eats so sparingly that the natural heat can consume the members' superfluities by their fewness, or is so strong that it can lay waste whatever superfluities there are) — it is fitting, then, to exercise before food. But for those who eat little, little exercise suffices; for those who take rich meals, more is needed. For those who exercise little, a small light meal suffices; those who use much strong bodily exercise need a rich gross meal. But before lunch and supper, let moderate uniform exercise come first.

Moderate is that by which a man is made more cheerful and his limbs lighter. For if pains or weariness remain in them afterward, without doubt it was immoderate — and so, when weariness begins, one must stop. Uniform is when the lower and upper parts are exercised proportionally — whether by walking, or riding, or performing some other exercise. Those who exercise by walking ought to bend somewhat to gathering something pleasant with the hands, as if working. Riding — whether mounting or dismounting or managing the horse — moves not only the columns of the feet but the back, the hands, and the arms, especially if he gives himself to hunting.

But that kind of motion should be chosen for exercise by which royal gravity and decency may always remain unharmed. Therefore the game of ball or of the lance, or wrestling with companions, in no way befits royal decency — for it renders the person himself contemptible, and detracts too much from public dignity.

## CHAPTER III: ON THE BATH

After the consideration of exercise follows the consideration of the bath. Therefore, to supply a defect in the regimen of health, let the steam bath be given to those in whom, by omission of exercise and rich feeding, a multitude of superfluities gathers in the hollows and in the regions between skin and flesh — or to those for whom, by multitude of sweat in exercise or some other heating, it is necessary. Thus the steam bath is fitting for some to provoke sweat; for others the bath [is for washing] after [it].

For a temperate body abounding in blood, if it eats moderately and exercises moderately, it is neither fitting to sweat nor necessary to wash for the traces of sweat. But if, from sweat provoked by its exercise, some need of washing should follow, take care that the body not be plunged [into cold] — for exercise should naturally come before food, for two reasons. The first is that it stirs the natural heat, by which the digestion of food is hastened — and it is expedient that the food taken find the heat awake, not drowsy. The second is that it resolves the body's superfluities and prepares their expulsion — for while they remain in the body, nature cannot perfectly perform its actions on the members, since the natural heat is hindered and the passages blocked through which the nourishment should pass.

In the heats of summer, roses boiled in a single boiling are put into the bath.

## CHAPTER IV: ON THE WASHING OF THE FEET [AND THE HEAD]

Let the feet be washed [often for cleanliness]. And for [the sake of] sight and hearing and memory, let [the head] often be washed and fomented with moderately warm water [?]. Let such washing be done in the evening, about bedtime, on those days when it does not happen that he sups.

Washing of the head should not wait beyond twenty days, nor be done more than once a week — and never on a full stomach, but before lunch, or long after; or if supper is planned, [wash] before supper.

## CHAPTER V: ON THE CONSIDERATION OF THE MEAL

The consideration of the meal is twofold in reason. The first is that exercise empties the body, and the emptied body needs a meal. The second is that exercise, in emptying, provokes appetite for food — which we call hunger, which is nothing but nature's plea for the necessity of eating.

For in a healthy body, then, let this be the rule of the times: that drink be taken when the thing itself requires it — that is, when one suffers thirst. And this is true thirst: when in the mouth and throat one perceives dryness — which never happens in healthy bodies except from food. Hence even such thirst is a plain warning that drink, sober and such as the body's nature and the season require, ought to be taken.

Likewise, he needs food who plainly hungers. Therefore it is fitting to treat in general, since nature briefly shows it: [a body well-complexioned and abounding in blood needs food and drink by this rule — that food be taken when hunger comes.] For nature embraces in the hungry what it does not appetite but spits out in the full — and food taken without appetite is borne by nature like a heavy pack, and is corrupted in the members rather than turned into them. For when nature spits it out or strives to turn it into its own substance, it corrupts it.

From this it is plain that in healthy bodies, in whatever season of the year, there is no other fitting hour of eating — whether at lunch or at supper — but when nature appetites to eat. Common observation of the evening meal testifies to this: that in the healthy it does not come from need of nature but from light supposition, from a perverse and indiscreet popular custom. Food must therefore be taken when nature appetites, and after eating, taking must be delayed — unless the Church's holy constitutions, or honest business, or religious devotion sometimes suggests that the hunger itself be borne in measure.

Measure must also be kept in eating: that eating not burden the stomach by tension with food. For as it harms to take food before the aforesaid [hunger], so it harms to take immoderately. [Bad] chewing — by which great injury is done to nature — is not redeemed by motion or by the heat and dryness of the air. Second, that food swallowed imperfectly is digested imperfectly: the body applies itself to many passions(?) in order to digest, and what is digested is turned into the members...

Therefore, for the moderately fed, it is not fitting after food taken to walk or labor or even to keep awake — especially if the body does not abound in natural heat. ## PRIMACY NOTE

No published English translation of Arnold of Villanova's *Regimen sanitatis ad regem Aragonum* was found after research. A 2014 Wake Forest University M.A. thesis (Toyin Ola) translated only the first section of the work — and from Jerónimo de Mondragón's 1606 Spanish version (*El maravilloso regimiento y orden de vivir*), not from the Latin — expressly stating that "no published English translations are known to exist." This is stated as a research finding — "none found after research" — never as a claim of being first, oldest, or only.
