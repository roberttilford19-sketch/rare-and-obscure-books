Song 89. Nikola, Bogdan, and Angelina

Chicks are crying, raven chicks,

And a fair song is being sung.

Are they chicks, or are they people?

They are neither chicks nor people;

They were seventy slaves.

Young Nikola enslaved them,

And shut them up in dark dungeons.

A letter fell from a wing;

Angelina looked at it,

And on the little feather was written:

"Bogdan is in dark dungeons."

Soon the dear one took wing... [she hastened]

How loudly the seventy slaves cry out,

That it is heard up to the blue sky:

"As we eat and drink,

Is there no one to rescue them?

Where have they put your brother Bogdan?"

All the slaves they released;

Only Bogdan they left behind,

For he has a fair wife,

And becomes a young queen.

"What torments is your brother in?

Take the razor quickly;

Shave my fair hair for me;

The younger brother will shave his hair;

Leave a lock and a braid;

Plait my long hair;

Tie it up with little threads at the back;

I myself will go:

Young Bogdan I will set free."

When Bogdan cried out,

It echoed up to the sky:

"Is there nowhere anyone

To come here to the gate?

For I have a letter to write

To my wife Angelina."

The grey dove heard him;

Bogdan gave out his right wing;

Bogdan wrote on the wing.

The dove flew away,

Flew to Bogdan's level courtyard;

With its wings it trembled,

As with Arab teeth;

With its eyes it looked down.

Angelina was sweeping the courtyard;

She turned her eyes to the whiteness,

She turned her mouth to the sky.

Then her brother-in-law spoke to her.

She cried out on her horse;

Dust and mist rose.

As soon as the deliya saw her,

Close the gates quickly —

On his horse he will ask for horseshoes —

Three hundred Hungarian zlatitsas!"

When Bogdan passed by,

She went with the horse and parted them;

She beat him with a golden mace;

He flung him into the middle of the courtyard,

Then the brother-in-law said to her:

"Now you are like a deliya.

And put out his eye.

When the slaves were freed,

When she came to the gate,

And spoke to Bogdan:

Song 90. Korun, Kondofila, and Her Son Kostadin

Korun loves young Kondofila;

He has loved her for nine years.

He went from morning to morning,

He went from evening to evening,

Until he had raised up her son Kostadin.

Then Korun grew frightened;

Then Korun grew wary.

Young Kondofila sent him word:

"O hey, Korun, sworn brother!

Why do you not come, as you used to come?

Has my fortune wearied you?"

Then Korun the highwayman says:

"Kondofila, sworn sister!

My fortune has not wearied me;

But I have grown very frightened

Of your son Kostadin:

They tell me he is more of a hero than I."

Then young Kondofila says:

"O Korun, sworn brother!

Has that care found you?

I will lie down sick of nafola [a feigned sickness];

I will lie for a week,

Without bread and without cold water.

I will send him into the green forest,

To seek healing water for me;

I will send him into the forest unarmed,

That you may wait for him, and destroy him,

And we may be as we have been."

Young Kondofila fell sick,

Fell sick, sick of nafola,

And lay for a week,

Without bread and without cold water.

Then she speaks to her son Kostadin:

"Alas, mother, alas, dear mother!

Tell me what you are sick of:

Are you sick, mother, of God?

Are you sick, mother, of an enemy?

Are you sick, mother, for priests?

Are you sick, mother, for doctors,

And for healing waters, mother,

That I seek for you all manner of bitters?"

Then young Kondofila says:

"I am not sick, my son, for doctors;

Only go into the green forest,

And seek healing water for me.

But I beg you dearly, my son:

Do not take your father's weapons!

Weapons are not taken for health's sake."

Her son Kostadin set out,

To go into the forest unarmed.

His sister learned of it,

And his sister said to him:

"Where are you going, my dear brother?

Where are you going into the forest without weapons?

Mother is not sick of God;

Mother is sick of nafola.

They are sending you into the forest unarmed;

There Korun the highwayman is waiting for you,

To destroy you, brother.

Come, let us go home;

I will give you my father-in-law's weapons."

Her brother went to his sister's,

And took his father-in-law's weapons.

And his sister said to him:

"Now go, brother Kostadin!

May you do it, may God help you;

Do not seek the healing water;

Seek instead Korun's head,

And bring it to our mother."

Her son Kostadin went off,

Went off into the green forest.

He found Korun the highwayman;

And Korun the highwayman said to him:

"Welcome, Kosta, good hero!

I have been seeking you often by torchlight;

I have found you now at noon.

Come, let us try ourselves!"

Kosta the good hero flew into a rage;

He spurred his swift horse,

Drew the folding sabre,

And cut him through the middle.

At that moment Korun did not yet know it;

He spurred his swift horse,

And rushed after Kostadin.

When his horse broke into play,

He fell down — he became two corpses.

Then Kostadin dismounted from the horse,

Took Korun's head,

Bound it in a white shirt,

And hung it behind him on the horse.

He turned back to his sister:

"Come, sister, let us go home!

What you told me, sister, I have achieved;

I could do it, and God helped me:

I have brought Korun's head."

They went to their mother,

And son Kostadin cried out to her:

"Here, mother, healing water for you!"

And he flung Korun's head at her:

"Love him, as you loved him!

You sent for him; I have brought him to you —

Though I went into the forest unarmed."

He drew his sabre; he cut his mother down.

He did not take her to the church to bury her;

He flung her into the white Danube.



Song 91. Gino the Arnaut and Fair Petra

Gino the Arnaut became betrothed,

Became betrothed to fair Petra.

He did not stay a day, nor a week;

He went off to the white fold.

God strike Gino's mother dead!

For she sent him the fiercest words:

"You are sitting, Gino, my dear son,

You are sitting at the white fold,

And you do not know that your white Petra

Has fallen in love with the village tax-gatherers —

Has fallen in love with the Turkish guards!"

Gino then flew into a great rage;

He did not come to his mother;

He went instead to Petra's house.

He found her in the wide room:

She had fallen asleep, and covered herself —

Covered herself with a white shirt,

And the dew had settled on her face.

Gino crept softly into the room;

White Petra did not know him.

He uncovered her face,

Wiped the dew off with the shirt;

He wiped it, and kissed Petra.

Petra still did not wake.

And he reached into his white pockets;

He took out a Frankish knife;

White Petra he destroyed in her sleep.

And Gino came to his mother,

And told his own mother:

"Petra, mother, has done me —

Done me a great dishonor;

And I, mother, have destroyed her."

Then Gino's mother said:

"Why, my son, did you not tell me?

Petra has done nothing to you;

It was I who deceived you!"

Gino the Arnaut flew into a rage;

Gino destroyed himself as well.

And he said to his mother:

"As Petra's mother will grieve,

So you, mother, shall grieve for me."



Song 92. Nikolche

Where has it been heard or seen,

That this is holy Sunday —

A son drags his father into court,

To pray to God?

Like Nikolche with his father?

Nikola reached out from the horse,

Bound his hands behind him;

Twice he bound his hands fast.

And he drove him before the horse.

His father says to Nikola:

"Where are you driving him?"

"May God grant, my son,

To the kadis, to the police officers,

That you turn back again!

One holy Sunday —

May your wife turn to stone,

And your little children to snakes —

May they hiss around the stone!"

They went and went,

And came to a new spring.

Nikolche dismounted from the horse;

Nikola went in to them,

To wash his eyes.

And he knocked at a gate;

No one opened the gate for him.

His father says to Nikolche:

"Nikola, son Nikola!

Unbind my hands,

That I may wash my eyes."

He looked up through the glass:

His wife had turned to stone,

And the children to colored little dragons.

Young Stoyan fell sick;

He neither dies nor gets up.

His mother says to Stoyan:

"My son Stoyan, Stoyan!

As you lie there dying,

Dying and not rising —

He has lain nine years;

He has rotted nine beds,

Song 96. Yankula

An army has surrounded Yankula,

From Stankamen as far as Bichica;

And Yankula has no news of it at all;

He just sits in the painted room,

And loves his little male child.

When young Yankulitsa came out,

Climbed up on the high divan,

Looked about her over the green forest —

What a wonder, and a great one, she would see!

An army has surrounded Yankula:

Horse by horse, like a thick forest,

And heroes like tall pines,

Sharp sabres like lightning,

And rifles like small stars,

And banners like small clouds.

And young Yankulitsa turned back,

Went into the painted room,

And says to Yankula the hero:

"Get up, get up, my master!

Leave your little male child,

And come out, outside to the outer court:

What an army has surrounded you!"

And Yankula the good hero arose,

Went out of the painted room,

Climbed up on the high divan,

Looked about him over the green forest —

What a wonder, and a great one, he would see!

For he saw the army spread out.

And Yankula the good hero cried out,

And called to his mistress:

"Yankulitsa, young mistress!

Bring me the three-hundred-fathom sash;

Bring me the folding sabre,

That folds among nine folds,

Every fold a priceless stone,

That cuts trees and stones,

That is worn in the white bosom;

Take out my six-winged arrow;

And go down into the earthly stables;

Saddle my painted horse for me;

Girth him with nine girths;

Under his mane, a yellow mace."

Young Yankulitsa brought them to him.

And he mounted his swift horse,

Went in into the midst of the army,

And asked Yankula the good hero:

"O you young standard-bearers!

With what thought will you make war?

Do you seek Marko Kralyevik,

Or do you seek Yankula the hero?"

And the young standard-bearers say to him:

"We do not seek Marko Kralyevik;

We seek Yankula the hero."

"If you seek Yankula the hero,

I will find Yankula the hero for you.

Draw yourselves up on two sides!"

He deceived the young standard-bearers;

They drew themselves up on two sides.

Yankula the good hero smiled to himself,

And says to them: "Come, let us try —

Am I a hero, that I should leap over you?"

And he spurred his swift horse,

Drew out the folding sabre,

Swung it to the right side,

And cut down the young standard-bearers;

Turned the swift horse to the left:

Not a living soul was left to him.

And Yankula went into his courtyard,

And went into the painted room,

And saw young Yankulitsa,

Where she weeps, where she sheds tears —

Sheds tears down her white cheeks,

Wrings her hands from her white aprons.

Then Yanko the good hero says to her:

"Why are you weeping, young Yankulitsa?

If you do not believe it, go down into my courtyard,

And see my swift horse:

The horse is all swimming in blood.

And see my sharp sabre:

My sabre is all blunted now."

At once young Yankulitsa arose;

She did not go to see the swift horse;

She climbed up on the high divan,

Young Yankulitsa looked about her,

Looked about over the green forest —

Might she see the army encircling?

She saw there was no army encircling.

She came quickly down into the wide courtyard,

And saw that swift horse:

The horse all swimming in blood;

And she saw the blunted sabre.

Then she took faith in this:

That Yankula had destroyed them all.



Song 97. Yankula's Death

Yankula, young Yankula,

Yankula Voivode the Wallachian!

Yankula fed his horse

In that city of Thessaloniki,

At the inns of Thessaloniki,

On fine rice straw.

Yankula watered his horse

On Negosh wine, on Blagush.

Yankula gave his horse grain

Of great[-city] wheat.

Yankula shod his horse

At the farriers of Thessaloniki;

He begged the farriers:

"O farriers, dear brothers!

Shoe my horse well for me;

Lay kara-grosh horseshoes,

Set in golden nails."

Yankula made his saddle

At the saddlers of Thessaloniki,

Of pure silken broadcloth.

Yankula made his bridle,

Grosh-leather straps

At the goldsmiths of Thessaloniki.

His horse speaks to him, says:

"Why do you beat me at the manger?

Will you sell me, then?

Or will you trade me

For another, finer horse?"

Yankula speaks, says:

"O my horse, my comrade!

Neither do I sell you, horse,

Nor do I trade you, horse.

Word has come from the Wallachian banner:

I am to go to Kosovo,

Kosovo, the wide field.

A Turkish horde has arisen,

A Turkish horde, a mighty army;

I will go even there."

He mounted the horse and set out;

He went to Kosovo field,

Went into the Turkish horde,

Drew out his sharp sabre,

And set himself to cutting.

As he went into the midst of the horde,

A rifle cracked — Yanko fell.

His horse stood over him;

His horse equipped, girthed;

His horse made a divan for him:

With its mane it made him shade,

With its tail it brushed away the flies,

And shed small tears;

And there it too fell beside him —

Beside him it fell, and gave up the ghost.



Song 98. Mati

Mati, o Mati the widow!

Mati was left a widow,

Left with three little children.

Mati wondered in wonder,

With this heavy wealth,

What to do, what to make of it.

Mati speaks to the villagers:

"Villagers, you headmen!

What is fine in the world —

Fine and fortunate —

Mati will make it."

The villagers said to Mati:

"The finest thing in the world,

Fine and fortunate,

Is to build bridges for yourself:

Over the bridges, Mati, will pass

All sorts, Mati, and all sizes,

Old, Mati, and young,

Lame, Mati, and blind."

Mati did not listen to the priests;

She asked the prisoners:

"What is fine in the world —

Fine and fortunate?

Mati will make it."

And the prisoners said:

"Fine, Mati, in the world

Is to build yourself a dungeon:

In the dungeon, Mati, will lie

All the fine young heroes.

So Mati bought up

All the dungeon-masons,

And they set to building the dungeon.

Nine years they built it:

By day they built, by night it fell.

Then her veil fell down;

Her son Ivancho

Fell into the dark dungeon.

Then the dungeon held firm;

Then the dungeon was built.

In the churches, Mati, will come

Every kind of faith —

Small, Mati, and great,

Old, Mati, and young,

Lame, Mati, and blind."

Mati did not listen to the villagers;

She asked the priests:

"What is fine in the world,

Fine and fortunate?

Mati will make it."

The priests said to Mati:

"Fine, Mati, in the world..." [The two-column page tangles the counsels — churches, bridges, dungeon; the widow builds the dungeon, and her own son falls into it, and then the walls stand. — Ed.]



Song 99. Stoyan and the Sultan

A mother sat down to eat

With nine born sons —

To eat, and also to drink.

Their old mother asked them:

"Sons, nine sons!

As you eat and drink,

What shall I ask you?

Whatever is dear and precious to each —

That, my sons, I will do for him."

Stoyan spoke to his mother:

"Mother, o mother, this is dear to me:

To walk in the green forest,

To wear a scarlet banner,

To gather a company for myself."

Ivancho speaks to his mother:

"Mother, o mother, this is dear to me:

Black buffalo to buy,

Black fallows to plow,

White wheat to sow."

Then Stoyan gathered his heroes;

Then Stoyan led them

Through the dense green forest,

Along the Sultan's border.

The Sultan sits under a tent,

And eats, and drinks;

He was eating roast lamb.

Stoyan seized the Sultan

As one traps a sparrow.

The Sultan speaks to Stoyan:

"Bravo to you, Stoyan!

I was once a hero;

You are more of a hero than I."

And he led the Sultan

To nine human graves,

And nine full of silver.

The Sultan speaks to Stoyan:

"Take, come now, Stoyan,

Take as much as you can —

And leave some for me,

For I am old; I cannot anymore."



Song 100. Lazar and Petkana

Where has it been heard or seen,

In that empty Zagora,

A woman to bear twins three times —

Three times, three boys each time!

The Zagora boyars rose up:

They were nine brothers,

And one sister, Petkana.

Petkana grew, grew tall;

She became a girl for marrying.

Suitors came for Petkana

From nine villages away.

Eight brothers will not give her,

Nor their old mother either —

Not until the nine born brothers.

Only her brother Lazar would give her:

He'd give her, he kept saying:

"Let us give Petkana, mother,

Into that empty Zagora!"

Since they gave Petkana away,

The black plague entered the house;

And all of them perished —

And one old mother.

Only there remained, remained

Petkana's little mother.

Every day at the graveyard,

Every day, every week,

There she dusked, there she dawned.

In the middle of the field, a tall tree;

On the tree, a little bird:

It bewails them all,

And gives out for their souls;

But it does not bewail Lazar,

Nor give out for his soul.

It ruined Lazar:

"Where has it been heard or seen,

The living to walk with the dead,

As Petkana walks with Lazar!"

Petkana speaks to Lazar:

"Brother, o brother Lazar!

Do you hear how the bird sings?

And go, sister, let us go!

Shall you be ruined, Lazar,

That you gave Petkana away

Into that empty Zagora?"

They went and went;

They came to the meadows.

Petkana speaks to Lazar:

"Brother, o brother Lazar!

All the meadows are mown;

Your un-mown ones are left."

Lazar was praying to God:

"God, o dear God!

Make me, God, fashion me:

My bed, a good horse;

My cross, a yellow flask!"

And God heard him;

And God made for him:

His bed, a good horse;

His cross, a flask of grain... [wheat]

Lazar speaks to Petkana:

"I fell sick, fell ill;

I could not mow them."

Then they came to the vineyards:

"All the vineyards are dug;

Yours are undug."

Lazar speaks to Petkana:

"I, sister, fell sick;

I could not dig them up."

They went and went;

They came to the church.

Then he cried at the little gate:

"Sister Petkana, Petkana!

Come out, sister, outside."

And Petkana came out;

She kissed his hand,

And spoke to Lazar:

"Lazar, brother Lazar!

Why is your ring —

Your betrothal ring —

Mouldy on your finger, brother?"

Then he took the ring off,

And handed it to Petkana;

Lazar speaks to Petkana:

"When mother asks you:

'Who brought you, Petkana?'

You tell her, Petkana:

'My brother Lazar brought me.

If she does not believe you,

Come to us as a guest.'

And show her the ring,

The betrothal ring."

Petkana got up to go;

They went and went;

They crossed the green forest,

Came out on the wide field.

Lazar went to the graveyard;

And Petkana to her mother.

Her mother stood at the gate...



Song 101. Marko, the Arab, and Marko's Wife

Marko was up on the divan;

He looked out over the white roads,

And spoke to his wife:

"O wife, my dear good!

What will the mists and dusts be,

Below Varosh, along the white roads?"

"O Marko, my dear master!

Can it be a great ambush for us?

See — the coiled gates are knocking."

Marko spoke to his wife:

"Go down into our courtyard,

And see what is knocking at the gates!"

Young Marko's wife went;

When she looks — a Black Arab.

"Do you hear me, young wife of Marko?

Is Marko Kralyevik at home?

I have heard he has drunk much wine,

And wagered on a wager.

If he wishes us to wager,

The two of us to drink wine:

If he out-drinks me,

I will give him my good horse;

I will give him my sharp sabre,

With the sabre to cut off his head.

If I out-drink him with the wine,

He shall give me young Marko's wife."

She ran up onto the divan:

"O Marko, my master!

Alone, she says, has the hunt come to us:

A Black Arab has come to us;

He wagers on a wager.

If you out-drink him with the wine,

He will give you his good horse;

He will give you his sharp sabre,

With the sabre to cut off his head;

If the wine out-drinks you,

You shall give him your young beloved."

"Open our coiled gates;

Let the Black Arab come in!"

They opened the gates of the courtyard;

He tied his horse in the long stable;

Then he climbed up on the divan.

They drank for three days and three nights;

In three days neither of them was drunk.

But Marko had grown weary

Sitting and drinking wine.

Marko spoke to the Arab:

"O Arab, you Black Arab!

Give me leave to walk a little!"

Marko walked a little in the courtyard;

Quickly he climbed the stairs;

He climbed up for wine that was burnt...

A chabur-cup full he drained —

A chabur-cup, seventy okas.

God strike the Black Arab dead!

He had put in bitter herbs;

And a drowsiness came over him, drowsiness felled him.

Marko lay down to sleep a little,

And would not easily wake.

God strike the Black Arab dead!

For he says to young Marko's wife:

"Dress yourself, young wife of Marko!

Good luck to you, who will keep house with me;

And good luck to me, with you — even better!"

"Thank God, the One God," she says;

"For you will deliver me

From this heavy drunkard!

Only wait, stay, while I gather

All my bridal clothes,

And take Marko's weapons!"

God strike the Black Arab dead!

He does not wait for young Marko's wife;

He leapt onto his hero's feet,

Ran straight to the lower stable,

Untied his own horse from there;

Then he untied Marko's horse too,

And mounted young Marko's wife.

He does not drive it as a horse is driven,

But flees like a strong wind.

They came near the city of Thessaloniki.

There young Marko's wife says:

"O Arab, o golden Arab!

Marko has sworn brothers here;

They will know me at once,

And will destroy you on the spot.

Take off your silken fair clothes,

And let me dress in them,

That I may seem a dread deliya to them;

And you — make yourself out to be a prisoner,

As if you had cut down many people,

Committed many sins in many places,

And I am taking you straight to Constantinople."

They went to a cold inn,

To an inn in the city of Thessaloniki.

Young Marko's wife proclaims:

"Hear me, citizens of Thessaloniki!

This fellow is a great offender:

He has shed so much blood along the roads;

He has done so many crimes, the fellow;

I am taking him straight to the Sultan.

Let no one destroy the Arab,

For no one gives me any answer!"

At night young Marko's wife arose;

She cut his head off to the shoulders.

In the morning, when it dawned,

The dread delibasha then cries:

"Do you hear me, citizens of Thessaloniki?

Of you I demand the Black Arab!

Who cut his head off in the night?

Understand it well, citizens:

I will not leave one alive here!"

But the citizens of Thessaloniki beg:

"We cannot, they say, find out

Who destroyed the Arab.

Demand of us whatever you will demand,

That we give it, and be quit of you."

"Then give me twelve loads,

All of them mules, with young muleteers;

So I forgive you the Arab."

The citizens of Thessaloniki gave them;

They gave everything she asked.

And she drove the twelve mules;

From Thessaloniki she turned back.

They came out below the city of Thessaloniki,

Below Thessaloniki, into the green meadows.

Marko Kralyevik met her.

Marko Kralyevik spoke out:

"Do you hear me, dread delibasha?

Are you going by sea, by water,

Or are you going by the white roads?

I will ask you: tell me something!"

The dread delibasha spoke out:

"Tell me, Marko, Marko Kralyevik!

What trouble, Marko, has found you?"

"Did you meet a Black Arab,

Carrying a fair bride?"

"O Marko, Marko Kralyevik!

I myself come from a far-off place;

I myself met a Black Arab,

Beyond Istanbul, thirty halting-places,

Where he was carrying a fair bride —

In her looks, like Marko's wife.

"O Marko, Marko of Varosh!

Have you no tsardom, man, to command?

Can you not find another for yourself?

She wanted a husband, and chose a husband!

Do you hear me, Marko Kralyevik?

Come now, Marko, turn back:

Drive the twelve loads for me;

Load them with my servant;

Load and unload them."

They set out along the white roads.

Here and there the woman drives him on;

She beats him with a three-stranded whip —

She beats Marko Kralyevik.

Here and there she tramples his heels;

From his heels black blood flowed.

They came down to Piroka,

Reached below white Pletvara. [The brothers' footnote: a village near Prilep. — Ed.]

Then again the dread delibasha —

Again she set about asking Marko:

"O Marko, Marko Kralyevik!

When you see young Marko's wife,

Could you, indeed, know her?"

Marko Kralyevik spoke out:

"Do you hear me, dread delibasha —

May no ill-will remain with you!

When you look at the raw earth,

You resemble young Marko's wife;

When you look at the fair sky,

Nowhere is there anyone more dread than you."

And they came into white Pletvara.

Young Marko's wife leapt from the horse,

Mounted Marko, her master:

"I am your young wife!

I forgive you my black blood,

And forgive you the three-stranded whip

That beat me on head, on forehead."

They went up onto the divan,

And unloaded the twelve loads of wealth.



Song 102. Marko Fasting

Marko fasted the Lenten fast —

Seven weeks; seven times he ate,

Without wine, without fierce rakia.

And Easter Day came;

Marko got up on Holy Saturday;

He dressed, adorned himself finely.

Marko's wife was dressing his horse,

And young Marko's wife says to him:

"O Marko, my lord!

Shall I lay on your sharp sabre,

Or shall I lay on the yellow mace?"

And Marko Kralyevik said to her:

"O hey, young wife of Marko!

I am not going to the Tsar's army;

I am not going anywhere to a wedding,

That I should take my sharp sabre.

I am going to the church of the Archangel,

To the Archangel's church, to communion."

God strike young Marko's wife dead —

Because she was wise and prudent!

She took the folding sabre,

That folds in twelve folds —

In length, twelve spans;

In breadth, it was four —

And laid the sabre on him secretly;

She laid it on the horse in its mane;

She kissed the horse's black eyes,

And said to the horse:

"Horse, horse, by your black eyes!

If you see Marko anywhere in trouble,

Speak to him of his sharp sabre!"

Marko Kralyevik set out,

To go to the church of the Archangel.

As he went into the green forest,

Gino the Arnaut met him.

And Marko Kralyevik said to him:

"Good morning, Gino Arnaut!"

"May it be good to you, unknown deliya!"

"Where to, Gino Arnaut?

Where have you driven your horse to?"

Then Gino Arnaut speaks:

"O hey there, unknown deliya!

I am going to Marko Kralyevik's.

Marko is in the church of the Archangel.

I will seize his sister Angelina for him;

I will do him a great dishonor.

For it is now three years' time:

I want her always, and he will not give her to me."

Then Marko Kralyevik said:

"Go in health, Gino Arnaut!

If you can do it, may God help you!"

When Marko had parted from him,

He grieved sorely and wept tenderly,

And spoke soft words with his horse:

"Alas, horse, alas, swift horse!

If only I had my sharp sabre,

To cut Gino down on the pathways!

He will do me a great dishonor;

He will seize my dear sister!"

God granted: the horse answered:

"O Marko, my lord!

Do not lay up great care for yourself;

Search instead in my mane:

You will find your sharp sabre there."

And Marko took his sharp sabre;

Marko drove his horse back;

And Marko Kralyevik said to himself:

"Alas, God, alas, dear God!

Now, God, help me;

Again I will fast the Lenten fast;

Again Easter Day will come,

And I will go to church to communion."

He overtook Gino the Arnaut,

And Marko Kralyevik said to him:

"Wait for me, Gino Arnaut!

Whose sister are you going to seize?

Come, let us try ourselves first —

Try ourselves in the green forest —

And afterwards, go seize the sister!"

Gino the Arnaut stopped.

They grappled with the sharp sabres;

They broke the sharp sabres;

But the heroes did not destroy each other.

They grappled with their slender arms;

And Gino the Arnaut seized him:

He seized Marko Kralyevik,

Buried him in the earth to the knees,

Once more, up to the waist —

Only to cut his head off.

God strike Marko Kralyevik dead —

Because he was wise and prudent!

What did Marko Kralyevik say?

"O hey, Gino Arnaut!

I fall at your feet:

As you wish, so destroy me.

Only I beg you dearly:

Take me down to the lower ground.

There is a young inn-keeper woman there;

I have drunk much wine there;

I drank it, and did not pay for it.

Destroy me before her eyes,

And give her eyes their pleasure,

For she is my chief enemy!"

Gino the Arnaut was deceived;

He flung the fine chain over him;

He flung him behind him on the horse,

And carried him down to the lower ground.

The young inn-keeper woman spied him —

She was not his chief enemy;

She was his sworn sister.

"Come, come, Gino Arnaut!

Are you carrying my asmi?

God forgive where you caught him!

For it is exactly three years:

He drinks wine, and gives me no money!"

Gino went onto the ground;

The young inn-keeper woman took him;

Marko she hid in the painted room;

And she serves Gino the Arnaut —

Serves him with fierce rakia;

After the rakia, lordly dishes;

After the dishes, three-year-old wine;

And in the wine, bitter herbs.

Gino the Arnaut got drunk.

And Gino the Arnaut said to her:

"O hey there, young inn-keeper woman!

I beg you: keep him well,

Lest our asmi run away;

For I will lie down and sleep a little."

And the young inn-keeper woman said to him:

"Have no care, Gino Arnaut;

I have him locked up!"

She gave him a painted cushion,

And flung a silken quilt over him,

That Gino the Arnaut might sleep.

As soon as Gino the Arnaut fell asleep,

The young inn-keeper woman arose.

She took her husband's sabre;

She opened the painted room;

She took off the fine chain,

And gave him Marko's sharp sabre:

"Rise now, Marko Kralyevik,

And destroy our asmi!"

Marko Kralyevik uncovered him,

Uncovered Gino the Arnaut,

And took the head from his shoulders.

And Marko Kralyevik said to her:

"Angelina, my dear sister!

Until now I have held you as a sworn sister;

From now on, as a sister born!"

They took Gino the Arnaut,

And flung him into the white Danube.

Marko went back home;

When the Lenten fast came again,

Marko Kralyevik fasted again. [So the 1861 printing ends the song. — Ed.]



Song 103. Todorka and the Kiahche

Word has come from the Tsar:

"Whoever has a son, let him send him;

Whoever has none, let him come himself."

Ivan had no son —

Only a daughter, Todorka.

He wonders and broods

How to do, how to manage.

When Todorka heard it,

She spoke to her father:

"Do not grieve, little father!

Until young Todorka is here,

You will not see an army.

Go now, father, go now:

Buy me a Frankish sabre,

And a slim boyliya rifle,

And a fed little horse;

And I will go to the army,

And become a young soldier;

No one will know me."

Todorka arose and went;

She went for seven years;

No one, nowhere, knew her.

The Tsar speaks to the kiahche:

"Take the army out for me

On the level field to sport —

To sport the hero's horo.

Look at them all in turn,

That you may know the maiden."

When it was on Lazarus' Day,

It came to Todorka's mind

That her mother is at the market,

Like her age-mates.

In the Tsar's garden she sang;

She sang, and chimed in;

And at night she sang this song,

Where the distaffs are sold.

The young kiahche heard her...

And he spoke to the Tsar:

"The kiahche took the army out...

'Here, Tsar, is a woman-soldier,

Who, on the night of Lazarus...'

Still she did not know the maiden."

And the Tsar speaks to the kiahche... [The two columns of the closing page tangle the end of this song; song 86 of this collection is its fuller twin. — Ed.]



Song 104. Stoyan and the Turk

Young Stoyan made a wager;

Young Stoyan made a wager

With the Turk from across the sea,

To drink rakia:

If Stoyan gets drunk,

The Turk shall take from Stoyan

Kalinka, his first-wed;

If the Turk gets drunk,

Stoyan shall take from the Turk

His black Arab horse.

Kalinka was their servant;

Stoyan pours out the cup [for himself];

The Turk pours behind his shoulder;

Until young Stoyan fell asleep.

"God strike you dead, Stoyan!

God strike you down, destroy you!

Now you are dreaming in your sleep:

The Turk has taken Kalinka away!"

When young Stoyan heard it,

He shed small tears,

And spoke to his mother:

"Saddle my little horse for me,

And hang up my sabre for me."

And he spoke to his little horse:

"Little horse, whirlwind-horse!

If you overtake the Turk —

To win Kalinka back for me —

I will silver-plate your head!"

Then he mounted his little horse,

And as he rode along the way,

The whistle whistles and speaks:

"Stay, wait, Turk!

Let the two of us wrestle,

And the two of us stab each other!

If you stab me,

Kalinka is yours —

Kalinka, my first-wed;

If I..." And his little horse righted itself;

Stoyan reached them;

Kalinka dismounted from the horse.

Kalinka, the cunning sprite,

And then spoke to the Turk... [The 1861 leaf ends the song mid-fight; the winning-back of Kalinka is its settled burden. — Ed.]



Song 105. Momchula Voivode

Voivode Momchula is feeding his horse;

In the evening he shoes his horse.

Then young Momchulitsa says:

"O master, Momchula Voivode!

I will ask you — tell me truly:

In the evening you shoe the horse;

By morning the horse is unshod."

Momchula Voivode answered:

"Do you hear me, you my bride?

Since you ask me, I will tell you truly:

In the evening I shoe the horse;

By morning the horse goes unshod.

I go off myself to the city of Thessaloniki;

There I love a Jewish maiden.

From there I go to the city of Belgrade;

There I love Mara of Belgrade.

And again I go to the city of Istanbul;

I love Tsar Kostadin's sister."

God strike young Momchulitsa dead!

She wrote a scrap of a letter,

And sent it to the city of Istanbul,

To that Tsar Kostadin:

"A curse on your life!

Momchula loves your sister.

Hear me, Tsar Kostadin!

You will not catch Momchula by heroism.

When he goes to the city of Manastir,

He is wont to go without weapons.

Gather a little something for yourself,

And choose out three thousand men;

Rise early on Saturday,

And you will arrive quickly on Sunday;

He will be in church at the service."

What did Tsar Kostadin say?

"You are a fine one, Momchula Voivode!"

And he gathered three thousand men;

And they mounted the swift horses;

Straight they pulled for the city of Manastir;

They arrived as church was letting out;

The people were coming out of the church.

And what of Momchula Voivode!

He mounted his swift horse,

And went out of the city of Manastir.

Momchula's horse trembled;

And Momchula said to his horse:

"O my horse, o good horse!

You have never done such a thing before, on other roads."

Then his horse answered him:

"God strike our mistress dead —

Lest she has done some betrayal!"

Then it came to Momchula's mind;

He looked about over the white roads:

Mists and dust-clouds were rising;

Armed men were surrounding him.

And again the horse spoke to him:

"You, master, are a hero, and I am a hero.

Mount on my shoulders,

And go into the first horde:

If we have no luck —

As God helps us."

The horse spurred like a strong wind;

The hero split the whole horde;

He raced up to his stone courtyard.

The coiled gates were shut.

He spied his dear sister;

And she calls, as loud as her voice goes:

"Quickly, dear sister, open for me,

That I take my weapons,

That I cut down those bastards!"

"O brother, my dear brother!

God strike your young wife dead!

When I was sleeping,

She smeared my fair hair;

She smeared it onto these posts."

"Pull, sister; another will grow;

For we are both lost."

And his sister flew into a great rage;

She tore her hair from the posts;

A tuft remained on the posts.

She raced into the painted chest,

Took out three hundred ells of cloth,

And flung the cloth across the wall.

He caught hold of the white cloth;

His sister pulls him:

"Pull, sister, if God helps!

If He helps, that we break free,

We shall make her mother weep!"

God strike young Momchulitsa dead!

She took a knife from her bosom,

And cut through the three hundred ells of cloth.

And the hero fell behind the courtyard;

Then his head broke on him;

Arms, legs, head were smashed.

Tsar Kostadin arrived;

He sat down on the silken bed,

And speaks to young Momchulitsa:

"O bride, young Momchulitsa!

Bring me Momchula's clothes,

The ones he dressed in on Easter."

She opened the painted chests;

She took out silk and velvet;

She took out the sable cap.

When the Tsar put it on his head,

The cap settled onto his shoulders;

On the cap were three hundred drams of gold.

"What else did Momchula have!"

She took out Momchula's boots.

When the Tsar saw the boots,

He pushed both his feet in,

And both his feet stuck fast.

Then the Tsar marveled —

That such a good hero had perished.

Then he cried out to his men:

"Seize young Momchulitsa,

Who betrayed her own master!

Lift her and bind her;

Bury her up to the knees;

Smear her with tallow and tar;

Set fire to her from her fair hair;

Burn such a witch!

And they set her alight; she burned up;

She became mists and dust-clouds.

Momchula's sister spoke out:

"O fortunate Tsar Kostadin!

Look on me, wretched me,

As I would grieve for my brother!

See the Tsar's blood from my head;

I have no care for my hair;

I have no grief from pain;

I do not even want my own life;

I only mourn my dear brother,

Who perished through a faithless wife."

His sister took him up;

Tsar Kostadin spoke to her:

"You are right, fair maiden!

As one salts, so shall one sup;

As one does, so shall one find.

Stay alive, and comfort yourself;

Another crop of hair, maiden, will grow."

He took the fair maiden with him,

And carried her off to his own courtyard.



Song 106. Gyuro

Two dragons are fighting on the mountain;

Two rivers of black blood are flowing;

And at the end of it, a black dungeon;

In the dungeon, Gyuro the good hero;

In his hands he holds a falcon-chick.

With his fingers he snaps, feeds the grey falcon;

He sheds tears, waters the grey falcon;

And Gyuro the good hero speaks:

"O you, falcon-chick!

I do not feed you to grow big,

That you may see my courtyard —

Deserted, overgrown with wormwood."

The falcon-chick answers:

"Last night I passed by your courtyard;

In the middle of the courtyard, a dry tree;

On the tree, three cuckoos.

The first cuckoo has gone mute;

The second cuckoo has gone hoarse;

The third cuckoo keeps looking."

Gyuro the good hero answers:

"The one that calls and has gone mute —

That is my old mother.

The one that calls and has gone hoarse —

That is my dear sister.

The one that calls and keeps looking —

That is my first love."



Song 107. Rada of Budin

From Panagyurishte.

"O Rada, white Rada!"

A Tsar is tsaring in Constantinople;

White Rada is in the city of Budin.

There were Turks; there were Greeks;

There were young janissaries;

They could not break

The new city of Budin,

Until the Tatar Pasha came —

The Tatar Pasha with the Tatars.

Twice they charged, three times they struck;

Petra Ban they destroyed;

Ivan they cut down;

White Rada they enslaved.

The loads shook loose;

The padlocks were smashed.

They bound her white face —

White face with a white band,

Black eyes with a black band.

They put her into a coach;

They made a Turk coachman,

To drive Rada's coach.

And Rada answers him:

"Turk, o coachman!

Undo my white face —

My white face from the white band,

My black eyes from the black band —

That I may look up and down,

Up and down along the horo,

Along the horo, along the girls,

In case I see someone

Of my dear kin."

They were going little and much;

They came beside the sea:

Moravian girls are dancing the horo.

And Rada answers him:

"Coachman, be my brother!

Give me a Wallachian knife,

That I cut this apple,

That I moisten my mouth."

The coachman gave her —

Gave her a Wallachian knife.

White Rada stabbed herself —

Stabbed herself in her poor heart.

The coachman answers:

"You will, Rada, see

Your poor kin, Rada,

When the willow bears grapes,

And the osier bears yellow quinces."



Song 108. Stoyan and the Little Jew

Young Stoyan made a wager,

Made a wager with a roast lamb...

He plied him with red wine,

Fed him full with white rice,

For he has a fair wife,

And a well-fed little horse,

That in a day goes to Istanbul,

And comes back again.

The Jew of another faith says to Stoyan:

"Stoyan, come now, Stoyan!

If you go and come back,

My shops are yours to keep —

Together with the little shop-boys.

If you go and do not come back,

I will take from you, Stoyan,

Your fair boxwood-fair wife,

With the fine, well-fed horse."

Then Stoyan mounted

That well-fed little horse;

He came out of the gate;

A Jewess met him face to face;

She handed him rakia.

It was not rakia;

It was a magic spell.

Stoyan dozed on the horse;

The sun trembles to set;

Stoyan spent the night in a village.

The horse speaks to Stoyan:

"Do you know, Stoyan, do you remember,

That I shall run hard?

Mountains I shall topple,

Stones I shall turn to dust."

Stoyan reached Istanbul,

And came back again.

The Jew stood under his shop,

Shedding small tears.

Stoyan — come now, Stoyan!

The Jew speaks to Stoyan:

"My... are yours to keep —

My seven shops,

Together with the little shop-boys."

He plunged his hands into his golden pockets,

Took out a silken kerchief,

And bound up his eyes [the Jew's, in token of the wager lost].



Song 109. Nacho

Nacho was left,

Left without mother, without father.

Nacho was an orphan,

For twenty-two years,

At strangers' doors.

And on the eve of Easter,

On holy Sunday —

People go into church;

Nacho tightens the plough;

He sheds small tears,

And prays to God:

"O God, dear God!

Is it not today —

Today is a feast-day,

Holy Sunday;

Everyone at Easter

Goes into church;

And I keep tightening the plough,

And yoking buffaloes."

He went to the field;

The first furrow he ran;

His buffalo ploughed,

And cursed them:

"Buffaloes, buffaloes!

You have not gone astray so much;

Now you have lain down on me."

And his buffalo speaks to him:

"Nacho, orphan Nacho!

Unhitch the plough;

For it has caught

On nine cauldrons

With golden coins."

Nacho unhitched

The empty plough

From the nine cauldrons

With golden coins.



Song 110. Brother and Sister

When there was captivity over the land,

Yankula was taken captive,

And he was carried to the upper land,

Where the sun rises.

Yanika was taken captive,

And carried to the lower land,

Where the sun sets.

Their mother remained

Between the Black Sea.

Yankula became

A good hero for marrying;

Yanika became

A good maiden for marrying.

Yankula mounted a carriage,

To travel a foreign land,

To seek a fair bride,

A bride his match against himself;

He drove off even a slave-woman,

To sing to his little male child.

He found nowhere a maiden to his liking,

Nor any married woman,

Nor any little widow.

Only Yankula went

Where the sun sets:

He found the maiden Yanika,

Betrothed her, and took her.

She stayed nine months;

She bore a little male child;

The child like a fierce snake —

It is not rocked, not held,

Song 113. The Fierce Serpent and Gruyo the Child

From Panagyurishte.

A fierce serpent has become famed,

A fierce serpent, a shade-dwelling serpent,

With three heads, with three tails,

On the Tsar's roads.

It blocked the Tsar's treasury road,

And the Tsar could not get past.

The Tsar wonders, the Tsar broods

What to do about the fierce serpent.

Word spread of three deliyas,

Three deliyas in a new village:

The first is deliya Petar;

The second is deliya Marko;

The third is a little child,

A little child, Gruyo the chief.

The fortunate Tsar answers:

"Call me the three deliyas,

To destroy the fierce serpent!"

Deliya Petar went off,

Walks the roads, sings a song:

"Wherever you are, fierce serpent,

Come out on the roads for me!"

The fierce serpent came out;

It raised one head;

It stretched out the horse's legs;

It raised a second head;

It bound his white hands;

It raised a third head,

To drink his black eyes.

Deliya Petar was frightened;

He butted his horse backwards.

Deliya Marko comes toward him,

Comes toward him, sings a song.

Deliya Petar answers:

"Turn back, deliya Marko!

That serpent cannot be destroyed —

That serpent, that power."

Deliya Marko passed him by,

And cried and shouted:

"Wherever you are, fierce serpent,

Come out on the roads for me!"

The fierce serpent came out;

When it saw the little child,

It raised one head,

And stretched out half of him;

It raised a second head,

And stretched out the horse's legs;

It raised a third head,

And bound his white hands.

The little child cried out:

"Hear me, God, and see me:

Free my right little hand,

That I draw my sharp sabre!"

Then he drew the sharp sabre,

And destroyed the fierce serpent;

He took its three heads,

And carried them to the Tsar,

To the Tsar's level courtyard.

And the Tsar answered them:

"O you, Gruyo, little child!

The capital is yours outright:

I will get up, and you shall sit down."

Deli Marko answers:

"Turn back, little child!

I could not destroy it —

Destroy the fierce serpent!"

The little child passed him by;

He cried and shouted:

"Wherever you are, fierce serpent,

Come out on the roads for me!"

The fierce serpent came out;

It raised one head,

Stretched out the horse's legs;

Raised a second head,

Bound his white hands;

Raised a third head,

To drink his poor heart.

Deliya Marko was frightened;

He turned back again.

A little child comes toward him —

A little child, Gruyo the chief,

Beating a tattoo (?) on his horse.

Song 114. Dimni Marko and the Turkish Voivode

Dimni Marko has come out

To walk a walk

Through that green forest,

My first enemy!

I beg you, give me —

Give me your sharp sabre,

"Angelina, first love!

Do not trouble yourself for me:

Cry out, sing out!"

Heavy drowsiness seized him,

Heavy drowsiness on his eyes,

Heavy sorrow on his heart:

Loud-voiced to the blue sky,

Singing over all the earth —

That the drowsiness pass from me,

That the heavy sorrow be scattered."

"Come now, Marko, master!

Here a song is not sung;

Here walks the Turkish Voivode,

She cried out a song —

Singing, loud-voiced;

Loud-voiced to the blue sky,

Singing over all the earth.

"Those are Angelina's voices!"

And they got up, and came down;

They came down from the forest;

They seized Dimni Marko,

Bound him in iron;

They drew out the sharp sabre,

To cut off his fair head.

Then Angelina says:

"Come now, Marko, master!

You begged me; give me —

Give me your sharp sabre,

That I myself destroy him,

That I cut off his fair head."

She swung, and cut through

When Dimni Marko got up,

He swung himself left and right;

He cut down the seymens;

Song 121. Marko and the Dukadin Child

Marko sat down with his mother to sup;

Over him is the Evening Star.

And Marko Kralyevik spoke:

"O hey, Evening Star!

You stand so high for me;

I will ask you something;

I beg you, tell me truly:

Have you anywhere caught sight

Of anyone to be found more of a hero than I?"

And the Evening Star spoke to him:

"O you, Marko Kralyevik!

If you want me to tell you truly,

Take your swift horse,

And go to the Dukadin land.

There is a Dukadin child there;

The child is seven years old;

Seven times more of a hero than you."

Then Marko smiled.

Then his old mother says:

"Why do you smile, my dear son?

Do you smile at my old age?

Do you smile at your child?"

Then Marko Kralyevik says:

"Do not touch me, mother — why do I smile?

I have a road to go now;

I will go to the Dukadin land.

Just bring out my swift horse."

Then he mounted his swift horse.

Before the first cocks had crowed,

Marko had swum the Black Sea;

By the time dawn broke,

He came out beside the white roads.

There he found three young ploughmen,

And Marko Kralyevik said to them:

"Good morning, three young ploughmen!

I will ask you — do not deceive me:

Where does the Dukadin child live,

Who is seven years old?"

The three young ploughmen answered him:

"O you, unknown deliya!

His own house is known:

His eaves are strung with pearls;

His... of yellow ducats;

His coiled gates are of steel;

His posts are of marble."

Marko went to his gates;

Once he knocks, and twice he calls:

"Come out, Dukadin child!

Let me see what child you are;

Let me see your heroism!"

The Dukadin child came out,

And the Dukadin child said to him:

"O you, unknown deliya!

It is not the custom in our land

To welcome friends on the road;

Come home instead, let us sit down,

Eat, drink a little;

And afterwards let us try ourselves."

Marko Kralyevik went.

They ate, they drank three days and three nights;

They got up on the fourth day;

They went into that level field.

Then Marko Kralyevik said:

"Come now, let us throw a stone,

To see our heroism —

Who will be a hero above heroes."

Marko took a stone to throw;

The little stone that he threw up,

He threw it exactly three hours [away];

They searched for the stone, and found it.

The Dukadin child threw up his;

The little stone that he threw up,

They searched for it three days and three nights;

Nowhere did they find the stone.

Marko Kralyevik flew into a rage,

And said to the little child:

"Come now, let us try ourselves

With sharp sabres, with the swift horse."

The Dukadin child was deceived,

And took his swift horse.

As he set the horse to dancing,

Marko Kralyevik rushed on him,

And cut him through the middle.

The child had not yet known it,

And the Dukadin child said to him:

"O you, unknown deliya!

Something pains me in my heart."

And Marko Kralyevik said to him:

"O hey, little child!

When I was a child like you,

Many times my heart pained me;

Little children — so their hearts pain them.

Just girth your swift horse tight,

That we try ourselves once more!"

As the child girthed his horse tight,

The horse raged, broke into play;

The child fell — became two corpses.

And Marko Kralyevik rushed down,

And took his good head.

He went to the seven kings;

He told them of the child's head —

That he had much heroism,

Seven times more of a hero than Marko.

The seven kings answered him:

"God strike you dead, Marko Kralyevik!

Why did you destroy this foolish child?

And may your grave be unknown!"

A ploughman is ploughing, the young farmhand;

They have done him a great dishonor!"

Marko was in the forest hunting;

He heard the young farmhand

Where he sings that grievous song,

"I beg you, child, tell me:

Have you heard, or have you seen,

Who has carried off Marko's bride?"

"O hey there, unknown deliya!

Gruitsa Novogov has carried her off."

Marko did not go back home;

He wanders through the green forest;

He found him in that Warm Forest,

Where Gruitsa the child is fleeing,

Where he is fleeing with Marko's bride.

"Wait, wait, Gruitsa, little child!

Marko Kralyevik drew near him,

"O hey, Gruitsa, little child!

Here the place was very level:

Come, let us make our horses dance!"

The hero fell — he became two corpses.

Marko Kralyevik cut him open,

To see in his heart

What child-heroism he would have,

That had done such great wonders,

To carry off Marko's bride!

He had twelve hearts;

All the hearts had fallen asleep;

Only one was awake.

He took the hearts; the hero let him go [left him].

There he found seventy kings,

"See Marko's heroism!"

The seventy kings answered him:

"God strike you dead, Marko Kralyevik!

This is not your heroism;

It is your villainy!

They drove him out; Marko fled.

Song 125. Marko and Murat-Beg

Tsar Murat-Beg is crying out:

"No Turk shall drink wine;

No Christian shall wear green."

Everyone obeyed the Tsar;

Marko Kralyevik did not obey:

He wears a green robe,

And by force made the Turks

Drink wine in Ramadan.

Tsar Murat-Beg heard of it,

And set three young criers.

They cry out down through the city of Istanbul:

"Whoever is a hero, born of a hero,

Let him seize Marko Kralyevik!

I will give him a great gift:

The city of Skopje beside the Vardar;

And Adrianople, the wide market-streets."

No hero undertook

To seize Marko Kralyevik.

Only a Black Arab undertook

To seize Marko Kralyevik —

Either to bring Marko back alive,

Either Marko, or Marko's head.

And he mounted his swift horse,

And rode through the city of Istanbul.

He drove on Greeks and Armenians,

To search for Marko Kralyevik.

Marko sits at a cold inn,

Eating, drinking, with no care at all.

He heard of the great pursuit;

He rose on his hero's feet;

He found the Black Arab!

Then Marko Kralyevik said:

"O hey, Black Arab!

What is this great pursuit?

Do you want something, or are you seeking someone?"

And the Black Arab said to him:

"I am seeking Marko Kralyevik;

Either to seize him alive,

Or to take his head."

Then Marko Kralyevik says:

"God strike you dead, Black Arab!

You are speaking with Marko, and you do not know Marko!"

He drew his sabre and cut off his head;

He took his horse;

He went to Tsar Murat-Beg,

And Marko Kralyevik said to him:

"O hey, Tsar Murat-Beg!

Whose head were you asking me for —

Marko's, or the Arab's?"

Then Tsar Murat-Beg said:

"O you, Marko Kralyevik!

As you have done, Marko, keep on doing;

Let no one hinder you."

And he gave him a great gift:

The city of Skopje beside the Vardar;

And Adrianople, the wide streets.



Song 126. The German Queen and Marko

The German Queen is writing a letter;

She is writing — to whom shall she send it?

She will send it to Marko Kralyevik;

She will send the letter by the raven.

Then the raven answered:

"O hey, German Queen!

I do not know Marko;

If I saw him, I would give him the letter."

Then the German Queen says:

"You are to go to the Ohrid nahiya;

Seek Marko in the inns;

By his toast you will know him."

The raven came to the Ohrid nahiya;

He found Marko in the inns;

By his toast he knew Marko;

He flung the letter onto his lap.

And Marko took the white letter;

He reads the letter; he sheds small tears.

What a wonder, and a great one, he would see!

The German Queen has sent:

"Are you alive, Marko, or are you dead,

That you have not come into our land?

It is now thirty years;

Black Arabs have come down;

We cannot hold a wedding;

The heroes have all grown old;

The maidens have all grown old."

Then Marko Kralyevik says:

"O hey, my faithful company!

Are you going, now to go —

To go into the German land,

To cut down the Black Arabs —

For it is thirty years

Since a wedding was held there?"

They rose from the cold inn;

They took Sekula the Child with them,

For he was handsome and little;

They made Sekula the bride;

They went into the German land.

The Black Arabs rushed down,

To carry off the fair bride.

They gave them the bride;

A great crowd gathered;

They went to look at the bride.

They went to fetch her and bring her back;

They went to them unexpectedly;

And they set about cutting them down:

They made blood up to the knees;

They cut down the Black Arabs —

Let not one of them remain.

They took Sekula the Child;

They went back to the Queen.

She gave them a great gift;

She gave them nine tower-treasures;

And she kept them a full three months,

That they might eat and drink.



Song 127. Marko as Godfather

Kings and Bans gathered;

Gathered at a cold inn;

They drink wine, they take counsel:

"Whom shall we set as kinglet over kings?"

Marko Kralyevik answered:

"Let us set Mitre the pauper,

For he has a fair bride,

For she is wise and prudent."

They called Mitre the pauper:

"We will set you as kinglet over kings;

You are fit to be our kinglet.

Charge your young Mitre's wife

To prepare a lordly supper,

That it suit as for kings."

Mitre the pauper wondered;

Mitre went straight home,

Sorrowful, very cast down.

Young Mitre's wife met him:

"O Mitre, my lord!

Why are you so very cast down?

What need has driven you?"

Then Mitre the pauper says:

"Young Mitre's wife, fair bride!

The Kings and Bans called me;

Called me to the cold inn;

Set me as kinglet over kings;

Sent me word that you prepare a supper,

That it suit as for kings.

O hey, young Mitre's wife!

We are very poor;

We have no wine and rakia;

We have not even a crumb of bread.

With what shall we make lordly dishes?"

Then the fair bride speaks:

"My lord, Mitre the pauper!

Do not take up care for that:

I will prepare a lordly supper,

Even if I take God's credit for it."

Then young Mitre's wife arose;

She took everything on God's credit;

She took wine and rakia;

She made a lordly supper;

And welcomed the Kings and Bans;

And set them at the honorable table.

At the very top sat Marko Kralyevik;

Beside him, Sekula the Child;

Beside him, the Voivode Detelin.

She set them a lordly supper;

She serves them wine and rakia.

The Kings and Bans eat and drink;

Between themselves they take counsel.

Young Mitre's wife, wise and prudent,

Took her little male child,

And flung him into Marko's lap:

"Welcome, Marko Kralyevik!

Welcome, honorable godfather!

I will take you by Saint John,

That you shave our little male child."

All the kings looked downcast;

All the kings looked at the ground;

They raced one another into their white pockets.

Marko gifted him up to three hundred ducats;

They all gifted the child,

Some counted, some uncounted.

When young Mitre's wife took him —

Took the child from the lap —

Marko plunged into his white pockets;

It is not known how many ducats he gave.

When they got up to go home,

They all gave great gifts.

"Go in health, Kings and Bans!

Go in health, our godfather!"

Afterwards young Mitre's wife arose;

She paid the credit everywhere;

And she came to keep more treasure

Than they had, to eat and journey lordly.

Such a wonder Mitre's wife wrought,

Because she was wise and prudent.



Song 128. Stoyan and Dimana

From Panagyurishte.

Stoyan was setting out from below —

From below, from the winter pastures.

Neither with a nosegay, nor with mittens,

Nor on a white cake...

All his kin sent him off —

His mother's kin, his father's kin —

The headmen with red wine,

The headmen's wives with white cakes,

The brides with mittens,

The girls with painted nosegays.

Dimka sat in the garden,

At her painted frame, embroidering,

And singing a fair song.

Stoyan does not look at his kin;

Stoyan peered through the fence —

And his knives fell

Into Dimka's little garden,

Into the scarlet tulip-bed.

Stoyan speaks to Dimana:

"Love, o Dimka, Dimana!

Get up, hand me my knives

Out of your little garden!"

Dimka, Dimana arose,

To hand him the knives;

She did not hand him the knives;

She gathered all sorts of flowers,

And twined a painted nosegay —

Of basil and of tulips —

And handed it to Stoyan:

"Here, Stoyan, this nosegay!

By day wear it in your bosom;

By night lay it at your head,

That you may know, o Stoyan,

I will wait for you until spring —

Until you come, young Stoyan,

From below, from the winter pastures —

That we may take each other."

Stoyan speaks to his mother:

"Mother, my little mother!

Come, mother, settle it:

I will be betrothed.

Let me have Dimka Dimana.

When her nosegay withers..."

"Settle it, mother; get me married!"

And his mother speaks:

"My son Stoyan, Stoyan!

She is not, my son, for us;

For a fiery dragon loves her."

Stoyan speaks softly:

"Love, o Dimka, Dimana!

Wait for me, love, until spring,

Until you come from below —

From below, from the winter pastures —

That we may take each other."

"Go, and ask, dear mother;

Ask for her; let them give her.

I myself am the fiery dragon."

And Dimana speaks to him:

"O Stoyan, Stoyan!

I would have waited for you until spring..." [The 1861 leaf ends the song on Dimana's answer; the dragon-suitor is Stoyan himself. — Ed.]

Stoyan speaks to Dimana:

"Love, o Dimka, Dimana!

You made a great speech:

That the fiery dragon loves you;

You do not go with the girls to the horo;

You do not go with the young brides for water."

Dimka, Dimana obeyed;

With the girls she does not go to the horo;

With the young brides she does not go for water;

She waited for him until noon —

Until young Stoyan came.

Song 130. Kalina, Yovan, and Stoyan

Stoyan speaks to his mother:

"Be damned, mother,

Kalina's enemies,

Kalina's, and mine too!

For they slandered Kalina to me —

That she is sleepy, drowsy,

And lazy at her work;

She milks the cows and dozes;

She sweeps the house and..."

Three drops of blood dripped

On Kalina's shoes,

And on my bosom.

Kalina, clever and wise,

Plunged her hands into her right pockets,

Took out a white kerchief,

And wiped the blood away —

The blood from the shoes,

From Stoyan's bosom.

And a grief remained with him;

So young Stoyan fled away,

And fled far away —

Across the Danube, into Wallachia.

From Kalina's griefs [she grieved for her].

And he settled nine years,

And gathered much wealth.

And young Stoyan bought

All sorts of small presents;

And went back home,

And spoke to his mother:

"Mother, my little mother!

Are you well, then, are you alive?

Is Kalina well?

Is the bride fair?"

His mother speaks to Stoyan:

"My son Stoyan, Stoyan!

We are well, and we are alive;

And Kalina is well;

And the bride is fair;

Since you, my son, married,

She has become twice as fair.

She shines more than the clear sun;

She gleams more than the bright moon.

My mouth split

Into three, four pieces."

And I bent down, mother,

To put the shoes on her —

"Stoyan, my son, Stoyan!

She will not come to us;

She is not forgiven by her godfather;

She has not returned from her mother;

For your bride-man has come..."

"Go, my son, you go to them."

Young Stoyan obeyed;

He went off to Kalina's.

He took a golden flask;

He invited the kin;

And they came to him as guests.

And Yovan says to them:

"Faithful company, covenanted!

I have something to ask you:

I have a green field;

A fallow stag has got the habit

Of trampling my field.

Shall I destroy the fallow stag,

Or shall I reap the field?"

No one had guessed it;

Young Stoyan had guessed it,

And speaks to Yovan:

"Sworn brother Yovan!

Do not listen to sworn brother Yovan;

Only he asked for Kalina;

He asked for her and they gave her to him.

I begged him, mother:

'Sworn brother Yovan!

Call me as your bride-man,

To be bride-man to Kalina,

To Kalina, to my beloved!'"

Young Yovan obeyed him;

He calls him as bride-man and leader:

"It was their custom, mother:

The bride-man goes in front,

To lead the bride out,

To put the shoes on her.

And I went in front,

To lead the bride out.

As I went into the house,

Then I saw Kalina:

Kalina had fallen asleep in the courtyard;

Fine dew had broken out on her.

Young Stoyan wonders and broods

How to wake her.

He was loath to wake her himself.

She woke of herself,

And spoke to Stoyan:

"You are welcome, my little brother!

Come, let us go into the house;

Young Yovan is not here."

And they went into the house;

They prepared, they supped.

Young Stoyan left

His knives on the pillow.

And now young Yovan comes,

And knocks at the gates.

And Stoyan leapt up, and fled;

He forgot his knives —

Knives on the pillow.

When young Yovan saw them,

He speaks to Kalina:

"Love, fair Kalina!

Dress yourself up fine;

Make yourself fine and adorned;

Put on purple cloth;

Over the purple, scarlet;

Over the scarlet, golden.

Then fill the golden flask;

Then it lit them for three days...

If the stag comes to you,

Do no harm to it:

(The thought left on the stag?)

'Rather reap the field;

Do not destroy the fallow stag.

If you destroy the fallow stag,

Another will get the habit,

Of trampling your field!'

Then young Yovan arose;

He seized Kalina,

Smeared her with black tar,

Wrapped her round with white raw cotton,

And set young [Kalina] alight:

She lit them for three days." [The two-column pages braid Stoyan's old love for Kalina into the wedding song; the sense carried here is as the columns give it. — Ed.]



Song 131. Dimana Carried Off

"O you, Dimana, poor Dimana!

Poor Dimana, an only child of her mother!

God strike your mother dead,

Who woke you early on Sunday,

Washed your head, braided you,

And sent you to the harvest —

To reap barley, to gather bread —

At two paths, at two roads!

God strike the wretched Gurbets dead!

They passed along the road; they carried you off;

They took you to a Gurbet village;

They kept you for three years,

In summer on the sun, in winter on the threshing-floor;

They made you a wretched Gurbet woman.

You bore three little male children.

And the wretched Gurbets speak:

"O you, Dimana, poor Dimana!

We will send you off — tell us truly:

Do you know your village —

Your village, your father's house?"

And poor Dimana spoke out:

"O you wretched, bad Gurbets!

I do not know my village —

My village, my father's house."

The wretched Gurbets... lied;

They gave her two staffs in her hands,

Two staffs in her hands, a... on her shoulder,

That she might go from village to village.

She pulled straight for her own village;

Straight she pulled to her aunt's:

"Grant me, aunt, by Peter,

By Peter, by Paul!"

"Off with you, wretched Gurbet woman!

How do you know my sons?"

From there she went to her mother;

She stood at her door:

"Grant me, mother, by Dimana!

By Dimana, your daughter,

Whom the wretched Gurbets carried off!"

And her mother spoke out:

"Off with you, wretched Gurbet woman!

How do you know my daughter —

My daughter, poor Dimana,

Whom the wretched Gurbets carried off?"

Then poor Dimana spoke out:

"O my mother, you old mother!

I am your poor Dimana,

Whom the wretched Gurbets carried off!

Do not look on my black face;

The wretched Gurbets blackened me.

They kept me for three years,

In summer on the sun, in winter on the threshing-floor.

Only look at my breasts:

Do the three white moles still stand?"

Then her mother knew her;

Knew her, and embraced her —

Her own daughter, poor Dimana;

And for joy the two of them wept;

As they wept, so they fainted away.



Song 132. Anka of Shchip

Petre carried off, carried off —

Carried off Anka of Shchip;

Took her far away —

Far away, to the German land,

Into a German monastery;

And laid on her the Christian name:

They named her Angelina.

She stayed three years;

She bore three little male children.

Her brother Ibrahim learned of it:

"I will sell my houses;

I will mortgage my fiefs;

I will buy your brother back."

Anka of Shchip speaks:

"O my dear brother Ibrahim!

Do not sell your houses;

Do not mortgage your fief;

I have changed my faith;

I have three little male children.

The Christian faith is sweeter;

The Christian faith smells

Like white basil at dawn,

Like a red rose."



Song 133. Nikola and Maria

Nikola speaks to his mother:

"Give me, mother, my knives —

My two knives, daggers —

That I go to Maria's,

To see whether she has got up,

Whether she has lit the lamp,

Whether she is sewing on a painted frame,

Of sleeves?"

Her frame is a table;

The grey falcon is Nikola;

The golden bell is her voice;

The two nets are the priests,

Who will marry you.

Bird-lime, bird-lime sleeves?"

Maria's mother was waking her:

"Get up now, my son Maria [daughter Maria],

And light the lamp for yourself,

To sew on the painted frame —

Yet do not take Nikola,

For they call him a drunkard;

He runs wild in wine,

And goes astray in rakia.

Do not speak of it, maiden Maria:

He never drinks wine,

And you are always a boyar's lady."

Maria speaks to her mother:

"Mother, my little mother!

Old you are, and foolish you are;

You do not realize in your sleep

That your wedding is coming;

Your frame is finished;

The gifts you have not sewn."

Maria speaks to her mother:

"The gifts be damned!

I will not take Nikola,

For they call him a drunkard;

He runs wild in wine,

And goes astray in rakia."

She embroidered on the painted frame,

Embroidered a grey falcon

With twelve stitches;

On his neck, a golden bell;

On his wings, two nets.

"Do not grieve, Maria:

That is a sign for good,

For you are to be married:

The grey falcon is Nikola;

The golden bell is the groomsmen;

The two nets are the priests,

Who will marry you."

Maria speaks to her mother:

"Mother, my little mother!

Old you are, and foolish you are.

Do you not realize that Maria will die?

Do you not realize about your son —

That Maria will die?

What sort of son have I reared?"

Nikola caught her,
