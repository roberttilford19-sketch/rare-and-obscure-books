Song 173. Temishvar Gyuro, Marko Kralyevik, Yankula Voivode, and the Bare Child

Temishvar Gyuro walks a walk;

Walks a walk over the Tsar's land;

Walks a walk for three years' time,

To choose a maiden to match himself.

He went round seventy cities —

Seventy and seven cities;

Nowhere did a maiden please him —

Personal and fair to match himself.

When he went to the city of Slankamen,

There a maiden would please him.

He betrothed her, and gave his ring;

Gave his ring: three loads of treasure.

They made their word: within one week —

Within a week they want a heavy wedding.

And what of Temishvar Gyuro!

He mounted his horse Karaman.

Then Temishvar Gyuro says:

"Do you hear me, my old mother?

Knead me two white loaves;

Draw me two measures of wine.

I will go to the city of Prilep;

I will set Marko Kralyevik as godfather;

The elder bride-man: Yankula Voivode.

I know, mother: I have betrothed myself —

Betrothed myself from the city of Slankamen."

Then his mother says:

"Thanks be to God — a change I shall see!"

She tucked up her skirts and her sleeves;

Kneaded two white loaves;

Drew two measures of wine.

And what of Temishvar Gyuro!

He invited two sworn sisters,

To knead cakes for the wedding.

And he took two white loaves;

And he took two measures of wine;

He mounted his horse Karaman.

As godfather he invites Marko Kralyevik;

As elder bride-man, Janko of Sibin;

And he has no younger bride-man,

Who will lead the young bride.

He asked his mother:

"Do you hear me, my old mother?

Teach me, as God has taught you,

What I shall do for a little bride-man!"

Then his mother says:

"My son, Temishvar Gyuro!

Mount your horse Karaman;

Set out along the white roads;

Whatever you meet, my son, whatever you overtake,

Seize a sworn brother by Saint John's brotherhood,

And make him the young bride-man."

Fair the mother, who taught her son!

Fair the son, who obeyed his mother!

And Temishvar Gyuro mounted —

Mounted his horse Karaman;

Set out along the white roads.

There is no hero to meet him;

There is no hero to overtake him.

When he went beside the salt sea,

There met him a child — naked, barefoot,

Bareheaded, the Bare Child —

Up to his arms, dug into the sand,

Where he plays with small stones.

Temishvar Gyuro passed him by;

He went a little way, and at once it came to his mind,

As his mother had taught him:

"When, my son, you find, when you meet [someone] —

Choose him as bride-man!"

Temishvar Gyuro turned back,

To the child, the Bare Child:

"Good morning, Bare Child!

Will you go, that I make you my sworn brother —

A sworn brother by Saint John's brotherhood?

This week I am to be married, young;

Be my younger bride-man!"

The Bare Child was puffed up:

"Off with you from here, unknown deliya!

Do not be joking with me!

If I lift up a cold stone,

And strike you between the two eyes,

Your eyes will spurt out of your head!"

Temishvar Gyuro swore:

"I am not joking with you, brother!

It is not, brother, that I jest with you:

So help me, brother, Saint John's brotherhood!

And so help me the four fasts,

Which we fast once in the year —

The heaviest, the ... fasts!"

The Bare Child believed him;

He came out of the cold sand.

And what of Temishvar Gyuro!

He flung him on the horse behind him;

He carried him off to his own courtyard;

He opened the painted chests for him:

"Look," he says, "Bare Child:

Which clothes will please you?

Those clothes, child, you shall wear!"

He chose clothes to match himself;

The Bare Child dressed himself.

Then he let him into the painted room,

Where the heavy weapons stood:

"Look, child: which weapons do you want?

Those weapons, child, you shall gird on!"

The child chose a mace —

In weight it was a light one hundred and sixty okas;

He chose that sharp sabre:

In length, sixteen spans;

In breadth, up to four spans.

Then he let him into the horse-stable:

"Look, child: which horse do you want?

That horse, child, you shall mount!"

The child went into the horse-stable,

To try the horses for riding.

The horses pull hard at their halters;

He pulls horses, and flings them out into the courtyard.

When he came to the horse Karaman,

He pulls — the horse does not stir.

He chose the horse Karaman;

He knew him: fiery, headstrong —

Seven times he had gone to war;

He had struck with the Black Arabs;

He lies down face-first on his knees.

He saddled him with an inlaid saddle;

Bridled him with a gilded bridle;

Girthed him with twelve girths.

And they unfurled three silken banners:

They will go for the young bride.

The Bare Child pulls in front;

Of them all he is clearly the better hero —

Like a billy-goat in a dry flock.

They set out along the white roads;

They will pass through a green forest.

There met them a dread calamity —

A dread calamity: Hala-Haletina,

Haletina, the Black Arabin.

His lower lip strikes his chest;

His upper lip strikes his forehead.

He has a head like two drums;

He has eyes like two bowls;

He has a mouth like a little gate.

He has teeth like four pitchforks;

He has legs like Thessaloniki pillars.

When that empty mouth claps,

Fire flies out of his mouth;

Over the forest it pours out the leaves.

Then the Black Arabin says:

"You are going for a young bride;

When you come back again,

Whose shall the young bride be?"

And the child's eye did not blink;

The child pulls along the white roads;

They went off for the young bride.

And what of the maiden's mother!

To one a kerchief, to another a ...:

To Marko she gave a heavy mace;

To Yankula, a chestnut switch(?);

And to the child she gave nothing —

Because he was like a stranger.

They gave him the young bride —

To guard her from evil on the roads —

Because he was clearly the better hero.

And they beat three pairs of drums;

They set out along the white roads.

Marko Kralyevik pulls in front;

And after him, Yankula Voivode;

After Yankula, Temishvar Gyuro;

And after Gyuro, the garlanded wedding guests.

They will pass through the green forest,

Where the Black Arabin sits —

Sits on the wide road.

He sits naked, ungirt;

And the Black Arabin speaks:

"Do you hear me, garlanded wedding guests?

Fling down what has been gifted to you,

If you want to come out of it alive!"

And what of Marko Kralyevik!

He was a hero, but he was frightened —

Because Hala-Haletina was dreadful.

Marko first flung down his gifts;

And after him, Yankula Voivode;

They had all flung down their gifts;

Gifts they flung, and all fled away.

Temishvar Gyuro fled with them;

From fear, Gyuro forgot

That he had a young bride behind him.

The Bare Child was left alone —

Left alone with the young bride.

Then the Black Arabin says:

"Fling down, child, what they gifted you,

If you want to stay alive!"

The Bare Child spoke out:

"You fine Black Arabin!

To me they gave nothing;

They gave me the young bride,

To guard her from evil on the roads.

I give my head; the bride I do not give."

The Black Arabin spoke out:

"Give me, child, the young bride!

If I spit, I will drown you all;

If I blow, I will carry you all away;

I have no sabre to dip in blood;

If I gape, child, I will swallow you."

The Bare Child spoke out:

"Stand up, fine Black Arabin!

Stand up, fine one, let us try ourselves!

Let us see which of us is more of a hero —

Whose mother bore more,

Whose sister rocked more!"

The Bare Child waited for him,

Until the Arabin caught his horse.

He caught the fat horse —

The fat horse, the forest-breaker horse.

And what of the Bare Child!

The child drew [his mace] and stood at the mark.

The Arab flung his heavy mace;

And what of the child's horse!

The horse lay down face-first on its knees;

Rolled about on the green grass;

The mace passed over the child;

And nothing at all befell the child.

The Black Arabin spoke out:

"Wait, child, that was a feint —

Wait, child, let me fling once more!

I have just now flung badly, child."

The Bare Child spoke out:

"You fine Black Arabin!

One mother bore me, one only;

I will fling first; you will fling after.

God is fair: as He gives to us —

Either to you, man, or to me."

The Arab saw it could not be otherwise;

The Arab drew [his mace] and stood at the mark.

The Bare Child was cunning;

From down low he measures his mace.

The Arab's horse: fiery, headstrong.

The child flung with his heavy mace;

The Arab's horse fell to its knees;

He struck the Black Arabin —

Struck him in his wide chest.

The Arab fell from his horse to the ground;

The Arab fell; the child attacked him;

He drew his Damascus sabre —

And on the sabre, twelve mirrors;

The sabre shone like the summer sun.

When he swung with that sharp sabre,

He cut off the Arabin's head.

From the force, the sabre slipped from his hand;

It cut through seven beeches;

And still nothing had happened to the sabre.

Then he went under the Arab's tent;

There he found thirty pack-horses of treasure —

Thirty pack-horses, laden with treasure.

All of them were bridled;

As he was about to take the young bride,

He would go off to the Arab land.

The child took the Arab's head;

He put it in a bag — the bag would not hold it;

He flung the head into a ... (?).

He led off the young bride;

He led off thirty pack-horses of treasure;

And he led off the Arab's horse.

Straight he pulls to Gyuro's gates;

He stood outside the gates;

The Bare Child listened:

He hears Marko, where he is blessing:

"Until now we drank for joy, for mirth;

And from now on, for the child's soul —

For our child has perished."

The Bare Child flew into a rage;

He drew out his heavy mace;

When he struck those heavy gates,

He tore the doors out with their hinges;

He drove the doors into the middle of the courtyard.

He unloaded thirty pack-horses of treasure;

He loosed two good horses;

Loosed the horses, to walk about;

He climbed up on the high divans;

He rolled the Arab's head —

Rolled it over the honorable table.

And what of the garlanded wedding guests!

They fell face-down on the table.

And what of the Bare Child!

He seized Marko Kralyevik:

"Come here, man, Marko Kralyevik!

Since you cannot guard a bride —

What business have you going as godfather?

Let me teach you, that you may be taught!"

He struck him fifteen maces.

Then he seized Yankula Voivode:

"Come here, man, Yankula Voivode!

Since you cannot guard a bride —

What business have you going as bride-man?

Let me teach you, that you may be taught!"

He struck him sixteen maces —

Maces of one hundred and sixty okas.

Then he seized Temishvar Gyuro:

"Come here, man, my sworn brother Gyuro!

Since you cannot guard a bride —

What business have you with a bride at all?

Let me teach you, that you may be taught!"

He struck him twenty maces.

Then the young bride says:

"Strike, brother, as much as you like;

Only mind you leave them their souls;

Lest you break, brother, some bone of theirs!

Three times, brother, I have been turned back

By that Black Arabin —

Lest it happen a fourth time!"

And what of the Bare Child!

He arrayed the garlanded wedding guests in order;

Arrayed them at the honorable table...

Whoever can bear a mace —

To one, five; to one, three maces —

Let no ill-will remain with any one.

Then he gathered them at the honorable table;

Gathered them, to drink wine.

He swung the heavy mace over them:

"If you do not want to drink wine,

Once more I will array you —

Array you with the heavy mace."

They all sat down, the garlanded wedding guests;

All sat down at the honorable table —

One from fear, and one for love's sake —

Sat down to drink wine.

They ate and drank for three weeks' time.

The wedding was, brother; it went by;

The godfathers and wedding guests went off.

And what of the Bare Child!

He loaded thirty pack-horses of treasure;

He mounted the Arabin's horse:

"Stay with God, my sworn brother Gyuro!

What we asked for, God has granted us:

To you, the bride; and to me, the treasure!"

The child went off to his own courtyard;

And the child built lodgings;

And the child, young, got married.

From the heroes the song has remained —

To be sung, to be told of.

That, brother, was from long ago;

Had it not been, it would not be famed.

What we have heard was all merry —

All merry, and in health from God;

They were all in health, in order, in ranks.

Song 178. Gyuro the Little Goldsmith and the Pasha of Ohrid

From Prilep.

"O Gyuro, Gyuro, Gyuro the little goldsmith!

Gyuro the little goldsmith, his mother's only one!

God strike the Pasha of Ohrid dead,

Who invited you to Ohrid, to a wedding!

A toast he sent you — fierce rakia."

And Gyuro's mother speaks to him:

"O my dear son, Gyuro the little goldsmith!

That toast is not dear to me —

That toast of fierce rakia,

Which the Pasha of Ohrid sent you."

Gyuro the little goldsmith says to her:

"O mother, mother, aged mother!

You have grown old; you have grown foolish.

This is what he has; this is what he sent."

And Gyuro the little goldsmith made himself —

He made himself a silver carriage —

A silver carriage, golden wheels;

And he made himself a golden apple;

And he mounted his old mother;

And he mounted his young bride;

God strike the Pasha of Ohrid dead!

As soon as he saw Gyuro the little goldsmith,

At once he came out at the gates,

To welcome Gyuro the little goldsmith;

And the Pasha of Ohrid speaks to him:

"O Gyuro, Gyuro the little goldsmith!

They took her into the women's courtyards.

That very hour, they gifted him:

To Gyuro the goldsmith, golden straps;

"O Gyuro, Gyuro, my dear son!

The gifts they have gifted are not dear to me."

And Gyuro the little goldsmith spoke out:

"Hush, do not speak, you old mother,

Lest the Pasha of Ohrid hear us!

If he hears us, he will destroy us."

His speech was not yet finished,

When the Pasha of Ohrid arrived;

And he seized Gyuro the little goldsmith;

And cut him up, piece by piece.

And Gyuro's mother was left —

Gyuro's mother, an old slave-woman;

And his bride, a young lady.

Song 180. Mitre and Petre

Two brothers made a division;

Over nothing did the brothers quarrel;

They quarreled over the city of Kamena.

Mitre says: "My father granted it to me."

Petre says: "My father left it to me.

As your father is to you, so is he to me."

And Mitre, the elder, arose;

He took his brother Petre;

He flung him into a dark dungeon;

He kept him for nine years.

God strike young Mitre's wife dead —

Because she was wise and prudent,

She quickly looked in on him.

What did she bring him every evening?

She brought him one white candle;

Every week, a white loaf;

Every week, a white change of clothes —

As long as he lay in the dark dungeon.

Petre lies sound in the dungeon;

Mitre lies sick in the room;

He lay there up to nine years.

Then young Mitre's wife says:

"O Mitre, my lord!

You are not sick with a sickness from God;

You are sick from your sins —

What you did to your brother:

That you keep him in a dark dungeon.

As he is a brother to you, so he is a brother [to me?];

And he wants his father's wealth.

Why did you not give him his father's wealth?

Why did you fling him into a dark dungeon instead?

Until he comes out of the dark dungeon,

Until he gives you forgiveness,

There is no health for you anywhere."

Then Mitre says to his wife:

"God forgive where she laughed at me!

That I have forgotten my brother!

I do not believe my brother is alive.

Only — here, take my keys,

And take my dear sister with us;

Unlock the dark dungeon;

Bring out my brother's bones;

Bring them, that I may kiss them.

Perhaps God will forgive me,

And my soul will part [from me] —

For I have committed great sins."

And young Mitre's wife took them;

She took the keys of the dungeon;

She took Mitre's dear sister with her;

They unlocked the dark dungeon;

They brought out Petre the good hero;

They washed him, and dressed him anew;

They set him on the high divan.

When he saw his dear brother,

He arose on his hero's feet;

And his sickness healed that very hour.

They embraced each other, and kissed each other;

The brothers made forgiveness between them.

Then Mitre spoke finely:

"O Petre, my dear brother!

Since I have seen you once more with my eyes,

Come now, brother, I will marry you;

And never again shall we quarrel;

Never again shall we have a division!"

Petre bowed to Mitre;

He betrothed him, and married him;

They took him a fair bride.

Never again did they quarrel;

And they never again changed their division,

As long as they had life.



Song 181. Rabdul and Mirche

Mirche and Rabdul quarreled,

Over that land of Misir,

Over that land of Kamenica —

Kamenica, the horse-shoeing place.

Rabdul-Beg the voivode flew into a rage;

He flung Mirche into the dungeon.

Mirche lay up to nine years;

His hair grew to his waist;

His eyelids like swallows' wings.

Rabdulitsa, wise and prudent,

Did him great service:

Every day, a white loaf;

In the evening, one white candle;

Every week, a white change of clothes.

God strike King Latin dead!

He sent him a white letter —

A white letter, black-written:

"As you see best, Rabdul-Beg Voivode,

As you see best, come to me here,

That you may wed me my fair bride to me,

That you may baptize my foolish little male child."

Rabdul-Beg Voivode reads the letter;

He reads the letter; he sheds small tears.

"Whoever has gone to King Latin's —

No one of them has come back to me!"

He flung the letter over the high divan;

He wrings his hands from his white knees;

He sheds tears down his white cheeks;

From his cheeks onto his white apron;

From his apron onto the high divan —

Until he had flooded the divan.

His fair bride saw him:

"O hey, Rabdul-Beg Voivode!

What need has driven you,

That you wring your hands from your white knees,

Shed tears down your white cheeks,

Until you have flooded the divan?"

"How should I not wring my hands?

How should I not shed bitter tears?

God strike King Latin dead!

He sent me a white letter,

To invite me as honorable godfather,

That I wed him his fair bride,

That I baptize his foolish little male child.

If I do not go, he will come here;

He will enslave our rich wealth;

He will enslave you as a young slave-girl.

God strike him dead — God punish him!

I will go there; I will not come back."

And the fair bride speaks:

"My lord, Rabdul-Beg Voivode!

If only Mirche were a little younger —

As he was a hero, so was he wise.

You flung him into the dark dungeon;

Come, call him, that he come out of it;

At least let him teach us a lesson."

And Rabdul-Beg Voivode speaks:

"O bride, my dear good!

Of Mirche, only bones are left!

It is now nine years

I have forgotten him in the dungeon."

"Go, call into the dark dungeons!"

Rabdul calls into the dark dungeons:

"Are you alive, or are you dead?

For I have forgotten you in the dungeon.

Now come out of the dark dungeon!

Either now, or never — Mitre!"

And from inside, his brother Mitre [Mirche] —

He did not even open his eyes at him;

Nor answer him with his mouth.

Rabdul turned back, weeping.

And the fair bride went off:

"O Mirche, my dear brother!

Come out of the dark dungeon!

By the service I have done you —

I have done it for nine years:

Every day, a white loaf;

In the evening, one white candle;

Every week, a white change of clothes!"

The younger Mirche spoke:

"O bride, my dear sister-in-law!

I am no man to come outside:

I have hair all the way to my waist;

And my eyelids like swallows' wings."

Rabdul-Beg Voivode spoke:

"Come out, younger brother!

I will set three young barbers on you;

I will cut your hair to the waist.

He will enslave our rich wealth!"

And the younger Mirche came out;

He set three young barbers on him;

He shaved his hair to the waist;

He trimmed his swallow-wing eyelids.

And the younger Mirche speaks:

"Does my falcon-chick still stand [by me]?"

The chick had not even smiled at him;

At once the chick flew into his lap.

"Come, give me letter and inkwell!"

They gave him letter and inkwell.

He wrote to his companions:

The first was Pletikosa Pavle;

After him, Gruitsa the Child;

After him, Sekula the Foolish;

After him, Gyuro the Drunkard.

"As you can, be found in the courtyard — [the brothers' note: find yourselves.]

For the younger Mirche has come;

He is gathering his soul, and will die."

And the falcon-chick went off;

As he told them, so they mounted their horses;

They found themselves in the courtyard, before the chick.

They ate and drank three days and three nights.

And the younger Mirche speaks:

"O you, faithful sworn brothers!

For the living brotherhood we have had,

I will send you with my dear brother;

I will send you to King Latin's.

He has invited him as honorable godfather,

That he wed him his fair bride,

That he baptize that little male child.

If he does not go, he will come here;

He will enslave our rich wealth;

He will enslave my dear sister-in-law,

To carry her off as a young slave-girl.

I do not grieve for the wealth we have;

I grieve only for my dear sister-in-law —

For I have lain nine years,

And she has done me service.

Only go [the brothers' note: go] to King Latin's,

Lest the king destroy my brother;

And do not disgrace your heroism."

Song 182. Yankula and Nikola

Two brothers made a division.

The younger, Nikola, said to him:

"O Yankula, my dear brother!

We will divide the inheritance:

You have a fair bride;

You have a foolish little male child.

I leave you the rich wealth;

I leave you the fields and meadows;

I leave you nine tower-treasures.

I want for myself nine stable-horses;

I want for myself two grey falcons —

That I walk through the green forest;

That I walk over the wide land."

Yankula's heart was not sated

By all the wealth left to him;

His grief fell only on the horses.

And he arose early in the morning,

To go into the green forest;

He took his two grey falcons;

And he charged his wife:

"O wife, my first love!

I will go into the green forest.

You prepare lordly dishes;

You call my dear brother in;

You serve him with fierce rakia;

After the rakia, lordly dishes;

After the dishes, three-year-old wine;

After the wine, a cup with poison —

That you poison my dear brother,

And all the wealth remain to us."

Then young Yankulitsa took them;

She prepared lordly dishes;

She called Nikola in for dinner;

To serve him with fierce rakia.

And young Yankulitsa said to him:

"O Nikola, my dear brother!

Let me tell you a great wonder:

What your dear brother told me —

That I should poison you.

And I do not agree to that —

To destroy you, good hero."

Then Niko the good hero said:

"Since it is so, my dear sister-in-law —

We have now feasted finely —

I will lie down on the divan to sleep;

You sit beside me, and weep;

Cover my white face;

Cover it with cotton cloth;

Light ... candles;

Loosen your fair hair;

Lament me, lament me, with a loud voice.

When my dear brother comes,

Let us see, brother, what he will do."

As she learned the teaching,

So young Yankulitsa did.

And Yankula wanders through the forest;

He hunted no game at all.

He spied a crested eagle;

And he loosed one grey falcon at it,

To fight the crested eagle.

The grey falcon answered:

"O Yankula, my lord!

Loose my brother too for me;

For I cannot fight him alone."

And he loosed the other falcon too.

Then the crested eagle said:

"Alas, God, alas, dear God!

What evil is this that we have done!

While we were two brothers together,

No evil ever found us;

No one ever fought us.

Yesterday we parted as brothers;

Yesterday they bent my brother with an arrow;

And today, me with the falcons —

They will take me, and destroy me!"

Then Yankula took thought with himself —

Of what he had charged his wife:

To poison his dear brother.

"These are chicks in the forest;

And they too are brothers who have fought each other.

How shall I, wretched, live!

A curse on all my wealth,

When I shall not have my dear brother!"

He spurred his swift horse;

He went home at a gallop.

What a wonder, and a great one, he would see!

Yankulitsa sits on the divan;

She laments Niko, her dear brother.

Yankula took his sharp sabre —

To destroy himself.

God strike young Yankulitsa dead,

Who took her steel ...;

On the ... she caught his sabre for him;

He did not destroy himself.

And young Yankulitsa said to him:

"Do not destroy yourself, Yanko, my lord!

What you told me, I did not do it;

Your brother has only fallen asleep."

And he went onto the divan, to his brother:

"Get up, get up, my dear brother!

I have sinned against you; forgive me!"

The two brothers embraced each other.

And Yankula said to Nikola:

"Come now, my dear brother;

Let us divide the wealth exactly!"

And Nikola then said to him:

"Brother, let us not divide the wealth;

Let us instead be brothers;

Let us sit as we have sat."

The Tsar Sultan Selim writes a letter.

To whom will he send it?

He will send it to Salakim Todor,

From whom he asks four gifts:

The first gift — his fair bride;

The second gift — his crested banner;

The third gift — his swift horse;

And the fourth — the village of Kalofeo.

Salakim Todor reads the letter;

He reads the letter; he sheds tears like hail.

Young Todoritsa saw him;

Young Todoritsa said to him:

"O Todor, my lord!

What need has driven you,

That you read the letter and shed tears like hail?"

Then Salakim Todor says:

"O hey, fair bride!

If I told you, what would that do for me?"

And young Todoritsa said to him:

"If I have nothing I can do for you,

As you grieve, so shall I grieve."

And Salakim Todor tells her

What the Tsar Sultan Selim has sent —

That he asks him for four gifts:

The first gift — his fair bride;

The second gift — the crested banner;

The third gift — my swift horse;

And the fourth — the village of Kalofeo.

"All the gifts I could grieve to lose;

But how could I grieve to lose you!"

And young Todoritsa said to him:

"O Todore, my lord!

Do not take up that care at all!

Just send him a white letter —

A white letter, black-written:

'O hey, Tsar Sultan Selim!

You ask me for four gifts;

I give you [instead] four stones!'"

As she taught him,

So he obeyed his bride;

He sent him back a white letter.

When the Tsar read my letter,

Then he flew into a great rage;

He sent Turks, Janissaries;

They dug trenches beneath the earth;

They went into the ground-floor cellars,

To steal young Todoritsa.

Todor sat on the high divan,

To eat his lordly dinner,

With his dear old mother;

And he holds their little male child.

And young Todoritsa came down,

To draw three-year-old wine.

When she saw a wonder, and a great one!

In the cellars: Turks, Janissaries —

And the Turks are about to seize her.

And young Todoritsa said to them:

"O hey, Turks, Janissaries!

Are you wise men, or are you fools?

Why do you molest me?

I am not young Todoritsa;

I am the black little slave-girl;

Todoritsa sits on the divan.

Tell me what you are seeking;

I will deceive Todoritsa for you;

I will bring her to you here."

And the Turks, Janissaries, said to her:

"The Tsar Sultan Selim has sent us,

To seize young Todoritsa,

And carry her before the Tsar."

And they let young Todoritsa go;

She went off to the high divan;

They sat down, and they dined.

Then young Todoritsa says:

"O Todor, my lord!

At dinner I eat lordly dishes,

And in my heart — brambles and stones!

What a wonder, and a great one, has come to me:

What the Tsar Sultan Selim has sent;

He has sent men to seize me.

When I went down to draw wine,

Then I found Turks, Janissaries;

I told them I was the black little slave-girl,

And I escaped from their hands.

Now what shall we do?"

Then Salakim Todor says:

"O hey, fair bride!

Do not take up that care!

Since they have come secretly into the cellars,

You sit on the high divan;

Hold our little male child.

And he arose on his hero's feet;

He took his swift horse;

He took his yellow mace;

He took his sharp sabre;

He took his crested banner;

And he says to his fair bride:

"You watch the crested banner;

As long as it stands, do not take up care.

If the crested banner falls,

Then open the gates yourself."

When he went into the ground-floor cellars,

Some Turks he cut down with his sabre;

Other Turks he trampled with his horse;

He made blood up to the knees;

He tilted the crested banner;

His horse foundered in the blood.

Then young Todoritsa cried out —

Cried out laments with a loud voice —

And made as though they had destroyed him.

And Todor called out from the cellars:

"Hush, hush, young Todoritsa!

For the Turks have not destroyed me;

I have cut down all the Turks here."

And young Todoritsa came down;

What a wonder, and a great one, she would see!

All the Turks inside, cut down.

They climbed up onto the high divan.

The Tsar Sultan Selim heard of it —

All his Turks had been destroyed for him.

No great rage fell on him;

He gave him a great gift instead:

As a gift, he gave one swift horse;

And he gave him one sharp sabre.



Song 192. Mitre and His Sister Angelina

Mitre sat down with his mother to supper;

They sup; they make conversation:

"O mother, o dear mother!

How is it I had no dear brother —

Either a brother, or a dear sister?"

"O Mitre, o my dear son!

Do you know, my son, do you remember

When you, my son, were [a child] in captivity over the land?

You had one dear sister —

A dear sister, the maiden Angelina;

Dervish-Beg Voivode stole her from you."

"O mother, o dear mother!

Do not tell me who stole her from me;

Tell me instead: whither did he go?"

"O Mitre, o my dear son!

He went across the Black Sea."

"O mother, o dear mother!

Come, knead me a very white loaf;

Fill two measures of wine for me.

I will go to invite my sister;

To invite my sister for a first visit;

Perhaps letters will be forgotten."

And his old mother arose;

She kneaded him a very white loaf;

She filled two measures of wine.

He mounted his swift horse;

He flung himself into that Black Sea.

When the first cocks crowed,

Mitre was in the middle of the Black Sea;

When dawn dawned,

He came out at the shore, at the coast.

There he found three young slave-women;

And Mitre the good hero says to them:

"O hey, three young slave-women!

Where does Dervish-Beg Voivode sit?"

Then the three young slave-women say:

"O you, unknown deliya!

His houses are known of themselves:

His empty gates are of steel;

On his ramparts, heads are arrayed."

Mitre went off as far as there;

Once he knocks, and twice he calls:

"Come out, my dear sister,

For I have come, sister, to invite you —

To invite you, sister, for a first visit!"

Then his dear sister says:

"It is not in my power, brother, to invite you;

Only let us talk together,

Until Dervish-Beg Voivode comes;

He is now in the forests, hunting.

They went [the brothers' note: they went] onto the high divan;

They set lordly dishes;

They brought out the white loaf.

Then his sister Angelina says:

"Come, brother, now you shall sleep a little;

I will work great wonders."

And Mitre lay down, to sleep a little;

And his sister Angelina arose;

She flung the fine chain over him;

She kneaded a very white loaf;

She kneaded it all of ...

And Dervish-Beg Voivode came home.

Then young Angelina says:

"Welcome, Dervish-Beg Voivode!

See, it is now three days and three nights;

Where have you wandered through the green forest?

You have hunted no game at all;

And I sit on the high divan;

A great catch I have caught for you:

My brother, your enemy;

And I put him in the fine chain."

Then Dervish-Beg Voivode said:

"Come, set me a little something to eat!"

She set the white loaf before him —

Which was all of ...;

And she set three-year-old wine before him,

And in the wine, bitter herbs.

Dervish-Beg Voivode dined;

And lay down, to sleep a little.

And young Angelina arose;

She fed his swift horse;

She fed him poisonous fodder;

She unlocked the painted chest;

She gathered a little wealth, much wealth;

She went to her dear brother;

She took the fine chain off him;

And his sister Angelina said to him:

"Get up, brother; now let us flee!

Either now, brother, or never!"

And Mitre the good hero arose;

He took two good horses;

He fled across the Black Sea.

They found their old mother;

When their old mother saw them,

When she saw them, at once she gave up her soul.



Song 193. The King and Queen Rusanta

Queen Rusanta wept;

And the King, the lord, has come to us.

And the King, the lord, speaks:

"O hey, Queen Rusanta!

What need has driven you,

That you shed tears down your white cheeks?

Have you worn out your pearl necklaces?

Have you worn out your silken clothes?"

Queen Rusanta spoke:

"O hey, King, my lord!

I have not worn out pearl necklaces;

I have not worn out silken clothes;

Only, it is now nine years,

We have seen no child at all —

Neither a girl, King, nor a boy."

And the King, the lord, speaks:

"Have you yourself not understood?

Do you not see your red robe?

When you came to me as a bride,

Your robe went edge to edge on you;

But now edge does not meet edge."

A little time she waited;

She bore a foolish little male child.

She swaddled him in green silk;

She put him in a golden cradle;

She wrapped him in cloth-of-gold swaddling;

She set him in the middle of the courtyard —

In the middle of the courtyard, on a marble slab;

With her foot she rocks him; with her mouth she sings to him:

"Hush-a-bye, hush, foolish little male child!

Hush-a-bye, hush — your father has waited for you in sorrow!

Hush-a-bye, hush — your mother has thirsted for you!"

And the King, the lord, heard her:

"O hey, Queen Rusanta!

Why do you curse the foolish little male child?"

He went into the painted room;

He unlocked the painted chest;

He brought out cotton cloth;

He smoothed the cotton cloth;

And he wrapped Queen Rusanta in it;

He buried her in the earth to the knees;

He smoothed her fair hair;

And he set her fair hair on fire.

Her hair burns, until [it reaches] her black eyes.

Queen Rusanta spoke:

"O hey, King, my lord!

Absolve my great fault;

Do not burn my white face,

That you so often used to kiss!"

"Burn here; burn out here —

If you have no pity for a child!"



Song 194. An Only Son and His Mother

What is that clamor in the upper, lower land?

Is it muddy water it brings me?

Is it a mighty fire burning for me?

Is it a mighty Pasha beating me?

It is no muddy water it brings me;

Nor is it a mighty fire burning;

Nor is it a mighty Pasha beating me.

It is that black Plague:

She does not beat the old and the young;

She beats whomever she finds a hero.

She struck an only son of his mother;

Struck him, and killed him.

Every day his mother goes to his grave:

Every morning: "My son, good morning!

Every evening: "My son, good evening!

Is your earth-house warm, my son?

Is your earth-bed soft?

Is your pillow-stone soft?"

Out of griefs, the earth spoke:

"Warm is my earth-house, mother;

Soft is my earth-bed;

And soft is my pillow-stone.

Do not curse the black Plague, mother;

She too, mother, is under orders.

Curse instead Isra the Barber,

Who left a lock of hair unshorn —

That there may lie for me a three-headed snake,

With twelve little snakes."



Song 195. Mitre and the Plague

Mitre the good hero became betrothed;

He betrothed a bride from far away.

As soon as he had ringed her with a ring,

He went off to the city of Istanbul.

Three years he sat there;

He earned three loads of treasure;

He set out to go home.

He lodged overnight in ruined Skopje;

He slept; in the morning he arose;

He took four horses;

He went off to drink water.

At the white fountain the horses drank their water;

There he found an old woman.

And Mitre the good hero said to her:

"O hey, grandmother, old grandmother!

What will this wonderful wonder be?

In the city of Skopje I spent the night;

Everywhere I hear cocks where they crow;

Cocks crow, and dogs bark;

People nowhere, grandmother, have I seen."

Then that old woman says:

"O Mitre, o my dear son!

I am not, my son, an old grandmother;

I am, my son, the black Plague.

All the people, my son, have fled."

Then Mitre the good hero says:

"As you live, God, grandmother; as you live, [by your] soul!

Look in it, grandmother — that empty register:

Am I too written in the register?"

The grandmother looked in the empty register;

Looked, and that very hour said to him:

"You too, my son, are written here;

You have no life, my son, longer than three days;

In one day, my son, you will perish."

Then Mitre begged her pitifully:

"I beg you, grandmother, as you live [by] God!

Make me three days, grandmother, to last —

Until I go to my bride;

For it is just three years

Since I betrothed her, grandmother;

I have not even seen her with my eyes."

Then she said: "Come, my son, go!"

He loaded three loads of treasure;

He went off to his young bride;

And he knocked at her heavy gates:

"Come out, maiden Angelina!

Let us once see each other with our eyes,

For now I am to perish!"

Poor Angelina came out;

She bowed down; she kissed his hand;

One load of treasure he left her.

He went off to his mother;

Mitre the good hero called out:

"Come out, my old mother!

Let us take leave of each other,

For now I shall perish for you!"

His old mother came out to him;

His dear brothers came out;

They spread two loads of treasure.

As soon as his mother embraced her son —

Embraced him and kissed him —

That very hour Mitre gave up his soul.

His dear mother tore out her hair;

His dear sister cries like a bird;

His dear brothers break their heads —

From grieving for the young Mitre.

They kept him three days unburied;

On the fourth they worked a wonder:

They gathered priests and bishops for him;

With great honor they buried him.



Song 196. (from Prilep)

Tsar Selim, Doykole, has turned dervish;

He dressed, Doykole, in white clothes,

To come out, Doykole, on Easter,

To watch them, Doykole, where they dance.

There came out, Doykole, the headmen —

As at Prilep, Doykole, the begs;

There came out, Doykole, the headmen's wives —

As at Prilep, Doykole, the beg-women;

There came out, Doykole, the young bridegrooms —

As at Prilep, Doykole, the sipahis;

There came out, Doykole, the brides —

As at Prilep, Doykole, the sipahi-women;

There came out, Doykole, the youths —

As falcons, Doykole, over the forest;

There came out, Doykole, the maidens —

As partridges, Doykole, armed. [Doykole is the refrain vocative; the Easter dance lines are kept as printed.]



Song 197. The King, the Queen, and Maiden Ruzhitsa

The King and Queen are drinking red wine;

The King's servant [is] a Greek, the treasurer-lad;

And the Queen's [servant] is maiden Ruzhitsa.

The King would say to the Queen:

"O Queen, o mistress!

Come, let us exchange servants!"

It did not fall well for the Queen;

She drew out a Frankish little knife;

She swung it back, backwards;

And she struck maiden Ruzhitsa;

She struck her between the two eyes;

And she plucked out both her black eyes.

Maiden Ruzhitsa burst out weeping:

"Alas, God, for my black eyes!"

The Queen says to Ruzhitsa:

"Hush, hush, maiden Ruzhitsa!

I will give you two white cities,

That you may look on them as long as you live."

Then maiden Ruzhitsa speaks:

"I will be blind in two white cities —

When I have no eyes to look on them!"



Song 198. Maiden Ruzhitsa and Thirty Lads

Thirty lads sat down;

They sat by the Black Sea.

Maiden Ruzhitsa serves as servant;

Serves as servant, and speaks fairly to them:

"O hey, thirty lads!

For all of you I can be a servant;

For all of you I cannot be a love.

Whoever is a hero, born of a hero —

Let him strip off his silken clothes;

Let him put on a ... shirt;

Let him take a steel shield;

Let him swim across the Black Sea;

Let him come out at the shore, at the coast.

There there is a golden apple-tree;

Let him pluck three golden apples —

With him I shall be a love."

...

One youth bowed his head;

He arose on his hero's feet;

He stripped off his silken clothes;

He put on his ... shirt;

He took his steel shield;

He flung himself into that Black Sea;

He came out at the shore, at the coast;

He found the golden apple-tree.

He did not pluck three golden apples;

He tore the apple-tree out by the roots;

He swam back across the Black Sea;

And he brought it to the honorable table.

When maiden Ruzhitsa saw him,

She arose on her hero's feet;

She bowed down; she kissed him;

She seized them by the right hand;

She led the thirty lads off;

She went to her mother.

They began to hold a wedding for them;

A small wedding — exactly three months.



Song 199. Yovan and His Mother

Yovan vexed his mother greatly;

His mother flew into a fierce rage;

She fiercely cursed Yovan:

"O Yovan, o my born son!

Why do you vex me so, my son?

May God grant that you turn mad,

And go off into the green forest,

And become, my son, a dark-brown stag:

In winter to winter in the cold shade;

In summer to summer in the hot sun;

And to wander for exactly three years;

In the fourth, let your mind come back to you!"

His son turned the curse back again:

"O mother, o dear mother!

As you say, so let your curse be!

I shall turn mad for you, mother.

When I turn mad, mother,

When I go into the green forest —

May Korun the Highwayman come,

And destroy your elder son;

Enslave your rich wealth;

Take your nine stable-horses;

Take your three young brides —

The brides as black slave-girls;

And the foolish children as servants —

And you yourself a goose-girl,

To pasture other people's geese on the roads!

As they uttered their curses,

So their curses seized them.

Young Yovan turned mad;

He went off into the green forest.

Korun the Highwayman came;

He destroyed his elder brother;

Everything he said came to pass.

When the fourth year came,

Yovan's mind came back to him;

He set out to go home;
