Song 134. Todor and His Mother

"O Todor, Todor!"

Young Todor rose early,

On fair holy Sunday;

He sweeps the church, and sings;

And then drove them after —

The shepherds, and the herdsmen —

With a nosegay of green basil

Toward their lower fold.

His sister-in-law spied him:

"Tell me, brother Todor:

What book have you kept on Sunday?

You sweep the church, and sing;

You read the book, and weep.

What is found for me in the book?

Can it be a plague is coming,

Or an empty dearth?"

Todor speaks to his aunt:

"Aunt, be a mother to me!

Let me tell, and not lie to you,

What I read in the book, and weep for:

It is found for me in the book:

I will perish young —

From my dear birth-mother,

From my first love Petkana.

I will rise, and flee;

I will go far away;

I will not ask for my mother;

Nor will I send word —

Lest my mother know me."

And then Todor fled;

He crossed the Danube,

And went off into Wallachia,

And stayed nine years.

God helped Todor;

He became a famed cattle-dealer;

He gathered great wealth;

He drives a great fortune:

Nine droves of sheep,

Droves of grey cattle,

And nine waking sheep.

Todor drives the shepherds on:

"Shepherds and herdsmen!

Drive the flock gently;

I will go on ahead,

To prepare you fodder

For the great grey flock."

Then Todor went off;

He knocked at a gate.

Petkana came out —

Todor's bride.

Todor speaks to his bride:

"Fair girl, bride!

Is there lodging for me?

For I am driving a great fortune;

I dare not sleep in the open field."

Petkana knew him —

Todor's bride.

She speaks to a wedding-guest:

"Young wedding-guest, young road-fellow!

I have an old mother-in-law;

Let me go and ask her."

And young Petkana said:

"Dear mother, little mother!

A young traveler knocks at the gate;

He begs you for lodging;

He drives a great wealth;

He dares not sleep in the open;

Let it not be young Todor!"

Then her mother-in-law said:

"You whore of a bride!

Will Todor come back like that?

He perished long ago;

That is just a young traveler.

Let him come to us,

That we may refresh the traveler,

And destroy the young traveler,

And take his wealth."

Petkana's candles had been lit, Todor's bride.

The bride obeyed;

They brought in the young traveler;

They prepared a warm supper;

They mixed wine and rakia,

And sat down to sup.

The wine drugged Todor;

He lay down sweetly, and fell asleep.

His mother crept near;

She bent over Todor;

She took his knives;

Todor's mother stabbed him.

His horse neighed to Todor;

As it neighed, it spoke:

"Todor perished last night —

From his dear birth-mother."

Then his mother heard it:

That he had been her dear son Todor.

"Why does Todor not come,

To settle his wealth?"



Song 135. Pavel and His Sister

Pavel's horse neighed,

Early, early on Sunday morning.

When his old mother heard it,

His mother speaks to Pavel:

"Alas, alas, my son Pavel!

Why does the good horse neigh?

Is he hungry for grass?

Or is he thirsty for water?

Or is his oats not oat-fed?"

Deli Pavel speaks:

"Alas, alas, old mother!

He is not hungry for grass;

He is not thirsty for water;

He is not un-fed with oats.

Is he neighing for a sale,

That Pavel will be married?"

Pavel did not listen to his mother:

"Do not marry this year:

The wheat is from glutina [a poor kind];

The wine is from blackberries."

Pavel did not listen to his mother;

He took the golden flask,

And invited the bright sun

As young godfather,

The autumn moon as best man,

The fine stars — all bride-men.

A great wedding gathered:

Horse by horse, cart by cart —

They went for the bride.

When deli Pavel crossed,

When it was across the sea,

He sank up to his waist;

They crossed on dry sand.

When the bride crossed,

When they were coming back,

She sank up to her breast.

The sea, as it flowed in,

A great wedding crossed;

It even carried off stones.

When they went into the church,

The great wedding stopped;

The priests went dumb;

The deacons went blind.

One little deacon spoke out:

"Godfathers and best men,

And you, young bride-men!

Ask the bride

Whether she is a true girl from across the sea,

Or a true girl from across the mountains."

And they asked the bride:

She is not a true girl from across the sea;

She is a true girl from across the mountains —

Deli Pavel's dear sister.

The bright sun danced

With a gilded staff;

It parted the sea;

The bright sun crossed over;

The good horse sank

The good horse to its knees.

When the Autumn Moon crossed,

The good horse sank —

The good horse to its stirrups.

When the bride-men crossed,

On the horses, up to the saddles.

"O Dimche the interpreter!

His wife saw him

Three measures of yellow ducats.

All the nine watermills,

A bird lit on the windows;

It has not shamed you.

Where has it been heard and seen

"O Turk, convert!

When we were little,

We had an old mother —

An old mother, a dear father —

And I had one brother.

Captivity passed over the land;

The Turks enslaved us;

The Turks took my brother,

Where the sun sets for me;

Me they carried off,

Where the sun rises for me.

They destroyed our father;

They enslaved our mother.

I grew up, I grew tall;

Stoyan the hero bought me back,

When he was a little child,

Where shall I find the money,

The Turk was saying to him:

And your wife is not yours —

"O sister Angelina!

Song 142. The Seven Kings and the Serpent in the Well

Seven kings have sat down;

Sat down at a cold inn;

They eat, they drink, they take counsel,

To travel the land to its edge.

And Sekula the Child says to them:

"I beg you, uncle, take me with you,

That we travel the land to its edge:

Travel the Karavlach land;

Travel the Albanian land;

Travel the English land;

Travel the Arab land;

Travel the Turatin land."

Then Marko Kralyevik says:

"I will take you, my son, to travel —

But there, my son, it is bad:

There is no cold water there;

There is no tall shade there,

That we may sit and cool ourselves.

There is a ruined well there;

Inside it is a three-headed snake,

That eats everyone who goes in;

It gives no one its water."

Then Sekula the Child says:

"Take me, uncle, let me go!"

They took Sekula the Child;

They went to travel the land,

To travel the land to its edge.

They went into a green forest;

They found the ruined well;

And cold water longed in them [they thirsted].

Then foolish Sekula says:

"O you, seven kings!

Unbind your silken sashes;

Tie me around the middle;

Lower me into the ruined well,

That I draw one cold water."

They bound on the silken sashes;

They lowered him around the middle;

They gave him a green cup;

They let him down into the ruined well;

He drew one cold water,

And gave it first to his uncle.

Seven times they let him down into the well;

The seven kings drank.

Afterwards he went in himself, to drink.

The snake was very drowsy;

Seven times it did not know the child.

Just as he was about to drink,

Then the snake woke up;

It seized foolish Sekula;

It swallowed him up to the knees,

And again, up to the waist.

Then Sekula the Child cried out:

"Alas, uncle, alas, dear uncle!

Give me my sharp sabre,

That I cut down the three-headed snake!"

They gave him his sharp sabre.

The child swung, to cut down the snake;

He did not cut the three-headed snake;

He cut the silken sashes;

He fell down into the ruined well.

Then foolish Sekula says:

"O you, seven kings!

Go on; do not wait for me;

Leave my swift horse:

As the snake will eat me,

So the eagles shall eat him."

The seven kings set out;

They left his swift horse.

The horse neighs; it is heard in the forest;

His voice goes over three times nine mountains.

There are three coiled horos there;

The horos were all Samovila ones;

In front was Gyurgya Samovila;

Beside her, Erina Samovila;

They are his sworn sisters.

Gyurgya Samovila heard the voice:

"Somewhere Sekula's horse is neighing!"

And she sent Erina Samovila.

She coiled herself like white cloth,

Went into the green forest,

And found Sekula's horse.

Erina Samovila cried out:

"Let go, let go, three-headed snake!

Let my foolish Sekula go!

He is my dear brother!

Do not come, Gyurgya Samovila,

That she do you a wonder and a great one!"

Then the three-headed snake cries:

"Begone, begone, Erina Samovila!

Now I am sated with flesh;

This is what I sought, and this is what I found."

She coiled herself like white cloth,

And went back to where she was,

And says to Gyurgya Samovila:

"O you, my dear sister!

The three-headed snake has no fear;

It will not let our dear brother go."

Gyurgya Samovila coiled herself;

Coiled herself like white cloth;

And went into the green forest,

And sat on the ruined well:

"Let go, let go, three-headed snake!

Let my dear brother go!

If I come in to you, I will take you out,

Though the well be three hundred fathoms deep!"

The snake says: "You cannot come in."

She coiled herself like white cloth,

And went into the ruined well;

She seized foolish Sekula;

Seized him by his slender hands;

Pulled him out, together with the snake.

Then Gyurgya Samovila says:

"O you, three-headed snake!

How dare you eat my dear brother?

Gyurgya Samovila is his sister;

Erina Samovila is his sister!"

She drew the folding sabre,

Cut down the three-headed snake.

They took foolish Sekula;

They set him on the swift horse.

And foolish Sekula mounted;

He struck it with the yellow mace:

"Let me see you, my swift horse,

That we overtake our company!"

When Sekula's horse neighed,

Marko's horse turned around.

And Marko Kralyevik says:

"Go on, go on, my swift horse!

Why do you walk here, and keep turning back?"

And Marko's swift horse says to him:

"O you, Marko, my master!

A horse is neighing from our company;

Foolish Sekula has overtaken us."

And Marko Kralyevik says to him:

"O you, my swift horse!

Sekula is in the ruined well;

Lest you struck [him] with the yellow mace!"

They went on a little, to halfway;

Again Marko's swift horse speaks:

"O you, Marko, my master!

Look back behind yourself, behind the horse:

Foolish Sekula is coming."

Then Marko Kralyevik says:

"I will strike you with the yellow mace;

I will strike you between the black eyes!"

Again his swift horse says:

"Where do you want to strike me here?

Look back, to see him!"

Marko Kralyevik looked back;

He saw foolish Sekula:

He rides his horse as if he were flying.

And Marko Kralyevik says to them:

"O you, seven kings!

See a wonder, and a great one!

Foolish Sekula has overtaken us!

How can it be? How was he saved?"

They overtook them; they kissed them;

They kissed them, and they asked them;

And he told them of the great wonders

That his sister wrought —

His sister, Gyurgya Samovila.



Song 143. The Seven Heroes and the Arab

Marko of Prilep walks a walk;

Walks a walk across Kosovo field;

There he built seventy monasteries,

Without viziers and without the Tsar's leave.

From there he went to wide Skopje;

There he set about building a monastery;

He built the monastery of Saint Dimitri.

When Marko had built the monastery,

Word of him reached the city of Istanbul —

To Istanbul, to Sultan Murat.

Sultan Murat set criers.

A crier cries down through the city of Istanbul:

"Do you hear, Turks and rayahs?

What a delibasha Marko has become!

What wonders are told of him!

Whoever is a hero, born above heroes,

Let him seize delibasha Marko,

And bring him alive to me!

I will give him three fair lands:

I will give him the Kamenica forest;

I will give him Sarajevo in Bosnia;

I will give him all bloody Bosnia —

Only let him bring Marko alive to me.

If he wishes, I will give still more land!"

One Black Arab undertook;

He came out before the Tsar, and bowed:

"Fortunate Tsar, from now to forever!

I am fit to seize Marko alive.

God strike the lands you are giving dead —

They bear neither bread nor wine!

Promise, Tsar, something else that you will promise!"

That Tsar, Sultan Murat, answered:

"I will give you the city of Stalakin,

Which Todor of Stalakin commands;

I will give you the city of Radomir,

Which the old Wallachian woman commands;

I will give you the city of Prilep,

Which Marko Kralyevik commands;

I will give you Marko's painted horse;

I will give you his mace and his sabre;

I will give you young Marko's wife —

Marko's wife, with her little male child."

Then the Black Arab boasted;

He went down into the Istanbul market;

Straight he pulled to the cloth-sellers;

There they cut him monk's clothing.

From there he went to the goldsmiths;

There he made gilded crosses;

On his head, a monk's cap;

On his hands, golden cuffs;

He made himself a Black Monk;

Then he went to the city of Prilep,

To Marko's heavy gates.

The Black Arab called and knocked;

Marko's mother came out;

He asks her where her son Marko is —

He needed priests and monks,

To render him honor-service.

Then Marko's mother said:

"My son Marko is not here;

Marko is building a fair monastery there —

A fair monastery of Saint Dimitri."

When the Black Arab learned this,

He girthed his horse with golden stirrups,

And went to wide Skopje;

Straight he pulled to the fair monastery;

He stood at the church door.

The Black Arab cried out:

"Come out, Marko Kralyevik!"

When Marko turned and saw:

"May you find it from God — what are you seeking?

What are you seeking here, Black Arab?"

Then the Black Arab said:

"Turn back, delibasha Marko;

Turn back; do not sin your soul!

I am not your Black Arab to you;

I am a Black Monk to you,

Straight from the clean forest, the Holy Mountain,

From the fair monastery of Hilandar.

Turn back; do not sin your soul!

The first priest or monk that comes,

The master must come out,

That he sing a prayer over him."

Then delibasha Marko said:

"I beg you, father, forgive me!

Forgive me — I did not know who you are!"

Then the Black Arab drew out —

Drew a letter from his bosom,

And laid it on Marko's head;

And set about singing a prayer.

With his right hand he holds the letter;

With his left hand he holds a chain;

He laid the chain on Marko's throat;

Bound Marko to the horse's saddle;

Took him out of the fair monastery.

Marko cries like a fierce serpent.

The Hayduk Debel Novak heard him;

He cried out to young Novak's wife:

"Go quickly, unsaddle the horse,

That I race to wide Skopje,

To the monastery of Saint Dimitri."

When Hayduk Debel Novak came,

When he looks — Marko is gone.

He girthed his horse across wide Skopje,

And chased the Black Arab,

To free Marko Kralyevik.

When he overtook the Black Arab,

Where he is dragging delibasha Marko,

Dragging him over the cold cobbles,

Hayduk Debel Novak cried out:

"You fine Black Arab!

Was the food you ate not enough for you,

That you have come to seize heroes?

They call me Hayduk Debel Novak,

From the high city of Kachanik.

Do you know, Black Arab,

If I swing my heavy mace,

If I strike you between the two eyes,

The two eyes will jump out of your head;

What was white day, will become night!"

Then the Black Arab turned around;

He turned his dervish's staff:

"Come," he said, "Hayduk Debel Novak!

A curse on the kingship you king with,

If you, Arab-like, do not slay me!"

Novak swung his heavy mace;

The Black Arab caught the mace —

Caught it on his little finger.

Then the Black Arab flung —

Flung a ring, caught Debel Novak;

He made them two good heroes.

Then the two good kings grappled,

To bite each other like fierce serpents.

Novak's child heard it —

Novak's Gruitsa, the voivode.

When he came to wide Skopje,

He loosed his horse across wide Skopje,

Here and there to catch the Arab;

He caught him at the Skopje gates.

Gruitsa the voivode cried out:

"You fine Black Arab!

He is not, fine one, delibasha Marko;

He is not my father Debel Novak!

They call me Gruitsa the voivode:

As muddy water carries off a torrent,

So I will carry you off, wretch!

Wait for a mace from me, for you!"

And the Arab held out his staff;

Again the Arab took his stand at the mark:

"Come, Gruyo, let me see you!

Let me see what heroism you have!"

He laid the mark at the Arab's knee.

When Gruitsa the voivode flung —

When he flung his heavy mace —

The Black Arab caught it;

Caught it on the Arab knee;

Flung a ring, and caught Gruyo;

He made them three good heroes.

They cry out sharper, like fierce serpents.

The younger child heard it —

The younger Hayduk Novak's child,

The younger Deli Tatomirche.

He loosed his horse across wide Skopje,

Here and there to catch the Arab;

He caught him just outside Skopje,

A horse[run] to seven hours' places.

Then Deli Tatomirche cried out:

"What a Black Arab you are!

You have come to seize kings!"

Then he held out the dervish's staff:

"Come," he said, "Deli Tatomirche!

I know you too, and the world knows you,

What heroism your heart has!

When you sat at Demir-Kapiya [the brothers' note: a place in Macedonia near the Vardar — the Iron Gate. — Ed.],

You did not let a bird pass by,

Let alone a man pass by!

And you had so much fear then —

Fear of the naked dervishes!

Let me see you, Deli Tatomirche!"

Then he swung his heavy club;

The Black Arab caught it —

Caught it on his left hand;

And locked him too in a ring;

He made them four good kings.

They cry out like fierce serpents;

The Arab drags them along the roads.

Yankula Voivode heard it —

The voivode from the city of Shidin [Shibenik?].

He mounted his wrestler horse,

And came to wide Skopje.

He saw the empty monastery standing;

His sworn brother Marko is gone.

It came to his mind that the Arab had seized him.

When he mounted the low-flying horse,

He came out of wide Skopje;

Here and there to catch the Arab;

He caught him on wide Kosovo.

Yankula Voivode cried out:

"Stop there, Black Arab!

I am Yankula Voivode to you:

As muddy water carries off a river,

So I will carry you off!"

And when the Black Arab heard,

He turned his dervish's staff:

"Come, you too, Yankula Voivode;

Let me see what heroism you have!

A curse on the kingship you king with,

If you, Arab-like, do not slay me!"

When Yankula swung his mace,

He caught it on his left hand;

Flung a ring, and caught Yankula;

He made them five good heroes.

They cry out like fierce serpents;

The Arab drags them along the roads.

When Milosh Orgyanin heard it —

Milosh Orgyanin, from the city of Orgyan —

He does not go to wide Skopje;

He pulls across field and mountains,

To get before the Black Arab;

Milosh asks for him, wants him.

From afar he swung his mace:

"Hold, fine Black Arab!

Why have you come into our land?

A Tsar is tsaring in the city of Istanbul,

And we in our land!

They call me Orgyanin Milosh!"

The Black Arab cried out:

"Come, Milosh — what heroism you have!

A curse be on me if you do not slay me!"

Milosh swung his heavy club;

The Arab held out his staff;

There was nowhere to lay the mace;

Then the Arab found himself in trouble:

The mace fell on the Arab's chest;

He flung a ring, and caught Milosh;

He made them six good heroes.

They cry out like fierce serpents.

Heaven and earth tremble;

To the edge of the earth their voices are heard.

As Saint Elijah thunders,

So the kings' voices thunder.

They cry out like fierce serpents.

Sekula the Child heard it —

Sekula, from the city of Budim;

Sekula, the Falcon's child.

He has warred a full nine years

Against Sultan Murat's army.

When he was sitting down to supper,

Sounds reached [him] from the gate;

And he cried out to his beloved:

"First love, young Sekulitsa!

Do not stand there, love, do not listen!

Quickly saddle my swift horse,

That I go out across the wide field!

Nine years I have been away from my land;

I hear an uproar over the wide field;

A Black Arab is walking my field.

Has he leave from the Tsar,

To trample my land?

Saddle the horse from the stable!"

When young Sekulitsa arose,

She saddled his painted horse for him;

And Sekula girt on his sabre,

And took his heavy mace;

He made the sign of the cross toward the rising sun.

When he stepped into the golden stirrup,

He flung himself onto the horse's shoulders.

Young Sekulitsa handed him —

Handed him a little cup.

When the cup touched his eyes,

He cried out to his beloved:

"First love, stay in health!"

Straight he pulled to wide Skopje,

To the monastery of Saint Dimitri;

He looks: the monastery stands alone;

He looks: his uncle Marko is gone.

It came to his mind that the Arab had seized him;

And he bowed to all the saints —

Most of all to Saint Dimitri.

Then he mounted his winged horse;

When he loosed him across wide Skopje,

From his hooves he drew out fire;

Sparks fly over his head.

When he came out of Skopje,

Then his painted horse said to him —

In a whisper he tells his master:

"My master, Sekula the Child!

If you want to catch the Arab,

Hold on tight to me:

I am fit to catch the Arab."

He spoke, and flew under the high heaven;

Here and there to catch the Arab;

He caught him in the green forest;

He got down from his horse;

He fell on the Lord's earth;

He took the cap off his head;

He bowed toward heaven, toward earth,

And to all the saints in order —

Most of all to Saint Dimitri:

"Come, God, help me!

Dear God, and Saint Dimitri:

Let me lay the Black Arab in my hands!"

When Sekula had finished his prayer,

He flung himself onto the horse's shoulders;

He caught the Black Arab;

He caught him in the green forest.

Sekula the Child cried out:

"Hold on, you wretched Black Arab!

He is not, fine one, delibasha Marko;

He is not, fine one, Hayduk Debel Novak;

They call me Sekula the Child —

By surname, the Falcon's child.

Nine years I worked for the Tsar.

Hold on, you Black Arab!

Let me see what heroism you have!"

And the Arab held out his staff;

And the Black Arab cried out:

"Let me see you, Sekula the Child!

I have seized six good heroes;

One ring more I have for you;

God will grant, I will seize you too:

As a young bride-man leads a bride,

So you shall lead the six kings."

Sekula the Child cried out:

"Let me see you, Black Arab!

Let me see what heroism you have!

Do not look that I am a child!

Listen, black calamity, let me tell you:

Come out onto the heroes' ground,

Let me see whose mother bore a son,

Whose sister kept a brother!

Let me see whose mother will weep!

Leave the six kings bound;

Leave them standing on the road;

And come out onto the heroes' ground!"

And the Arab had nothing else to do;

He came out onto the heroes' ground;

Sekula the Child came out too.

The child wanted to swing with his mace;

The Arab wanted to swing with his mace.

Sekula the Child cried out:

"Many Arab maces I have eaten;

Let me eat one more of yours!"

And Sekula stood at the mark.

The Black Arab set about measuring him;

He measures him neither up nor down;

Only he measures at the horse's forehead.

When the Arab flung his mace,

Sekula's horse in that instant lay down —

He lay down on the green grass;

And the mace passed over the head —

Over Sekula the Child's head.

Then Sekula the Child arose;

He does not measure at the horse's forehead;

He measures straight at the horse's chest.

When Sekula flung his mace,

The Arab's horse fell to its knees;

And the mace struck the Arab —

Struck him between the two eyes.

He tumbled from the horse to the ground;

The Arab fell; Sekula attacked him;

He drew the sabre from its scabbard,

And cut off the Arab head;

And laid it in the horse's nosebag;

Hung it on the horse's saddle.

Then Sekula the Child cried out;

He cried out to the six good kings:

"My uncles, and my godfathers!

I have destroyed the Black Arab!"

And what of Sekula the Child!

He drew the sabre from the scabbard,

And cut the six Arab rings,

And set the six good heroes free.

Sekula the Child spoke:

"My uncles, and my godfathers!

You go to wide Skopje,

To the monastery of Saint Dimitri;

There you are to wait for me.

I will go to the city of Istanbul —

To Istanbul, to Sultan Murat —

To ask him whether he gave leave

For the Black Arabs to walk our land.

If the Sultan gave such leave,

I will unsheathe the sabre from the scabbard,

And cut off his head too.

You wait for me at Skopje."

And Sekula the Child went off;

He went to the city of Istanbul —

To Istanbul, to Sultan Murat.

He bowed to Sultan Murat;

He kissed his hand and his knee;

Sekula the Child speaks to him:

"Fortunate Tsar, from now to forever!

What is this torment from the Arabs,

Who walk our land —

Setting kings in chains?"

Tsar Sultan Murat answered:

"My son, Sekula the Child!

Bring me one Arab head;

I will give you fifteen loads

Of fine treasure, all yellow florins."

When Sekula the Child heard this,

He got down off the divan;

From the horse's nosebag he pulled out the head,

And rolled it before Sultan Murat.

A three-year-old fever shook him;

And he says to Sekula the Child:

"Hear me, Falcon's child!

The Arabs are in your hands;

I want you to bring them to me dead;

Not alive — God strike them dead!"

And he gave him fifteen loads

Of fine treasure, all clean florins.

Sekula the Child pulled straight —

Straight he pulled to wide Skopje,

To the monastery of Saint Dimitri.

To him he added ten loads;

Five loads for the cold inn.

Then he found the six good heroes.

"God help you, six good heroes!"

"God give you good, Sekula the Child!"

"Come, my uncles and godfathers,

Let us sit at the cold inn!"

The seven good kings sat down;

They sat at the cold inn;

They eat and drink for three weeks' time;

They drank up five loads of treasure.

Then Sekula the Child said to them:

"Do you hear, you six heroes?

Do you hear? From here on,

Wherever you see a Black Arab,

See that you do not provoke him;

Send me word by a single bird:

I know how an Arab is flayed —

How an Arab's skin is flayed."

This wonder Sekula wrought —

To be famed, to be told of.

The song is left to be sung,

For all our brothers to hear.

From us, the song; from God, health!

Amen, God, for many years!



Song 144. Marko Kralyevik, Detelin Voivode, Ivo, and Fair Grozdana

They asked for fair Grozdana —

Three good heroes asked for her:

The first to ask was Marko Kralyevik;

Her brothers would not give her to him there,

Because Marko is a drunkard to them.

After him, Detelin Voivode;

Her brothers would not give her to him there,

For on his sabre he is a highwayman to them.

And the third, Ivo the good hero;

Her brothers betrothed her to him there.

Little Ivo stood betrothed;

He invites the garlanded wedding guests;

As he invites, so they came to him;

He set them at the honorable table;

They eat, they drink, they make a great wedding-feast.

Ivo went into the earthly stables;

Ivo the good hero speaks:

"O hey, sister Angelina!

Unlock your painted chest;

Take out my white clothes for me."

And sister Angelina arose;

She unlocked her painted chest;

She took out for him a silk shirt;

Over the shirt, a red robe;

On his head, a sable-trimmed cap.

He dressed, adorned himself finely.

They set out for the fair bride.

God strike Marko Kralyevik dead!

God strike Detelin Voivode dead!

They went into the green forests,

To wait for Ivo the good hero —

When they go for the fair bride —

Him to destroy, the bride to seize.

They went for the fair bride;

There they welcomed them finely;

All of them eat, and make merry;

But Ivo sits sorrowful.

His nine brothers-in-law saw him;

And the nine brothers-in-law speak:

"O you, faithful sworn brothers!

All of them eat and make merry;

Why does Ivo sit sorrowful?"

And the faithful sworn brothers tell them:

"O hey, nine brothers-in-law!

Why does Ivo sit sorrowful?

God strike Marko Kralyevik dead;

God strike Detelin Voivode dead!

Because you did not give them your sister,

See — they have thirty lads,

All of them heroes above heroes,

And they have gone into the green forest:

When Ivo goes with the bride,

They will seize Ivo's bride,

And destroy young Ivo!"

And the nine brothers-in-law speak:

"Wait, you faithful sworn brothers;

Let us ask our dear sister!"

"Dear sister, fair Grozdana!

God strike Marko Kralyevik dead!

God strike Detelin Voivode dead!

Because we did not give you to them, sister,

See — they have thirty lads,

All of them heroes above heroes,

And they have gone into the green forest:

When Ivo goes with the bride,

They will seize fair Grozdana,

And destroy young Ivo!"

Then sister Angelina speaks [Grozdana answers]:

"O hey, my nine brothers!

Has that care found you, brothers?

I will take off my bride's clothes;

Give me a hero's clothes;

I will make myself a young mushtuldzhiya [bride-leader];

And I will go in front, before the wedding guests;

And young Ivo shall come after me."

They gave her a hero's clothes;

She dressed, adorned herself finely;

She set out in front, before the wedding guests;

And young Ivo after her, with the wedding guests.

They came near the green forest;

There she found Marko Kralyevik;

There she found Detelin Voivode,

Where the thirty lads are sitting.

And they saw her; from afar they call:

"O hey, young mushtuldzhiya!

Is Ivo coming with the bride?"

The young mushtuldzhiya speaks to them:

"O hey, unknown deliyas!

A stone on you; a stone it was on him!

Him and us the brothers deceived;

To him too they did not give the bride;

They gave her far away, across the sea —

Across the sea, as far as Arvetino.

Only give me water to drink;

And afterwards I will pull along my road!"

She cooled herself with cold water;

And she set out to pull along her road;

She pulled her road through the green forest.

She found herself at Ivo's gates;

Once she knocks, and twice she calls:

"Open, sister Angelina!

Open your coiled gates:

Ivo is coming with the bride!"

Sister Angelina opened;

She welcomed the young mushtuldzhiya;

Welcomed him, and kissed him on the cheek;

She kissed him, and fell in love —

Fell in love with the mushtuldzhiya.

Afterwards Ivo came with the wedding guests;

Sister Angelina welcomed him:

"Welcome, my dear brother!

Dear brother, Ivo the good hero!

What a good thing has come to us from there!

We have not let slip such young goods!

But I beg you dearly, brother:

Give me in marriage to the young mushtuldzhiya!"

Then Ivo the good hero speaks:

"O hey, sister Angelina!

Is it a good thing for me, or for you?

She is not a young mushtuldzhiya;

She is our fair bride!"

Then the sister was ashamed;

She went into the earthly stables;

Lay down face-first; she sheds small tears,

Until she had flooded the stable.

And Ivo the good hero speaks:

"Hush, hush, sister Angelina!

As I have found a love for myself,

So I will find a lad for you."



Song 145. Marko, Sekula, and King Latin

Marko is sitting on the high divan;

A letter has fallen onto his shoulders;

He reads the letter, smiles under his moustache.

Young Marko's wife saw him:

"O Marko, my lord!

What need has driven you,

That you read the letter and smile under your moustache?"

And Marko Kralyevik speaks:

"O you, young wife of Marko!

King Latin has sent word:

The king has one dear daughter;

I have one dear son;

We two shall become in-laws,

And the two young ones shall take each other.

O you, young wife of Marko!

Go into the painted room,

Unlock the painted chest,

Take out the silken clothes,

Dress Sekula the child in them,

And prepare two swift horses."

And young Marko's wife arose;

Arose from the high divan;

Went into the painted room;

Unlocked the painted chest;

Took out the silken clothes;

Took out a red cap;

On the cap, three golden feathers.

She dressed Sekula the Child in them;

And took out two swift horses.

And the two good heroes mounted;

The child shines like the summer sun.

And the king saw them from the divans;

The king called his dear daughter;

Called her onto the high divan;

And teaches his dear daughter:

"O you, my daughter Angelina!

Go into the painted room;

Unlock the painted chest;

Take out your maiden clothes;

Dress yourself, adorn yourself finely;

Go out to the coiled gates.

Marko Kralyevik will come;

You shall go down and take Marko's horse;

Kiss his right hand;

And modestly turn back again;

And to the child, bow a bow."

As the king taught her, advised her,

So his daughter understood the teaching.

And she went into the painted room;

Unlocked the painted chest;

Took out her maiden clothes;

Dressed herself, adorned herself finely;

Went out to the coiled gates.

At once Marko Kralyevik came;

She went down and took his horse;

She kissed Marko's right hand;

And modestly turned back again;

And to the child she bowed a bow.

And King Latin welcomed them;

He set them on the high divan;

He set wine and rakia before them;

And they feasted a day... for a week.

And Marko Kralyevik speaks:

"O you, King Latin!

Enough we have eaten, enough we have drunk —

Enough we have drunk for great joy.

Now prepare daughter Angelina for us."

And King Latin speaks:

"O Marko Kralyevik!

How shall I give you daughter Angelina,

Before the child has done a heroic deed?

I have a Black Arab,

Whom I keep in a Black Dungeon,

Whom I have fed for seven years;

I will bring out the Black Arab,

That Sekula fight with him —

Fight with a slender waist."

Marko Kralyevik wondered;

He went to his swift horse;

He asked his old horse;

Then his old horse speaks to him:

"O you, Marko Kralyevik!

Let Sekula the Child take with him

The Frankish knives;

Let him lay them in his fair braid,

To cut up the Arab's trousers."

They brought out the Black Arab;

And Sekula the Child got down;

Got down into the level courtyard;

And struck with the Black Arab —

To fight with a slender waist.

They fought for three days and three nights;

Neither Arab nor Sekula fell.

From the Arab black blood flows;

From Sekula white foam spurts;

Towards the end, the child will perish.

Girls and brides rushed up;

And then the horse neighed to him —

The horse neighed; it came to Sekula's mind;

He took out the Frankish knives;

He cut up his black trousers;

And the Black Arab fell.

Sekula the Child seized him;

Seized him across the waist;

Stuck him in the earth up to the waist;

Drew out the sharp sabre;

Cut off his black head;

Set it on the high divan;

And sat down at the honorable table.

The king set wine and rakia before them;

The king set it before them for three days and three nights.

Marko Kralyevik speaks again:

"Enough we have wandered, King Latin;

Prepare the fair bride for us!"

And King Latin speaks:

"O you, Marko Kralyevik!

Before the child does another heroic deed,

I will not give you daughter Angelina.

I will heat up a strong oven;

It will burn for three days and three nights;

Afterwards I will fling the child into the oven —

Then I will give him Angelina."

Marko arose from the high divan,

And asked his old horse.

And his old horse speaks to him:

"Do not take up care for this at all;

Let Sekula the Child mount me,

And sport a little with me;

Beside the oven I will stop;

The strong oven — I will quench it;

I have... three cold wells."

As [the horse] advised him and taught him,

So Sekula understood the teaching.

When they had heated the oven,

Then he mounted his father's horse;

Beside the oven he sported with the horse.

The horse quenched the oven for him;

He dismounted from the horse; he went into the oven;

And the child came out of the oven,

And climbed up on the high divan.

Again the king set wine and rakia;

"O you, King Latin!

"Before the child does a third wonder —

Song 146. Shaina the Slave-Girl and Marko

Shaina the slave-girl has fled;

Fled from the city of Istanbul;

She has seized the green...;

She fled into the green forest;

She pitched a green tent.

The Tsar from Istanbul cries out;

Cries out for three days and three nights:

"Whoever is a hero, born of a hero,

Let him find Shaina the slave-girl;

Bring her alive to me here!

I will give him two white cities;

I will give him the city of Kamelia;

Skopje city, beside the Vardar."

No one undertook then;

Only a little yellow Jew undertook:

"O you, Tsar from Istanbul!

Give me seventy heroes;

I will go into the green forest;

I will destroy Shaina the slave-girl,

Or bring her to you alive."

And he took seventy heroes;

He went into the green forest.

When he came near the green tent,

The little yellow Jew rides hard;

From afar Shaina calls to him:

"Stop, you seventy heroes,

That I gather my black hair!"

They made a row, and sat down;

Sat down on the... meadow.

While she gathered her black hair,

And tucked up her skirts and sleeves,

And mounted her swallow-mare,

And took a Kalaklia sabre,

She swept about the... meadow.

Once she swung with her right hand —

She destroyed the yellow Jew;

As she struck to right and to left,

She destroyed the seventy heroes;

She made them seventy graves;

She buried them all;

The Jew was left unburied;

She left him, and a marble slab,

That eagles and lions might pick him.

The Tsar from Istanbul sends;

Sends a young Tatar;

Sends him into the city of Prilep;

Straight he goes to Marko's gates;

Once he knocks, and twice he calls:

"Come out, Marko Kralyevik!

For I am sent from the city of Istanbul;

I will give you a letter of trust.

They tell of you — a very good hero.

The Tsar from Istanbul will send you,

To make war with a woman-hero —

Her name is Shaina the slave-girl.

She was very little

When they enslaved her;

Alas, ... she did not know.

He cries out in the city of Istanbul:

Whoever is a hero, born of a hero,

Let him seize Shaina the slave-girl;

Bring her alive to me here!"

And a little yellow Jew undertook;

He gathered seventy heroes;

He went off to destroy her.

But Shaina was combing her hair;

She begged them to stop,

Until she gathered her hair;

She made an order until she gathered her hair.

"There is a standing stone there,

And in the stone, a swallow-mare,

That flies for me; it does not run for me.

When Shaina the slave-girl arose,

She tucked up her skirts and sleeves,

Girt on the Kalaklia sabre,

Swept about the... meadow,

Destroyed the yellow Jew

And the other seventy heroes."

Then Marko Kralyevik speaks:

"O you, young Tatar!

Go off into the city of Istanbul;

Let no companion go with me;

Alone I will go into the green forest."

And he went into the green forest;

Straight he goes to the green tent;

And Shaina sits under the tent;

From afar Shaina called to him:

"Welcome, unknown deliya!

Are you a Turk, or are you a Christian?"

Marko Kralyevik speaks:

"I am not a Turk, Shaina the slave-girl;

I am, Shaina, a Christian."

"Of what band and kin are you?"

"O you, Shaina the slave-girl!

I am Marko of Prilep."

Again Shaina the slave-girl says:

"O you, Marko of Prilep!

Bare your right arm,

That I see what marks you have?"

"O you, Shaina the slave-girl!

What do you need, to look for marks?"

Again Shaina the slave-girl says:

"Do not be angry, Marko Kralyevik!

Do you, Marko, remember

That we were two sisters:

The elder was Angelina;

The middle was Marko the Palanin;

And after him, Shaina the youngest?

When the revolt happened at Prilep,

When the Turkish janissaries came,

Then the Turks enslaved me;

They took me to the city of Istanbul;

Because I was of a great band,

They made me the Tsar's servant-girl."

Then Marko Kralyevik says:

"O you, Shaina the slave-girl!

Bare your white breasts,

That I see what mark you have?"

She unbound her silken sash,

And opened her white breasts.

When Marko Kralyevik saw,

Then he saw, and Marko believed.

Then he rushed, and embraced her;

He does not hug her as for an enemy,

But hugs her as for a dear sister.

From how hard they embraced each other,

Up to the knees they sank into the earth;

Marko weeps, and Shaina weeps;

They could not part from each other.

They parted, and scattered the tent,

Lest the angry Turks come.

He mounted his swift horse;

And Shaina, her swallow-mare.

She does not go into the city of Istanbul;

She goes into the middle of Prilep.

And he calls to young Marko's wife:

"Open our coiled gates for us!

Take the reward from our mother:

Our dear sister has been born to us again!"

And at once young Marko's wife went,

To take the reward from her mother-in-law.

As she told her, the mother fell face-down:

"What will this great wonder be!

It is now thirty years

Since they enslaved Shaina on me —

Enslaved her, the Turkish janissaries.

I eat, I drink, I shed small tears,

And I have not told you.

Only, where we have kin,

Invite guests and friends:

For Shaina has kept her faith."

Marko Kralyevik went on by;

Went on by through the city of Klisura,

Dashing Marko has hired himself

To guard the Smoky Forest for me —

The Smoky Forest and the green one.

In the evening he went round it;

In the morning he asked it:

Why are you scorched, forest?

Has fire burned you?

Three chains passed by —

Three chains of young slaves;

All maidens ringed;

All widows with foolish children."

Dashing Marko flew into a rage;

He took his swift horse;

He gathered a small army —

A small army, all Arabs;

He overtook the three chains;

He cut down the evildoers;

He set the young slaves free;

He took them out of the chains;

And gifted them with fine treasure;

And sent them off into the district.

Song 150. Yana and Gyuro the Merchant

From Prilep.

Fair Yana was on sale:

For Yana alone, nine thousand;

For her level figure, ten thousand;

For her white face, eleven;

For her white throat, twelve;

For her black eyes, thirteen;

For her slender brows, fourteen;

For her fair hair, up to fifteen.

Whoever valued her, did not out-value her,

Until Gyuro the merchant came;

He valued her, he bought her up,

And took her into his own courtyard.

And he charged his mother:

"Watch fair Yana well for me;

In the morning give her fierce rakia —

Fierce rakia, sweet apples.

Do not let her go with the brides —

With the brides to the cold water;

Do not let her go with the girls —

With the girls into the forest for flowers."

He left fair Yana,

And went off to the market-place.

His mother does not let

Fair Yana to the cold water,

With the girls into the forest for flowers.

God strike the wretched Vlachs dead —

The wretched Vlachs, all Arbanasi —

Who came to buy for me,

To buy white wheat!

They did not buy white wheat;

They carried fair Yana off.

When Gyuro the merchant came back —

Gyuro the merchant from the market-place:

"Where is," he says to his mother,

"Where is, mother, fair Yana?"

"O my son, Gyuro the merchant!

The wretched Vlachs came to me,

To buy white wheat;

They did not buy white wheat;

They carried fair Yana off."

Gyuro the merchant turned back,

And went into the Wallachian land;

He arrived on Easter Day;

And he found her — see — dancing;

And Gyuro the merchant rushed in,

To dance with fair Yana.

And fair Yana said to him:

"Dance gently with me, o Bulgarian!

You will break my nine rings —

Nine rings on my right hand!"

And he speaks to fair Yana:

"Dance for me, dance, fair Yana!

I made them; I will break them!"

He danced a little, and seized her;

He seized fair Yana;

He took her off to his own home.

The Sava fled with the Morava,

Along the Morava, beside the white Danube.

To weave cloth, to sew a sleeve.

The loom-shuttles sing; the loom hums;

Song 152. Yankula and Nikola

"O Yankula, Yankula!

A mother has not lamented [enough]

For your elder brother Nikola

Thirty-three years —

Nor will [she lament] for you, Yankula,

From now, my son, to forever."

Yankula did not listen to his mother;

He mounted his fed horse,

And went off to the mountain,

To the Rabuhchi mountain;

And cried and shouted:

"Where are you, brother Nikola!

Where are you, and whereabouts are you!

In what narrow defile?

Come to me now:

Your brother Yankula is calling you."

And Nikola heard him,

From that narrow defile;

He speaks to the heroes:

"Faithful company, covenanted!

Run the short paths;

Cut off the roads;

Seize the foolish drunkard;

Do not beat him, do not scold him;

Bring him to me,

That I may ask him:

From where he knows me, knows me —

For they call me Nikola."

His company obeyed him;

They ran the short paths;

They cut off the roads;

Seized Yankula;

They did not beat him, nor scold him;

They took him to Nikola.

And the two of them wrestled;

Young heroes, young Voivodas,

Had gathered as cattle-dealers

On the Rabuhchi mountain.

At Istanbul, at the Yedikule,

They were drinking red wine;

Each drinks to his brother;

Each one to another, to his health;

And Yankula has no dear brother —

There is no brother of his

To drink to his health;

And he drinks to his horse.

His company mocks him.

And he went home,

And spoke to his mother:

"Mother, my little mother!

I will, mother, go

To the Rabuhchi mountain,

To seek my brother Nikola;

For it is very grievous for a brother."

His mother speaks to Yankula:

"Do not go, my son Yanko!

Has mother lamented little

For your elder brother Nikola,

Thirty-three years,

Nor will [she lament] for you, Yanko,

From now, my son, to forever?"

"Mother, my little mother!

Why did you not bear me

A little brother, or a dear little sister —

Since we gathered as company,

We were drinking red wine;

Each drank to his brother;

I have no dear brother,

Nor a dear little sister,

To drink to their health."

His mother speaks to Yankula:

"My son Yanko, Yanko!

You had a dear brother —

A dear brother, my son Nikola.

Thirty-three years

He has wandered far away;

Mother did not know of him;

Now mother has learned of him..."

Three fathoms of earth they dug out,

And drove three down below;

No one overcame anyone.

Yankula cried out:

"Where is my mother — come!

To my brother, help yourself —

With my dear brother Nikola:

Whoever overcomes [the other]!"

Nikola guessed it —

That Yankula was his brother;

Then they let go of each other.

He gathered his company;

He paid off the drinkers;

They went home. [The finding of the lost brother is the song's whole burden. — Ed.]



Song 153. Stoyko Voivode

Early frost has fallen;

Earlier, before Cross Day.

The young heroes lay down,

All with green banners.

The heroes were emboldened:

How shall they pass, pass through —

Through the great city of Thessaloniki,

That no one notice them,

And not feel them?

"Stranin, dread deliya!

Look at us all in turn:

Whoever has a woman's face,

But a man's heart —

Let us take hired horses;

Let us load two horses

With maiden's and man's gifts —

Linen and silken —

And then let us pass

Through the great city of Thessaloniki;

And no one had noticed them —

Not noticed, and not felt.

Stranin is the young bride-man.

Three years the sabre unwielded,

Unwielded, nor ever sharpened.

Take it to the young cutler —

To my dear sworn brother —

That he sharpen my sharp sabre.

When I get up, I will pay him, overpay him."

Young Angelina took it;

The young cutler spoke out:

"Angelina, Doychin's sister!

If you give your white face,

Then I will sharpen the sabre on credit."

Fair Angelina burst out weeping;

At once she turned back into her level courtyard:

"O Doychin, my dear brother!

The cutler wants your black eyes,

That he sharpen the sabre on credit."

"Do you hear me, sister Angelina?

Take out my swift horse —

Three years the horse un-oated,

Neither tended the horse, nor combed,

Nor given water to drink.

Take him to the young farrier,

That he shoe the horse on credit.

When I get up, I will pay him, overpay him."

Young Angelina led the horse;

She led the horse to the young farrier;

He wants her white face for the shoeing.

Angelina runs weeping;

She tells her brother Doychin:

"The farrier wants your white face,

That he shoe the horse on credit."

"Come, call Imer the barber for me,

That he shave my head on credit;

When I get up, I will pay him, overpay him."

Fair Angelina went off:

"O barber, o Doychin's friend!

Come and shave my brother Doychin;

When he gets up, he will pay you, overpay you."

"If you give me your white throat,

Then I will shave your brother Doychin."

Angelina sheds hot tears;

Ashamed, sorrowful, she turns back.

Angelina the sister came home;

And the hour came to Doychin's mind:

"O sister, golden Angelina!

Take out three hundred ells of cloth for me,

That I bind up my waist;

My bones are already disjointed.

Give me the yellow mace;

Take out my sharp sabre;

Bring me my swift horse."

She took out three hundred ells of cloth for him;

He bound up his sickened body;

He girt on his sharp sabre;

He flung himself onto the swift horse;

He seized the yellow mace;

Straight he runs to the Black Arab.

Here he arrived at the white tent;

He cries and shouts, as loud as his voice goes:

"Come out, Black Arab!

Come out, you whore's bastard!

Come out, you charred stump!

Come out, let us two try ourselves!

Let you see a hero above heroes!"

The Black Arab came out;

He only screeched, like some dragon:

"Wait, Doychin, you yellow wax-candle!

Wait, wait, man — this mace

Weighs only seventy okas!"

The Arab flung his heavy mace;

Doychin caught it in his right hand.

"Wait, wait, you whore's bastard!

Black Arab, charred stump!

Let you see who Ailing Doychin is!

Wait for my yellow mace:

It weighs only three hundred okas!"

When he swung the yellow mace,

He struck him between the two eyes;

The Arab's head came apart;

As... then he stretched himself out;

He stretched himself out on the raw earth.

Ailing Doychin spurred his horse;

Spurred his horse to the city of Thessaloniki.

He paid the cutler a good payment;

He did not forget the good farrier;

Most of all, Imer the barber.

Their heads he set along the Thessaloniki roads.

Everything alive came out to look —

Young and old, at Ailing Doychin —

And to praise, to laud his heroism.

Barely had he come to his own courtyard

When Doychin lay down, and died.



Song 155. Ailing Doychin

From Kukush.

Doychin the hero lies sick;

He has opened a white tent;

Doychin's wife sheds tears.

He takes tribute from Thessaloniki:

One oven of white... bread,

One barren cow,

One keg of sweet wine,

And one little maiden,

To serve him the sweet wine.

Ailing Doychin saw her,

And says to her and speaks:

"Angelina, first love!

Have you grown weary of it —

That I lie three years,

Three years in bed?"

Then Ailing Doychin calls:

"Angelina, first love!

Take out three hundred ells for me —

Three hundred ells of fine cloth,

That I gather up my sick bones.

Saddle my swift horse for me —

My swift horse unshod,

Three years unfed.

Bring me my sharp sabre —

My sharp Damascus sabre;

And bring me the mace —

The rusted mace,

Three years unwielded."

Angelina obeyed him;

She brought him the fine cloth;

She armed his swift horse;

She brought him the sharp sabre,

The unwielded mace.

And she mounted his swift horse;

And Doychin cried out beforehand;

And he came out of Thessaloniki;

Straight he pulls to the white tent;

From afar he cried out:

"O Arab, Black Arab!

Black Arab with the thick lips!

Come out, Black Arab!

Let me see what hero you are!"

When the Black Arab heard him,

He seized the mace,

And mounted his swift horse,

And came out onto the ground;

And flung with the mace.

Doychin's horse, the trickster,

Fell down onto its knees;

It dodged the mace;

Then the horse sprang up again;

And Doychin cried out beforehand,

And mounted his swift horse,

And flung the mace;

It struck him in the chest;

It buried him in the black earth.

And a village was built there;

And it calls the Arab's name,

Which stands to this day. [The two Kukush columns tangle the fight's end; the Arab is buried where he fell. — Ed.]



Song 156. Doychin the Hero, Orphan Yanko, and King Marko, the Guards of Thessaloniki

They gathered, they assembled —

Up to three Bans, up to three Kings.

One of them is Doychin the hero —

Doychin the hero, from Thessaloniki.

And the second is orphan Yanko —

Orphan Yanko, from Yanina.

And the third is King Marko —

King Marko, from Prilep.

They eat, they drink, they make merry;

With the watch they guard the city.

All of them drew up in turn;

The turn came to Doychin.

He took the golden horn;

He looked up and down

Along the paths, along the roads.

On the seven towers of Thessaloniki

A painted ship has appeared

On the sea.

When they look out on the sea,

Inside sits a Turkish gentleman —

A Turkish gentleman, of another faith.

He does not come onto the wharf;

Straight he pulls to Besh-Chinar;

There he came out of the ship,

And opened a white tent.

When Doychin the hero saw him,

He mounted his swift horse;

And came down to Besh-Chinar;

From afar he cried out beforehand;

He says to the Turk:

"O Turkish gentleman!

Why do you not come onto the wharf,

To pay the heavy customs here?

Have you come to Besh-Chinar instead?"

The Turk speaks and says:

"I did not come to pay you —

To pay you the heavy customs.

I have come from the Sultan,

To command the city of Thessaloniki."

Grief fell on Doychin;

What shall we do, what shall we make of it —

To guard the city of Thessaloniki?

And by field and by sea

They guard the city of Thessaloniki.

He raised his mace,

And flung it at the Turk;

And the mace missed;

It did not strike the Turk.

Then the Turk cried out beforehand:

"Come out of the ship,

That we seize Doychin the hero!"

Doychin turned back again;

He shed small tears.

He went to the seven towers;

He speaks to his company:

"Why do you sit, why do you look on?

A Turkish gentleman has come —

A gentleman of another faith —

To command the city of Thessaloniki.

Grief has fallen on me..." [The 1861 leaf ends the song with the watch alerted; Doychin, Yanko, and Marko hold Thessaloniki together. — Ed.]



Song 157. Petre the Little Hungarian

Alay-Beg was gathering an army;

He cannot gather it,

Until Petre the Little Hungarian came:

He gathered a small army.

Kamber Kadina enslaved [them] —

With twelve serving-girls,

With thirteen slave-girls.

The slave-girls he laid in a carriage;

Kamber Kadina, before the horse...

Petre speaks finely:

"O Kamber the dungeon-keeper!

Do you know, do you remember,

When I was a little child?

I was a servant lad with you;

A fever shook me;

You gave me no bread;

And when you did give me some,

It was molded three weeks old.

You gave me no water;

And when you did give me some,

It was mixed half with earth.

Barefoot over small stones,

And bareheaded in the sun,

You drove me before the horse."

Barefoot over small stones,

And bareheaded in the sun...

The Kadina begged him:

"O Petre the Little Hungarian!

If you do not lay me in the carriage,

At least lay me on the horse!"

"For that I am driving you before the horse:

Barefoot over small stones,

And bareheaded in the sun.

You found me at fault for something;

For that you are driving me before the horse:

Barefoot over small stones,

And bareheaded in the sun." [The two-column page doubles the reckoning; the servant repays the dungeon-keeper in kind. — Ed.]



Song 158. Balyur Voivode and the Greek Slave-Girl

From Ohrid.

From the army comes Balyur Voivode;

Coming from the army, he bears a young slave-girl.

He has encamped on the wide field;

He has pitched a green tent.

Under the tent sits Balyur Voivode;

The young slave-girl stands upright before him;

She makes a divan for him, she serves as a servant;

She sheds tears, like hailstones.

Balyur Voivode asks her:

"O you, slave-girl, white Greek girl!

Why, servant, do you serve, and shed tears?"

"How should I not weep? How should I not grieve?

I left my little male child

In a dark stable with his hands let go."

"O you, slave-girl, white Greek girl!

Come now, you go; come, lift him up.

I set you free, that you may lift him."



Song 159. Murcho the Lord and Yankula Voivode

Two born brothers are dividing an inheritance:

The first of them is Murcho the Lord;

The younger, Yankula Voivode.

They divided villages and farmsteads;

They divided field and mountain.

There remained to them the wrestler horse,

And two brother-falcons;

There remained to them hounds and greyhounds —

His own houses, his own high mansions —

A grey flock of twelve thousand.

Then Yankula Voivode cries:

"My brother, Murcho the lord!

You have a wife: yours outright are the house,

The grey flock of twelve thousand.

You have a wife, and I am a bachelor:

Grant me the hounds and the greyhounds,

The swift horse, the two brother-falcons —

That I mount a horse, and hunt my hunt."

Then Murcho the Lord cries:

"My dear brother, Yankula Voivode!

I am not a cuckoo-bird, to sit in houses;

I am not a shepherd, to pasture sheep for you.

I am the elder; I will receive —

I will receive our father's weapons."

Murcho calls his first love:

"Give me the weapons! The horse, the swift horse!"

And his love armed him,

And pulled his swift horse out for him.

And Murcho mounted his horse;

He drove off the hounds and greyhounds,

And the two brother-falcon chicks,

And charges his love severely:

"I will go into the green forest,

To walk a walk, to hunt a hunt.

I leave Yankula Voivode alive;

When I come, let me find him dead.

Make him lordly dishes;

Make them with fierce poison;

Serve him the sweet wine —

Sweet wine with fierce poison.

If you do not obey me, love,

Your head will not stand on your shoulders.

If you obey me well,

I will gift you a great gift."

And he went into the green forest;

Three days he walked; three days Murcho hunted;

He hunted no game at all.

He loosed the two brother-falcons;

He loosed them high into the sky.

There they found an eaglet;

The two brother-falcons struck it:

One strikes it below the wings;

The other strikes it above the wings.

The eaglet overcame them;

It came down, down to the ground.

They brought it to Murcho the lord.

The eaglet sheds small tears,

And says and speaks to Murcho:

"If my brother had been with me,

Your chicks would not have come near [me]."

Then Murcho set about weeping:

"The chick [cannot] cannot be without a brother —

And what wonder has found me!"

And he turned back to go home.

On the road a Black Arab met him;

And calls to Murcho, and shouts to him:

"Where is, Murcho, your brother, to tear you away?"

Murcho loosed his horse to flee;

And the Arab flung his mace,

And struck him on the hero's shoulders,

Until his chest burst;

Black blood flowed out of him;

It barely tore itself away in flight.

And what of young Murcho's wife!

She was cunning and wise!

She prepared lordly dishes;

Instead of poison, she put honey-mead;

And she invited her brother-in-law Yankula,

To feast him with lordly dishes;

And she made him drink sweet honey-mead.

And she tells Yankula truly:

"My brother-in-law Yankula Voivode!

Your brother will come from the hunt;

He charged me badly —

That when he comes, he not find you alive.

I beg you, Yankula Voivode:

Lie down; be a living dead man."

And Yankula obeyed his sister-in-law;

And he lay down on the high mansion.

Murcho's wife covered him with cloth;

At his head she lit candles;

On her head she bound a black [scarf];

And she loosened her hair;

And she went out to the heavy gates,

To welcome Murcho the lord.

Here comes Murcho the lord;

From his hero's chest blood flows;

And from his eyes he sheds small tears.

From afar he asks his love

Whether Yankula Voivode is alive.

Murcho's wife speaks and says:

"I, my good one, obeyed you:

I gave Yankula poison;

And young Yankula died."

When Murcho the lord heard this,

It fell upon him very bitterly,

Because his brother is no longer alive.

And he got down from his horse;

In his fury he drew his sabre,

To cut down his first love.

Then Yankula Voivode cries out:

"My brother, Murcho the lord!

Have patience: I am still alive!"

When Murcho the lord saw him,

With joy he climbed onto the divan;

With brotherly love he greeted his brother;

And he gifted his love a gift.



Song 160. Neda of Kramya

Fair Neda of Kramya!

Her father-in-law speaks to Neda:

"Daughter-in-law, fair Neda!

It is now nine years

Since they brought you here;

Your brothers, Neda,

Have been dragged away from the wealth,

What your father left you;

Chorbadjis have become of us...

They did not come as guests to you,

To come and visit you.

Are they quite chorbadjis [rich],

Or are they quite poor,

That they have nothing to come with?

It is a grief left with Neda;

And then she went to the water —

To the upper, outer well."

There she found a lad of Kremya;

And then she speaks to the lad:

"Lad of Kremya, villager!

Are you well, are you alive?

Are your brothers well?

And has the wealth been dragged away,

What my father left me?"

The lad of Kremya speaks:

"Fair Neda, village girl!

We are well, and we are alive;

Your brothers are well;

And from the wealth they have been dragged;

What your father left you

Has become twice as much.

Now, Neda, they were getting ready

To come to you as guests."

And Neda said to him:

"Uncle, my father-in-law!

This is not the Pasha for me;

These are my brothers —

My nine brothers-in-law(?),

With nine dear sisters-in-law,

With nine little male children.

They are coming to me as guests!"

Neda rejoiced;

She went home;

She sweeps the courtyard, and sings.

Her father-in-law speaks to Neda:

"Daughter-in-law, fair Neda!

Since you, Neda, knew it,

Why did you not tell me,

Daughter-in-law, fair Neda,

That I might prepare... [hospitality]?"

"It is now nine years;

I have not seen you, Neda,

Sweeping your courtyards;

Now you sweep, and sing.

Is it dear to you, Neda,

That the Pasha is coming into the village —

That he will cross straight through the village,

And welcome the guests?"

She slaughtered a... buffalo;

She prepared all sorts of dishes;

And she loosed up to nine kegs —

Up to nine kegs of wine;

The tenth, of clear rakia;

And straight she goes to us...

They were putting nails on the horse's [shoes];

They were harnessing golden carriages;

And she welcomed the guests.



Song 161. Todora, Bogdan, and Nikola

From four sides word has come;

Todora has set out;

On the mountain, on the haystacks,

With white... Bogdan.

Bogdan speaks to Todora:

"Love, fair Todora!

Take for me, love, the holsters —

Up to seventy-seven."

Todora obeyed him;

She took his holsters;

And they went to the haystacks.

They crossed a wide field;

They came to a green forest.

Bogdan says to Todora:

"Love, fair Todora!

Cry out, maiden, and sing

That heroic song of mine,

That carries two voices.

He will know me by my voice;

He will at once know me:

He will destroy you, young one;

Me he will take young."

From one throat, two voices;

From one mouth, two words!

And Todora says to him:

"Love, o Bogdan, Bogdan!

I had cried out, I had sung;

But I dare not, I cannot,

For Nikola the highwayman:

For he asked for me before,

And mother would not give me.

Now they call him, my love,

With seventy heroes —

With seventy-seven —

In this green forest."

And Bogdan says to her:

"Do not fear, love Todora!

As long as Bogdan is with you,

With seventy holsters —

With seventy-seven."

Todora obeyed him;

The maiden cried out, sang

That heroic song of mine,

That carries two voices.

When Nikola heard her —

Young Nikola the highwayman —

He knew her by her voice;

And [went] to meet them across,

With seventy companions.

When young Bogdan saw them,

Holster after holster he flung;

Hero after hero they fell.

Then young Bogdan destroyed them.

Up to seventy heroes

Nikola had left behind.

And the two of them wrestled;

They wrestled for three days —

For three days, and three nights.

Bogdan says to Todora:

"Love, fair Todora!

Do not sit, love, and look on;

Help whomever you can —

Whoever is to overcome!"

She plunged into her right pockets;

She took out a Wallachian knife;

She cut Bogdan's sash-strap,

That Nikola might overcome him...
