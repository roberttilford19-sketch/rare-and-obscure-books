# DEDICATION — DRAFT

**For Dr. Elisabeth Targ (1961–2002)**

She went before the scientists and said: prayer works — I can prove it. Test it. What do we have to lose?

Picture that room. These are the most ruthless people in American science — they tear apart anything that smells of the paranormal before breakfast. She stood in front of them with distant healing, with *prayer*, and laid out a protocol so airtight they couldn't kill it. They funded her. $1.5 million — the first federal grant for distant-healing research that our search has found. The tightwads opened their wallets. That alone tells you who she was.

Then she ran the trials straight: forty end-stage AIDS patients, healers who never met them, only a photograph and a first name. The treated lived better. Fewer illnesses. Fewer hospital days. She claimed nothing she couldn't show and wouldn't guess at mechanisms she couldn't see.

For that honesty she was attacked from both sides — the believers who wanted a miracle, the skeptics who wanted a fraud. She answered both the same way: *study this more.*

She was forty when glioblastoma took her — the brain cancer, the very kind of disease she'd spent her life trying to beat. The healer studying healing, carried off by the thing she was fighting.

She didn't prove prayer works. She proved something rarer: that a serious scientist could walk into the shark tank with the impossible, walk out with $1.5 million, and not flinch. The door she opened is still open. Walk through it.

# Her Story

**** Assembled from the obituaries, Bronson's *Wired* profile, and the public record. Summarized in the dossier's own words.

## The short version

Elisabeth Fischer Targ (August 4, 1961 – July 13, 2002). Daughter of laser physicist and remote-viewing pioneer Russell Targ and Joan Fischer Targ (sister of chess champion Bobby Fischer). Stanford at 16. MD at 25. Psychiatrist, UCSF faculty, head of psychosocial oncology and director of the Complementary Medicine Research Institute at California Pacific Medical Center. Dead at 40 — two weeks short of 41 — of glioblastoma multiforme, the most malignant of brain cancers, the very disease she had been studying.

## A childhood with permission

Her father ran CIA-funded ESP experiments at Stanford in the 1970s. Elisabeth grew up inside that world: an ESP training machine at age 10, Christmas presents guessed before opening, hide-and-seek by clairvoyance. Bronson's line, via Russell: "She had more permission to be psychic than anyone in history." She was also, simultaneously, a prodigy of the straight world — skipping grades, lab work at 12, fluent in Russian, French, and German, translating Soviet parapsychology papers as a teenager. Both tracks were real. The dossier does not pick one to explain the other.

## The researcher

She chose AIDS in the mid-1990s because it was, in her words, a "gnarly disease" — medicine's greatest riddle. She ran the pilot, then the published trial, then won the NIH grants. She appeared on *Good Morning America* and *Larry King*, was written up in *Time*. She refused, always, to speculate about mechanism — her discipline was to ask whether an effect existed and leave the how to later. Colleagues on both sides respected the rigor of the attempt even when they disputed the conclusions.

## The diagnosis

March 2002: slurred speech, a slackening face, an MRI she could read herself. Grade IV glioblastoma, inoperable. She had spent the previous years learning everything about this cancer for her NIH trial. Now it was hers.

What followed is documented in Bronson's profile: the healers of the world descended — Lakota sun dancers, Russian psychics, acupuncturists, energy workers, her father's urgings to meditate. She tried them all while also enduring radiation that burned her scalp. She married Mark Comings, the theoretical physicist, on May 4, 2002, barely able to walk down the aisle. In her final weeks she could communicate only by hand squeezes and eye blinks. She died at 11:11 p.m., 111 days after diagnosis — a numeric coincidence her circle could not help but notice, two weeks short of her 41st birthday. (The birthday-cake dream had said 42; Bronson notes the near-miss without claiming it means anything.)

## The prayer question — honest verdict

Robert remembers that she "wrote a prayer as she was dying," and finds it powerful. The dossier checked this against the full Bronson text and the other sources. **It is not verified.** What the sources show:

- *Others* prayed for *her* — healing circles worldwide, Wednesday-night gatherings above Silicon Valley, websites tracking her progress, friends circling her body in prayer after death.
- She *received* ministry — telephoning her godmother, the Buddhist priest Joan Halifax, when suffering.
- Bronson's title, "A Prayer Before Dying," is the *author's* framing, not a description of her act.

She may well have prayed in her final months — a woman of her background, facing death, with a Buddhist godmother on the phone, almost certainly did in some form. But no source found in this pass documents her composing a prayer, and the dossier will not assert it. The record supports something nearly as strong: she died the way she lived — inside the question, letting the mystery stand, refusing to resolve it even for herself.

## Why she is a Boneyard case

A physician who brought the oldest human question — does intention reach across distance? — into peer review and NIH funding. A pilot with a miracle in it. A published study with real flaws and real courage. A replication that failed after her death. And the final irony, which needs no embellishment: the healer felled at forty by the exact disease she was trying to beat, mourned by the healers she had studied, prayed over by the circles she had measured. Death, the feller of all men, does not grade on effort.

# The Parents

**** Assembled with AI assistance from publicly available sources. Summarized in the dossier's own words; no copyrighted text reproduced. Verified Research Memorandum — Closed Internal Baseline [October 12, 2026]

## The red dress — verified, with corrections

Robert remembers Russell Targ telling, in public interviews, about forcing little Elisabeth into a red dress, her distress, and his later regret. The dossier traced this to its actual sources — and on the "public interviews" part, Robert's memory checks out.

Russell told the story on camera to Jeffrey Mishlove in the New Thinking Allowed interview "The Life, Death, and Afterlife of Elisabeth Targ" (recorded March 3, 2017), and published a written version the same year in the *Journal of Scientific Exploration* (Vol. 31, No. 2, pp. 293–295), in a paper titled "Apparent Communications from an Eager Spirit." Same incident, two tellings — the interview and the journal paper corroborate each other. It is not, in either telling, a confession of parental regret. It is presented as afterlife evidence.

The account, summarized from both: some years after Elisabeth's death, Jane Katra — Russell's writing partner and Elisabeth's friend and co-researcher — was at Duke University interviewing for a position when a staff member approached her with a message. The staffer described a tall woman with long dark hair, recently deceased, who was insistent: she had a message for her father, whom Jane knew. The message, in essence — *tell my father to remember when I was a little child and he and another person forced me to wear a red dress I didn't want to wear; it was traumatic for me; he should remember it too.*

Russell's telling of what actually happened: his mother — Elisabeth's grandmother, a publicist in New York — had sent a pretty red dress, with the idea that baby Elisabeth would wear it to meet grandma at the airport. Russell and his wife Joan tried to dress the child in it. Joan herself never wore dresses. Elisabeth, in Russell's telling, "wasn't having any of it, and ripped it off as soon as we let her loose." He calls the whole thing an absurd moment, an embarrassing caper from their early childrearing, something he and Joan never once discussed afterward.

The evidential point, for Russell: Joan had died five years before the Duke encounter. He was, by his account, the only living person who knew about the red dress incident. That a stranger could name it convinced him — in his words, via the Mishlove interview — of his daughter's post-mortem survival.

Corrections to the remembered version, stated plainly:

- Robert was right that Russell told it publicly: the March 2017 New Thinking Allowed interview is the on-camera telling, the JSE paper the written one.
- It was not Russell alone, and not discipline — it was both parents, dressing a small child for an airport greeting, at the grandmother's instigation. The child tore the dress off.
- The word "traumatic" belongs to the medium's reported message, not to Russell's own description. His words for it are "absurd" and "embarrassing."
- No source found in this pass shows Russell expressing the "I shouldn't have forced that" regret Robert remembers. In the paper, the incident functions as evidence, not as remorse.
- The dossier takes no position on the medium's claim. What is verified is that Russell published this account, in his own name, in a peer-reviewed journal of his field.

## The father: permission and pressure

Russell Targ — laser physicist, Lockheed pioneer, co-founder with Hal Puthoff of the SRI remote-viewing program, the CIA years — raised his daughter inside a household where psychic functioning was treated as ordinary. His proudest summary of the arrangement, given to Po Bronson: she had more permission to be psychic than anyone in history.

The texture of that permission, from Bronson's reporting: an ESP training machine — an early computer flashing one of four colors — which Elisabeth began working with at age ten. Hide-and-seek played by clairvoyance with a girlfriend. Christmas presents she was expected to name before opening; when she missed, her father teased her — *what's wrong with you?* His stated expectation of his daughter: intelligent, polite, and psychic. Bronson's gloss on the household: he was a force, he was famous, greatness was assumed — and her uncle was the world chess champion.

The dossier notes the double edge without resolving it, because the sources don't. The permission was total: no child in America grew up with fewer barriers between the strange and the normal. The pressure was total too: a famous father, a genius uncle, a family in which extraordinariness was the baseline. Elisabeth met the pressure — Stanford at sixteen, MD at twenty-five — and then aimed the permission at the hardest target available: prayer, in a clinical trial, with federal money.

Three generations of the strange: Russell's father William Targ, the Putnam editor who published a Blavatsky biography and von Däniken, introduced Russell to the paranormal through the family bookstore. The openness was inherited, not invented.

## The mother: Joan Fischer Targ

Joan Fischer Targ (1938–1998) — born in Moscow, older sister of Bobby Fischer, Master's in education, a pioneer in her own right: she founded computer-literacy programs in the Palo Alto schools and the Institute of Microcomputing in Education at Stanford. She died June 2, 1998, of a cerebral hemorrhage, at sixty — four years before her daughter.

What the record gives about Joan and Elisabeth specifically is thin, and the dossier will not invent closeness or distance. Two facts with human weight:

- Joan died four years before Elisabeth's diagnosis. Elisabeth did her NIH work, her trials, her television appearances, and faced her own cancer without her mother.
- The single domestic detail the sources preserve is from the red dress account: Joan never wore dresses. A mother who didn't wear dresses, a daughter who tore one off — the resemblance is the kind families recognize and never write down.

No source found in this pass records Elisabeth discussing her mother's influence on her path, or Joan discussing Elisabeth's work. If the relationship shaped the researcher, it did so off the record.

## Elisabeth's own words about her parents

Searched for and not found in this pass. Her published papers are technical and contain no autobiography. The interviews on record — Bronson above all — quote Russell about Elisabeth far more than Elisabeth about Russell. What she thought of being raised as the most psychically permitted child in history, in her own words, does not appear in the sources checked. The dossier marks this a genuine gap: the daughter's view of the father is the missing document.

## What the parents explain

They explain the *possibility* of her, not the *content*. A thousand children of famous scientists inherit permission; almost none aim it at distant healing in a peer-reviewed journal. Russell gave her the ontology — the universe may be nonlocal, intention may reach. Joan gave her, by all visible evidence, steadiness and her own pioneering streak in an unrelated field. What Elisabeth did with both — the rigor, the refusal to overclaim, the willingness to be attacked from both sides — was hers.

The red dress stays in the file for a different reason than Robert first thought. Not as a story about a father's regret, but as the last verified exchange between them: a daughter, dead, sending her father a message only he could understand, about a dress she tore off forty years before. Whether one believes the medium or not, Russell believed it. And a physicist who spent his career being disbelieved chose to publish it.

# The 1998 Study — Fairly Presented

**** Summarized from the published paper (Sicher et al., *West J Med* 1998;169(6):356–363; full text at PMC1305403) and Po Bronson's reporting. No copyrighted text reproduced.

## The pilot (1995, unpublished as a standalone)

In July 1995, Targ and co-workers enrolled 20 patients with advanced AIDS at UCSF in a randomized, double-blind pilot. Ten received standard care plus distant healing; ten received standard care alone. The healers lived an average of 1,500 miles from the patients. Over six months, four patients died — all four in the control group. All ten in the treatment group survived.

Caveat (from Bronson's later investigation): age was a confounding variable. Most participants were in their mid-twenties to early thirties; the four who died were the four oldest (three in their late thirties, one in his sixties) — and all four had landed in the control group. The pilot, in other words, may have shown mainly that older AIDS patients died sooner in the mid-1990s.

## The published study (1996–1997; West J Med, Dec 1998)

Forty patients with advanced AIDS. Design: each patient was computer-matched to a "statistical twin" (similar CD4+ count, age, prior complications); one of each pair was randomized to treatment, one to control. Forty healing practitioners — rabbis, Native American medicine men, bioenergetic healers, Christians, Jews, Buddhists, shamans — each spent one hour a day for six consecutive days directing healing intention at the treatment group's photographs, names, and lab data, rotating weekly for ten weeks so each patient received attention from ten practitioners. The healers never met the patients and were unpaid. Control-group photos sat in a locked drawer. Patients in both groups were free to pray for themselves and receive prayer from family — the design assumed a "baseline" of ordinary prayer for everyone.

What was measured (six-month follow-up): hospital days, doctor visits, and occurrence of 23 AIDS-related illnesses.

What was found:
- Control group: 68 hospital days for 35 AIDS-related illnesses.
- Treatment group: 10 hospital days for 13 illnesses.
- Doctor visits: 260 (control) vs. 185 (treatment).
- New illnesses: 12 (control) vs. 2 (treatment).
- Mood scores: markedly higher in the treatment group.
- No change in CD4 counts in either group — the virus itself was untouched.
- The difference was statistically significant at p < 0.05 (less than 1-in-20 by chance).
- Belief did not explain it: ~55% of patients in *both* groups believed they were being prayed for, and believers did no better than non-believers — undercutting a simple placebo reading.

The strangest detail: the largest differences appeared in the four months *after* the ten-week healing period ended, not during it.

Targ's own position, consistently: she refused to speculate about mechanism. Her line, in effect, was that the finding — if real — proved only one thing: that this should be studied more. She presented the data dozens of times and never offered a hypothesis.

## What the study was, in one sentence

The most rigorously designed distant-healing trial of its era — peer-reviewed, published in a mainstream medical journal, with striking positive results on secondary health outcomes — built on a pilot whose headline finding (all deaths in the control group) was likely confounded by age.

## What it was not

It was not a cure study (CD4 counts didn't move). It was not, as designed, even an illness study at first — see the criticisms section for how the endpoints were chosen.

# The Criticisms — Opponents Say, Counters Answer

**** Summarized in the dossier's own words from: Gardner's *Skeptical Inquirer* column (Mar/Apr 2001), Bronson's *Wired* investigation (Dec 2002), and the peer-review record. No copyrighted text reproduced. Each charge is stated as the opponent states it, then answered where an answer holds. Where no answer holds, the dossier says so plainly.

## How to read this section

Robert asked for the controversy structured so a reader sees the attack and the answer together — the way a footnote argues with the text. That is what follows. Nothing here is smoothed: the strongest charge against the 1998 study comes from a sympathetic writer, and the dossier does not blunt it.

## 1. The Texas sharpshooter

**Opponents say** (Po Bronson, reporting biostatistician Dan Moore, confirmed by Mark Comings): The study was designed to measure *mortality*. One month into the six-month trial, triple-drug antiretroviral therapy became standard practice and AIDS patients stopped dying — only one patient died, and the primary endpoint was dead on arrival. Moore then broke the randomization code and ran endpoint after endpoint: HIV physical-symptom scores (inconclusive), quality-of-life scores (inconclusive), mood scores (the treatment group did *worse*), CD4+ counts (no difference). Only then did hospital stays and doctor visits show significance. The 23-illness measure came later still, on the suggestion of an outside AIDS physician consulted *after* unblinding — and the team had not collected that data, so they went back through the charts retrospectively. Fred Sicher, who pored over those charts, had interviewed many of the patients up to three times and had seen the group assignments. The published paper lets the reader assume the study was designed all along to measure the 23 illnesses; it never states otherwise, and never mentions that this was the last endpoint tried, on data gathered after unblinding. Statisticians call this the sharpshooter's fallacy: fire at the barn wall, then paint the target around the cluster. Peer reviewer David Spiegel, told of the multiple endpoints after the fact, said it moved the work from "confirmatory" to "exploratory" — with three or four attempts, the significance bar should have been far stricter than 1-in-20.

**Counter:** Thin, and the dossier does not pretend otherwise. What can honestly be said: (a) Nothing in this account shows fraud — it shows the ordinary ways hopeful researchers fool themselves, which is a different and lesser charge, and the distinction matters. (b) Sicher disputed the worst of it: Bronson records that Sicher insists he could *not* recognize patients from their charts and never knew which group anyone was in — against Moore's worry that with only 40 patients, he could. The dossier reports both; it cannot adjudicate between them. (c) Targ's own biostatistician was the source of the criticism — the skepticism came from inside the team, which is to the team's credit.

**Where no counter holds:** The confirmatory reading of the 1998 study is dead. What was published as a positive trial was, by the standards Spiegel named, an exploratory study that found something suggestive. The results are *suggestive*, not *probative*. The dossier keeps that verdict without qualification.

---

## 2. The family business

**Opponents say** (Martin Gardner): Targ is the third generation of a psi-believing family — grandfather William to father Russell to Elisabeth — and her research is downstream of upbringing rather than independent inquiry. Gardner dwells on the childhood ESP games, the Christmas-present guessing, the grant-visualization schemes, to suggest she was looking for what she wanted to find: beliefs passing from parents to children, as they do in every religion.

**Counter:** Holds. A researcher's upbringing is not a methodological flaw. Double-blind randomization is specifically designed to neutralize experimenter belief — that is what the design is *for*. (Whether this particular trial held its blind is the sharpshooter question above, not the upbringing question.) Gardner's framing would disqualify anyone raised inside a tradition from studying it — a standard that ends most of science.

---

## 3. The prediction anecdotes

**Opponents say** (Gardner): The stories from her father's books — the 1980 Reagan-election "prediction," the Bay Meadows horse-race win — do not survive scrutiny. In both, she was handed the target object *after* the event; no transcript exists of everything she said beforehand, so cherry-picking cannot be ruled out.

**Counter:** None holds, and the dossier does not invent one. Gardner is right: the anecdotes are worthless as evidence. But they were never the study — they are biography. Even Targ's defenders should not lean on them, and this dossier does not.

## 5. No mechanism

**Opponents say** (Gardner): By her own admission, nobody has any notion of how healer and healee could connect across distance. An effect without a mechanism is not an effect.

**Counter:** Holds. "No known mechanism" is a statement about physics, not about data. Medicine is full of effects that preceded their mechanisms — aspirin, lithium, and in her era, much of what triple-drug therapy was doing. Targ's refusal to speculate was the correct scientific posture; mockery of it confuses humility with evasion. Bronson confirms she presented her data dozens of times and never once offered a hypothesis — discipline, not emptiness.

---

## 6. Taxpayer money

**Opponents say** (Gardner): Public money for this — Gardner tallies the funding with open distaste: roughly $800,000 from the Department of Defense, about $243,000 in first-year NIH money for the 150-patient HIV trial, about $203,000 first-year for the glioblastoma trial, $15,000 from the Templeton Foundation — taxpayer cash for prayer research. (Figures as Gardner reported them; the primary column text was not re-accessed in this pass.)

**Counter:** Holds in part. The funding bodies — NIH's Center for Complementary and Alternative Medicine, the DoD — judged the question worth asking through their own review processes. One can dispute that judgment without impugning the researcher who won the grants fairly. But the dossier concedes the underlying discomfort is legitimate: public money invites public scrutiny, and the scrutiny is what this section is.

---

## 7. The movement charge

**Opponents say** (Gardner, quoting correspondent Patrick Curry): Her work is a leading wedge of a nascent mystical movement — defective science leaking into medicine through an alliance of parapsychologists and mystics whose devotion is to belief, not evidence.

**Counter:** Holds. This is guilt by association. The trial's statistics stand or fall on the trial's methods, not on who cites the trial at conferences. Curry's charge describes a sociology; it does not touch a p-value.

---

## 8. The question itself is illegitimate

**Opponents say** (Gerald Weissmann, "NIH funding: not a prayer," *FASEB Journal* 2006): NIH should not fund prayer research at all — the question is not worth asking with public money, whatever the design.

**Counter:** Reframing, not refutation — and it is Targ's own. She never claimed proof of God or of prayer, only that the question deserved better studies. The extremists on both sides — prayer proven! / the question is illegitimate! — were arguing past her actual claim. The reasonable middle (below) was closer to her position than her attackers admit.

---

## The reasonable middle — not opponents

Richard Sloan (Columbia): religion comforts people, but "no really good, compelling evidence" links religious involvement to health outcomes. Herbert Benson (Harvard): believers fare better; whether prayer *itself* makes the difference remains unproven. These are not attacks on Targ; they are the careful center she herself occupied. The dossier records them because they are the standard her work should be measured against — and by that standard, she never overclaimed.

---

## Bottom line of this section

The 1998 study does not survive as proof of distant healing. The endpoint-shifting, revealed by Bronson, is fatal to the confirmatory reading — and the dossier states that without qualification or counter. What survives: a genuinely well-designed *attempt* at an impossibly hard question, run by a serious physician inside mainstream institutions, attacked from both sides, answered where answers hold, and left standing where they do not. That is the honest shape of the controversy, and the dossier will not reshape it.

# The Follow-Up — Plainly Stated

**** Summarized from the published record. No copyrighted text reproduced.

## What happened next

Targ did not defend the 1998 study as final. She did what a serious researcher does: she went for the bigger, better trial. In 2000 she won NIH funding — the first NIH money granted for distant-healing research that our search has found (Bronson: "The NIH had never granted money to study distant healing") — for two 150-patient trials: one a confirmation study in AIDS, one in glioblastoma, the brain cancer that had struck one of her pilot patients (who had, remarkably, recovered).

She died in 2002 before either trial read out.

## The AIDS replication (Astin et al., 2006)

Published four years after her death in *Alternative Therapies in Health and Medicine* (12(6):36–41; PMID 17131980), with Targ listed as a co-author, the larger randomized trial found:

- **No difference** between the distant-healing group and the control group in longevity, symptoms, or any clinically meaningful outcome.
- The **only** statistically significant difference: patients who received distant healing were more likely to *guess* they were in the experimental group — a belief effect, not a healing effect.

## What this means, honestly

The replication failed. The 1998 findings did not hold at larger scale with cleaner methods. This is the single most important fact in the dossier after the study itself, and it is stated without qualification: **the effect did not replicate.**

## What it does not mean

- It does not mean the 1998 trial was fraudulent. (See the criticisms section: the flaws were in endpoint selection and blinding discipline, not fabrication.)
- It does not mean the question is closed forever. Failed replications close *studies*, not *questions* — though they properly move the question back to "unproven."
- It does not erase the pioneering fact (see the pioneer section): she got the question inside the walls — peer-reviewed journals, NIH funding, a major medical center. That had not been done before at that level, on those terms.

## The glioblastoma trial

The NIH-funded distant-healing trial in glioblastoma patients — the cancer that killed her — was taken over by colleagues after her death. Its published outcome, if any, was not traced in this pass; this is an open item for further research.

# Pioneer Framing — Why It Stands

****

## The claim

Elisabeth Targ was a pioneer in bringing prayer, intention, and distant healing into legitimate clinical discussion at major medical institutions. This stands regardless of the replication outcome. Failed replications close studies; they do not un-happen the door she opened.

## The evidence for the pioneering role

1. **Peer review at the mainstream level.** The 1998 study passed review at the *Western Journal of Medicine* — not a fringe outlet — with Stanford's David Spiegel as primary reviewer, who called it the best-designed distant-healing study he had seen. Whatever its flaws (and they were real), it met the bar of its day.

2. **First NIH money for the question.** Her 2000 grant — $1.5 million for two 150-patient trials — was, by Bronson's account and none found after research, the first NIH funding awarded to study distant healing. Institutions do not fund questions they consider unaskable; after her, the question had a grant number.

3. **A major medical center as base.** She did this as head of psychosocial oncology and director of the Complementary Medicine Research Institute at California Pacific Medical Center — inside the walls, not outside them.

4. **Rigor as the standard.** Even her critics concede the *attempt* was serious: randomized, double-blind (in design), placebo-controlled in effect, with a biostatistician playing designated skeptic. The field's later work had to meet the bar she set, not the bar she cleared.

5. **Intellectual honesty about limits.** She never claimed proof of God, of prayer, or of mechanism. Her public position — that the work proved only that the question deserved better studies — is the rarest thing in contested science: a researcher who wouldn't overclaim her own positive result.

## What pioneering does not require

It does not require being right. It does not require replication. It requires going first, in earnest, where the institutions said not to go — and leaving the path marked for whoever comes next. By that measure, which is the only honest one, she qualifies.

## A note on "first"

The dossier makes no "first" claim in the crown sense. If a primacy-adjacent statement is ever needed, the house wording applies: *none found after research* — meaning no earlier instance of NIH-funded, peer-reviewed distant-healing trials at a major medical center has surfaced, not that none could exist.
