Chapter 29 — Dispelling Insufficiency (散不足）

The Lord Grand Secretary said: "I took the Worthies to be somewhat closer to the mark, yet you have matters exactly backwards, light and dark, like the bells on a Hu carriage jingling as they follow one another down the road. Have you scholars never seen the cicada of late summer? Its voice goes into the ear, and when autumn comes, the voice is gone. What has been born does not easily change its speech — you pay no heed to the trouble, and when the trouble arrives you fall mute. Too late."

The Worthies said: "Confucius, reading the historian's record, sighed with a long breath, grieving that true virtue was discarded and ruler and minister stood in peril. For the wise man and gentleman takes the whole world as his charge. A great charge thinks far; thinking far, he forgets what is near. With a sincere heart, grieved and pitying, compassion is added to all — therefore the loyal heart stands alone and unentangled. This is why the poet grieved and made his song, and why Bi Gan and Wu Zixu left their bodies behind and forgot disaster. When they so hated this haste in wearing men out, how could they keep silent? The Odes say: 'My grieving heart is as if scorched; I dare not sport or jest.' Confucius went restlessly from state to state because the age was sick. Mozi hurried to and fro because he pitied the world."

The Lord Grand Secretary was silent.

The Chancellor said: "I would hear you on Dispelling Insufficiency."

The Worthies said: "Palaces and rooms, carriages and horses, clothing and vessels, mourning and sacrifice, food and drink, music and delight — these are things human feeling cannot be rid of. Therefore the sage made measures and forms to fence them in. Lately the officers and great men have grown busy with power and profit and lax in rite and right; so the common people imitate them, and many exceed the measures. Now let us set it out in order:

"In ancient times, grain, vegetables, and fruit were not eaten out of season; birds, beasts, fish, and turtles were not eaten if taken out of season. So snares and nets did not go into the marshes; mixed-haired game was not taken. Now the rich drive and hunt and set their nets everywhere; they trap fawns and nestlings in ambush, sink themselves in wine, and spread their boats over the hundred streams — fresh lamb, nursling kid, young shoulder, yellow-mouthed dove; in spring goose, in autumn pullets; in winter winter-greens and warmed leeks — dredging out water-chestnuts, shepherd's-purse, and wild greens, fruits with their husks and otter furs.

"In ancient times, men thatched untrimmed rafters and lived in earth-dug caves with pottery propped beside them — enough to keep off cold and heat and shelter from wind and rain, and that was all. In the later age, the rafters were not hewn nor the thatch clipped; there was no work of hewing or polishing. The great man's hall had exposed beams for rafters, the scholar-gentleman's a rough-forged headpiece, the common man's a timber frame of split wood. Now the rich pile beams on wells, carve railings and shields, and whiten and plaster their walls.

"In ancient times, what did not fit the rule in clothing and what did not fit its use in vessels was not sold in the market. Now the people carve and engrave useless things and paint trifles; dark-yellow mixed with blue, five-colored embroidered clothes; puppeteers' womenfolk and mixed crowds; beasts and horse-tricks, fighting tigers, conjurers chasing men, strange worms and Hu dancing-girls.

"In ancient times, a feudal lord did not grain-feed his horses; the Son of Heaven, when he had occasion, sent his carriage to pasture. A common man's riding horse was just enough to replace his labor. So on the move it bore the halter, and at rest it went to the plow. Now the rich hitch teams of carriages and columns of riders, with outriders and closed carriages beside. The middling sort have light carts and short hubs, flowing manes and bound hooves. A single horse at its manger eats the food of a middling family's six mouths and takes the work of one able-bodied man.

"In ancient times, a common man wore silk only in old age; the rest was hemp and flax — hence the name 'hemp-clad.' In the later age, silk within and hemp without, straight collars without pendants, robes joined and unhemmed. Robes of gauze and figured silk are the dress of ruler and consort; cocoons, crepe, and fine linen are the festive adornment of marriage. Therefore patterned gauze and thin weaves were not sold in the market. Now the rich wear damask-embroidered gauze, the middling plain silk and ice-gauze. Common folk wear a consort's dress; base men occupy marriage's adornment. Patterned silk costs twice plain silk, and plain silk wears twice as long as patterned.

"In ancient times, the rude cart had no supple parts, the stacked carriage no posts. Later, wooden axles were unclothed, long hubs with many spokes, mats of rushes and covers of bamboo — no lacquer or silk in the ornament. Officers and scholars had plain-wood fittings and dishes of leather. Commoners lacquered carts with big axles and Shu wheels. Now the rich commoner has silver-yellow blossoms by the left rail, knotted tassels and wrapped poles; the middling sort have inlaid bits and painted axle-covers, earrings on the traces and flying hubs.

"In ancient times, deer-hide coats and hide caps with the hooves left on. Later, officers and scholars had fox and badger sewn at the seams, lamb and leopard at the sleeves. Commoners wore felted wool, ram-skins and hide boots. Now the rich wear sable and sea-otter, fox-white and wild-goose down; the middling wear felted cloth with golden thread, and yellow furs traded from Dai.

"In ancient times, the base rider had rope reins and leather girths and hide mats, and that was all. Later, leather saddles with yak-hair trim and unadorned iron bits. Now the rich have leather with silver studs, golden mouthpieces on patterned bridles, felt embroidered saddle-cloths — bright and showy; the middling paint lacquered leather with corded ties and sun-dried paintings.

"In ancient times, muddy cups and gourd drinking, with no goblets, cups, trays, or stands. Later, commoners' vessels were bamboo, willow, pottery, and gourd; only with the ancestral hu-lian vessels and sacrificial bowls did carving and red lacquer appear. Now the rich have silver mouths and golden ears, metal wine-pots and jade cups; the middling have Yewang cloth vessels and gold-inlaid Shu cups. One patterned cup costs ten bronze cups; the price is cheap, the use no different. Ji Zi's reproach began at the Son of Heaven; now it is in the common man's house.

"In ancient times, men baked grain and ate millet, and roasted a pig to feast one another. Later, at village drinking the old had heaped dishes and the young stood to eat — one sauce, one meat, drinking in turns. Later still, at feasts and weddings, bean-soup and white rice, minced fish and cooked meat. Now at the people's drinking and eating, dishes overlap in courses; roasts fill the board — braised turtle, minced carp, fawn's eggs, quail and partridge, oranges and citrons, pickled fish and preserved meats — a crowd of things with mixed flavors.

"In ancient times, commoners plowed and weeded in spring and summer, gathered and stored in autumn and winter, worked from dawn to dusk and through the night. The Odes say: 'In the day you gather the thatch grass; at night you twist the rope; hurry to roof the house — then you may sow the hundred grains.' Except at the year-end feast and sacrifices they did not rest, and except at sacrifices there was no wine or meat. Now at feasts and weddings, wine and food follow one after another; half their tasks are thrown away following the drink, their days spent fearing no shortage of occasions.

"In ancient times, commoners ate coarse food and potherbs; except at village drinking, the year-end feast, and sacrifices there was no wine or meat. Therefore a feudal lord did not kill an ox or sheep without cause; an officer or scholar did not kill a dog or pig without cause. Now in the lanes and side-streets men slaughter at market stalls without cause and feast together in the wilds; they carry grain to go and bring meat home. The meat of one pig is worth a middling year's harvest; fifteen bushels of grain is half a month's food for an able-bodied man.

"In ancient times, commoners offered fish and beans in sacrifice and in spring and autumn tended their ancestral shrines; a scholar had one temple, an officer three, and in season served the five household sacrifices; there were no sacrifices beyond the gate. Now the rich pray at famous peaks and gaze at mountains and rivers, beat oxen and drums, and perform dances with actors and images. The middling sort dwell by the roadside, with water-towers and cloud-terraces, slaughtering sheep and dogs to the music of lute and flute. The poor have chickens and pigs with the five aromatics; the village guardian scatters the offering, and the whole community gathers at the shrine-ground.

"In ancient times, men sought fortune through virtue, so their sacrifices were generous; they sought luck through right, so their divinations were few. Now the age is generous in conduct only toward ghosts: lax in the rites and earnest in sacrifice, slighting their kin and honoring the powerful, reaching the height of absurdity in believing in lucky days, listening to lying talk and hoping for gain, spending solid goods to feast on empty fortune.

"In ancient times, the gentleman rose early and lay late, diligent in his virtue; the small man rose and set with the sun, diligent in his labor. So the gentleman did not eat unearned food, and the small man did not eat idle food. Now the age decorates false conduct and works fraud; men become shamans and invocators for the people to gather fees and thanks, with strong hard tongues, some building fortunes by it — so those who hate work leave the root and learn from them. Therefore every street has its shaman, every ward its invocator.

"In ancient times, there were no beds with bolsters nor tables with screens; later, commoners had plain-wood beds and carved birch tables; scholars were not planed, officers had reed mats only. Now the rich have damask-embroidered curtains and screens painted with inlaid patterns; the middling have brocade curtains hung high, painted with cinnabar lacquer.

"In ancient times, hides and grass mats, without the addition of mats or the beauty of rushes; later, officers and scholars had double grass-woven mats, and commoners grass mats with rope binding and single reed mats. Now the rich have embroidered mats and supple feathers, rush mats on open beds; the middling have beach skins in place of felt, and sit flat on fine mats.

"In ancient times, cooked food was not sold, nor food marketed; later there were butchers and wineshops, selling wine, dried meat, fish, and salt, and that was all. Now cooked food fills the stalls and dishes fill the markets; work is slackened and abandoned, eating seeks the season — poached pig and leek eggs, dog and horse meat, fried fish and sliced liver, brined mutton and chilled chicken, mare's milk and wine, and strange market dishes without end.

"In ancient times, earth drums and clods for drumsticks, striking wood and slapping stone — to exhaust their joy. Later, ministers and officers had pipes and bells, scholars zithers and lutes. Of old, at the people's wine gatherings each followed his party's custom, playing the zither and beating the earthen pot, and that was all — no seductive sounds, no modulating turns. Now the rich have bells, drums, and the five musics, companies of singing girls; the middling sound the mouth-organ and tune the lute, with Zheng dances and Zhao songs.

"In ancient times, an earthenware coffin to hold the corpse, wooden boards for the outer case — enough to gather the frame and hide the hair and teeth. Later, paulownia coffins were unclothed and colored outer-cases unhewn. Now the rich have embroidered walls and close-fitted timbers; the middling catalpa coffins and nanmu cases; the poor paint their shrouds and robe their dead, with silk pouches and lined bags.

"In ancient times, grave-goods had form and no substance, showing the people they could not be used. Later there were stores of pickled goods, paulownia horses and puppet men for sacrifices, their things incomplete. Now men store richly and furnish the dead as if alive. In the provinces the corvée officers pour plain mulberry into puppet carriages with chariot wheels; a common man without means dresses wooden figures in silk.

"In ancient times, there was no mound and no trees; the return sacrifice was in the sleeping chamber, with no altar-house or temple-throne. Later, mounds were made; a commoner's grave was half a fathom high and could hide a man. Now the rich pile earth into mountains and plant trees into forests, with terraces and pavilions joined to towers, gathering vistas and adding stories. The middling have shrine-halls joined by screens, walls, gates, and lattice screens.

"In ancient times, when a neighbor had mourning, pounding did not answer pounding and the lane did not sing. Confucius, eating beside a mourner, never ate his fill; on a day when he had wept, he did not sing. Now the age exploits other men's mourning to beg wine and meat, sits down gladly and contests the bill, with singing, dancing, and actors, joined laughter and theatrical tricks.

"In ancient times, the boundary between men and women was lofty; the marriage garments were never recorded in the books. After Yu and Xia, there was surface cloth and inner silk, bone hairpins and ivory earrings; the wives of enfeoffed lords added brocade and still wore the plain skirt. Now the rich wear hide clothes and vermilion furs, heavy dew and ringed pendants; the middling long skirts and crossed pendants, jade disks and hairpins and earrings.

"In ancient times, in serving the living men gave all their love; in sending off the dead, all their grief. Therefore the sage made measured restraint — not empty display. Now in life men cannot give love and respect; in death they compete in extravagance; and though without a grieving heart, those who bury thickly and gift heavily are called filial, their names displayed to the world and their glory manifest to the age. So the people imitate one another in admiration, until they tear down houses and sell their property.

"In ancient times, the bond of husband and wife: one man, one woman, and the way of the household was complete. Later, a scholar had one concubine, an officer two, a feudal lord nine women of the wifely clans, and that was all. Now feudal lords have hundreds, ministers and officers dozens, the middling keep attendants, the rich fill their chambers. Therefore some women are left desolate and lose their season; some men die wandering without a mate.

"In ancient times, in barren years things were incomplete and in fat years the loss was made good; the old measures were followed and nothing new was made. Now craftsmen change and transform while clerks' hearts differ; they spoil finished works to conceal their intent. Their intent goes to the utmost of merit-making, their business to outward show. They pile up works to sell their names and do not pity the people's urgency. The fields are not opened, yet they decorate pavilions and hamlets; settlements lie as mounds of ruin, yet they raise their city walls high.

"In ancient times, men's labor was not spent on birds and beasts; the people's wealth was not taken to feed dogs and horses. Therefore wealth overflowed and strength had surplus. Now fierce beasts and strange worms, which cannot plow or weed, are fed by those who should be plowing and weeding. The people may lack whole cloth for short coats, while dogs and horses wear patterned embroidery; the people may have no gruel to follow their bran, while birds and beasts eat grain and meat.

"In ancient times, the ruler honored service and loved those below him; he employed the people in season. The Son of Heaven took the world as his household, and chamberlains each in their season furnished their public service — the common rule of past and present. Now the offices keep many bondservants and maidservants, who sit receiving clothes and food, privately pursue their own trades for base profit, work without exhausting their strength, and the offices are cheated of the truth. The people may have no store of a peck or a basket, while government slaves pile up a hundred in gold; the people do not let go their labor from dawn to dusk, while the bondservants loaf with folded arms.

"In ancient times, men drew near to their kin and held the distant at arm's length; they honored their own kind and held aliens cheap. They did not reward the meritless nor feed the useless. Now the Man and Mo barbarians, without merit, occupy markets in the offices, with broad houses and great mansions, sitting to receive clothes and food. The people may be unprovided from morning to night while the barbarians are glutted with wine and meat. The people work under dripping sweat; the barbarians cross their legs and sprawl.

"In ancient times, commoners had coarse grass sandals and bound silk and prized leather, and that was all; later, bordered shoes were not borrowed — pulled shoes and leather sandals. Now the rich have leather worked by famous craftsmen, light and fine to fit the foot, silk linings and bound soles, with crossed cords and straight patterns; the middling have Deng linings and hurried matting sandals; clumsy slaves and maidservants wear leather stacks and silk shoes; runners wear trimmed grass bindings.

"The ancient sage labored his body and nourished his spirit; he restrained desire and fitted his feelings; he honored Heaven and reverenced Earth, walked in virtue and practiced benevolence. Therefore Heaven above took pleasure in it, prolonged his line, and made his years abundant. So Yao, with fine brows and high brightness, enjoyed his state a hundred years. Then the First Emperor of Qin looked on strange wonders and trusted in omens and portents; he sent Master Lu to seek Xianmen Gao, and Xu Shi and others into the sea to seek the drug of deathlessness. At that time the men of Yan and Qi laid down their hoes and plows and contended to speak of immortals. The formula-masters then flocked to Xianyang by the thousands, saying the immortals eat gold and drink pearls and so live as long as Heaven and Earth. Thereupon he toured and hunted the Five Peaks repeatedly, built shore-side halls, sought the immortals of Penglai and their kin. In the counties he repeatedly visited, the rich furnished funds and the poor built the roadside. Afterward, the small fled and vanished, the great hid themselves away; the officials pursuing and searching hauled them in without principle. Beside the famous palaces, cottages and hamlets stood; no seedlings grew and no trees stood. The people's hearts left him, and those who resented and thought of him were more than half. The Book says: 'To offer much ceremony where ceremony does not reach the thing is called not offering.' Therefore the sage carries nothing but benevolence and right upon himself, and sets nothing but the true way before him. This is why the Former Emperor executed Wen Cheng and Wu Li, and why Emperor Xuan established the learned offices and drew near to the loyal and good — wishing to cut off the root of the strange and evil and light the road of utmost virtue."

"The palace in extravagance is the borer of forests; vessels in carving are the borer of wealth; clothing in splendor is the borer of cloth; dogs and horses eating men's food are the borer of the five grains; the mouth and belly indulged are the borer of fish and meat; expense unrestrained is the borer of the treasury; drains forbidden nothing are the borer of the fields; mourning and sacrifice without measure are the borer that wounds life; idleness completing change wounds work, and artisans reaching upward wounds farming. Therefore one cup of the patterned sort takes a hundred men's labor; one screen is finished by ten thousand men's work — their harm is truly great! The eye is trimmed to the five colors, the ear is busy with the five tones, the body reaches the utmost thinness, the mouth the utmost sweetness; work piles up on the useless, wealth is spent on the unnecessary; the mouth and belly cannot be made much. So when the state sickens, accumulated insufficiency makes government slothful; when the man sickens, accumulated insufficiency endangers his body."

The Chancellor said: "How is accumulated insufficiency to be governed?"

Chapter 30 — Relieving Want （救匱）

The Worthies said: "It is said: what bends the crooked makes straight; what rescues the patterned is plainness. In old times Yan Ying was minister of Qi and wore one fox coat for thirty years. So when the people are extravagant, show them frugality; when the people are frugal, show them the rites. Nowadays, if the sons and grandsons of dukes, ministers, and officers could truly restrain their carriages and horses, fit their clothing, be frugal in person, lead by honest simplicity, do away with gardens and ponds, reduce fields and houses, have nothing to do with the market rows within and nothing to do with the mountains and marshes without — then the farmer would have somewhere to apply his work and the woman worker somewhere to sell her product. If it were so, then breath and pulse would be at peace, and there would be no sickness of accumulated insufficiency."

The Lord Grand Secretary said: "The orphan speaks of filial piety, the lame man of his staff, the poor man of benevolence, the base man of governing. What is debated apart from oneself is easily praised; judgment from the sideline is easily approved; but meet the matter head-on and there is confusion. So Gong Sunhong wore a cloth coverlet and Ni Kuan a hemp robe, dressed like servants and maidservants and eating like hired hands. Huainan rebelled within; the Man and Yi raged without; bandits were not forbidden; luxury was not restrained. Like the shaman of a plague year, able only to work his mouth and ears — how can such men govern the insufficiency?"

The Worthies said: "In the time of the High Emperor, Xiao and Cao were the dukes; Teng and Guan and their kind were the ministers — in good order, which is to say, they were worthies. In the age of Wen and Jing and at the beginning of Jianyuan, the great ministers still had the rightness of contending and citing and holding to the true; after that time, most bore the intent of compliance and followed desire, few daring to speak directly, face the debate, and reprove to the point, bending the public to follow the private. So the Chancellor Wu'an pleaded his garden and fields and contested right and wrong before his lord. A nine-story tower, once it tilts, even Gongshu cannot set it straight; the root court, once it leans, even Yi Yin and the Duke cannot restore it. So Chancellor Gong Sun and Officer Ni kept their bodies in the way, divided their salary to nourish worthies, lowered themselves to receive scholars; their works stood manifest; their days lacked strength; there was no successor to Zigong and Zichan among men. And Ge Yi and Peng Hou and their kind broke the thread of it and tangled its order, demolished their guest lodges and debate halls to make horse stables and women's quarters, without the rite of nourishing scholars but full of proud and arrogant looks; propriety and shame decayed and men fought over profit. So good fields and broad houses left the people nowhere to go; those unashamed of profit filled court and market, those arraying fields and livestock filled the provinces; violent seizing and hauling, and beside the great houses and huge mansions the roads themselves do not pass. This is truly hard to cure; it cannot be worked upon."

The Lord Grand Secretary, flushed with anger, was silent and made no reply.

Chapter 31 — The Needle and the Stone （箴石）

The Chancellor said: "I have heard an elder of Zheng say: 'The gentleman composes his countenance and keeps violence and contempt at a distance; he brings out his words and breath and keeps vulgarity and perversity at a distance.' So words may be told and conduct may be copied. This is what the officers wished from the beginning to see. As for the swordsmen's talk and the gamblers' debate, with fine looks flattering one another, standing on power and not yielding to one another — they make the officers unable to take up the Worthies' counsel, and the Worthies and Literati get the name of being unyielding. For the scholars' sake, I do not take to this. Gong Sunlong said: 'Argument as a way is disputation, and so one must not fail to attend to it; attending, be generous to one another; being generous, they return to contention; contention without yielding enters vulgarity.' Now the officers show no benevolence and moreover bear the reputation of eating unearned rice; there is nothing more to demand in expiation of the disgrace. The offices have summoned Worthies and Literati — and as for reaching the people's great officers, we have not yet seen any who can use the needle and the stone to cure the people's sickness."

The Worthies said: "Jia Yi said: 'Sincere words make the language shallow and it does not enter; deep words go against the ear and miss the point.' So it is said: 'How hard it is to speak!' Even speaking is not easy; how much more putting it into practice? This is how Hu Jian could not get his death, and how Wu Chen barely escaped trouble. The saying goes: 'Five robbers hold one good man' — the crooked wood hates the straight cord. Now if we would use the needle and the stone and open the passages, we fear the trouble of Sheng Guan and Hu Mu; carrying needle-case and mugwort, we get the name of being unskilled. 'The wolf trips on its dewlap and stumbles on its tail.' The gentleman's road — the way of going and stopping — is narrow indeed. This is why Zishi sighed."

Chapter 32 — Removing Narrowness （除狹）

The Lord Grand Secretary said: "The wise man dwells in the great forest and meets wind and thunder without losing his way; the foolish man, even on a broad and spacious road, is sunk in dark confusion. Now the prefects and chancellors in person split the tally and receive the salute, presiding over the multitude of a province — the station of the ancient regional lords. They receive command and govern alone, cutting and ruling a thousand li, unimpeded from within. Good and evil are in their own hands. If they themselves cannot do it, whose fault is it? Where is the narrowness in the way?"

The Worthies said: "In ancient times, men advanced as scholars were chosen by the district and selected by the village; their talent and ability were debated, and then they were given office; equal to the task, then they were enfeoffed and salaried. So scholars cultivated themselves in the countryside and rose to the court; their conduct in seclusion was bright enough to show; from the near and the far no scholar was lost, and in small and great no merit was left behind. Therefore the worthy were advanced and used, and the unworthy were set aside and dismissed. Now the official road is mixed and unselective: the rich buy office with wealth; the brave shoot for merit with death. Stage-drivers and cauldron-lifters climb out to fill clerkships; accumulating merit day by day, some reach minister and chancellor. Trailing blue cords and wearing silver seals, they hold the handle of life and death and command the fate of the ten thousand people. The weak among them are like a wolf set to shepherd sheep — their disorder is certain. The strong are like a madman's sharp sword put in his hand — they will kill wantonly. Therefore in times past the people's suffering in the provinces could not be governed; some reached saw-necks, killing the innocent, and could not be corrected. Those who hold the reins do not walk their way, and the covering disorder grows worse. In ancient times, to enfeoff the worthy and salary the able reached no more than a hundred li; within a hundred li there was a capital, the border reaching no more than fifty — and still it was thought that one man's person cannot shine with knowledge to the full nor hear to the end, so marshals, officers, and scholars were set up to assist him, and governance was complete. Now the prefect or chancellor may be without the wisdom of the ancient feudal lords, yet he governs the rule of a thousand li, presides over the multitude of a province, applies the sage ruler's virtue, and wields the law of life and death — a heavy thing. None but a benevolent man can bear it; none but the right man can carry it. One man's body — order and disorder are in him; a thousand li turns and transforms with him; he must be chosen with ripe care. So the ruler has private men with wealth, but does not make private men of office; he hangs out rewards to await merit and ranks titles to await the worthy; he promotes the good as if it were not enough, dismisses the evil as if they were enemies — truly lest they, without merit, maim the people. To support the ruler's virtue and open the road of his ministers lies in choosing the worthy and using them as vessels; select and practice prefects and chancellors, and only then appoint them."

Chapter 33 — Against Greed （疾貪）

The Lord Grand Secretary said: "So. A man is a physician because he is clumsy, yet seeks much in fees; he is an official because he is often bad, yet encroaches and preys on the people. The senior official presses the junior officials; the junior officials press the people. So do not worry that the choice was not ripe, but that what is sought and what is got are different; do not worry that they are insufficient, but that they are greedy without satiety."

The Worthies said: "When in ancient times titles and salaries were fixed, a minister or officer had enough to enrich worthies and reward scholars; a scholar enough to better himself and his party; a common man as an official enough to replace his farming and eat his salary. Now the junior clerks' salaries are thin; the provinces' corvée is far, reaching the Three Supports; grain is dear, not enough to provide for one another. Commonly dwelling, they are straitened in clothes and food; when trouble comes, they sell livestock and sell their property. Not only this: corvée sends men back and forth, the tribunal summons and arrests, junior clerks with small accounts, on their rounds begging loans; the senior officials encroach and prey, the upper prefecture demands of the county below, the county demands of the district — where is the district to get it? The saying goes: 'Bribes flow downward, like water running downhill — they do not stop until exhausted.' Now the great rivers drink the huge sea; the huge sea receives it; and you would have the valleys and brooks be polite about the streams and pools — the incorruptness of the hundred officials cannot be got. If you would have the shadow straight, straighten the form; if you would have those below honest, first go yourself. So greed and meanness are in the leader, not those below; the teaching is in the government, not the people."

The Lord Grand Secretary said: "Worthy and unworthy have their substance, greed and meanness their nature; the gentleman keeps himself clean within and cannot purely teach the other. So the Duke of Zhou was not unable to correct the evil of Guan and Cai; Zichan was not unable to correct the falsity of Deng Xi. Those who within do not follow the teaching of father and elder brother, and without do not fear the crime of the penal law — the Duke of Zhou and Zichan cannot transform them; it must be so. Now to blame the officers one by one — can the officers bind their hands and feet and make them do no wrong?"

The Worthies said: "If the team of horses is unruly, it is the driver's fault. If the people are ungoverned, it is the officers' crime. The Spring and Autumn blames and mocks do not reach the commoners; it blames their leaders. So in ancient times, when an officer was about to face punishment, music was not enjoyed; the punishment fit, yet three times round he sighed and lamented. He was ashamed that he could not transform, and grieved that it was incomplete. The government teaching is dark and does not shine forth; the people stumble and fall and are not held up, like a baby at a well's edge, listening to it go in. If it is so, how are they the people's father and mother? So the gentleman is urgent in teaching and slow in punishment. One punishment corrects a hundred; one killing cautions ten thousand. This is why the Duke of Zhou executed Guan and Cai, and Zichan executed Deng Xi. One act of punishment applied, and the people follow rite and right. The transformation of those above upon those below is like the wind bending the grass — nothing does not follow the teaching. Why bind them one by one?"


* * *



Chapter 34 — Putting Punishment Last （後刑）

The Lord Grand Secretary said: "The gentlemen of old loved the good and hated the evil. A ruler does not keep evil men; a farmer does not keep useless seedlings. A useless seedling is the seedling's harm; a useless man is the people's thief. Hoe one harm away and the many seedlings grow; punish one evil and the ten thousand people rejoice. Even the Duke of Zhou and Confucius could not let go punishment and use evil. A house that has a rebellious servant, its vessels are not in place — how much more a rebellious people! The people are haughty toward love and listen to punishment. So punishment is what corrects the people; the hoe is what separates the seedlings."

The Worthies said: "In ancient times, men were guided by generous teaching and the clear law corrected punishment. Punishment in governing is like the goad in driving. A good workman cannot drive without a goad — or having a goad, not use it. The sage borrows the law to complete teaching; teaching complete, punishment is not applied. So he is awesome without killing; punishment is set up and not incurred. Now men discard the guiding reins and cannot stretch them, spoil rite and right and cannot guard them. The people fall into a net, and then they are hunted with punishment — like opening the pen and letting loose poisoned arrows, not stopping until all is done. Zengzi said: 'Above has lost its way; the people have scattered long ago. If you get at the truth of it, grieve and pity them — do not rejoice.' Not to grieve that the people are ungoverned, but to cut at one's own ability to catch the cunning, is like a fowler rejoicing to see birds and beasts hanging in his nets and snares. Those under heaven who are now punished need not have the evil of Guan and Cai or the falsity of Deng Xi; I fear the seedlings are all gone without discrimination and the people are cheated and ungoverned. Confucius said: 'A man without benevolence: to hate him too fiercely is disorder.' So when the people are disordered, return to the government; when the government is disordered, return to the man himself; the man himself made right, and the world is settled. Therefore the gentleman honors the good and pities the unable; his grace reaches the punished man, his virtue moistens the poor husband; he applies favor and is gladdened by it; he carries out punishment and is not gladdened."


* * *



Chapter 35 — Timely Labor （授時）

The Lord Grand Secretary said: "Together sharing the land, dwelling in this age, without disaster, calamity, or sickness, being poor alone: unless lazy, then extravagant. Without strange trades or side-income, yet being rich and provided: unless frugal, then diligent. Now you say, 'apply favor and be gladdened; carry out punishment and not be gladdened' — that is to pity men without conduct and to feed a lazy and extravagant people. So to give recklessly is not favor; to favor the evil is not benevolence."

The Worthies said: "The flourishing of the Three Dynasties had no sprout of disorder — teaching. The last age of Xia and Shang had no obedient people — custom. Therefore the king established schools and dormitories, made teaching bright, fenced in and guided his people; and when government teaching soaked through, their nature was benevolent and they understood the good. So when rite and right stand, the plowman yields in the field; when rite and right spoil, the gentleman contends in the court. Men contending make disorder; disorder makes the world unequal — so some are poor and some rich. Rich, then benevolence is born; provided, then contention stops. In the dusk, knocking at men's doors, asking for water and fire, even a greedy man does not grudge it. Why? Because he has plenty. If in governing you make beans and grain like water and fire, how could the people be without benevolence!"

The Lord Grand Secretary said: "Those who gamble, game, and race after pleasure are all the sons of rich families, not the insufficient. So when the people are rich, they crowd into insolence; rich, they grow proud and extravagant, sitting coiling like snakes and rising to do wrong — I have never seen their benevolence. To dwell in things without working, to use wealth without measure — though they have wealth like water and fire, poverty can stand and wait for them. If the people do not store, though the officers help them plow and weave, can they make them sufficient?"

The Worthies said: "When the Duke of Zhou was minister to King Cheng, the people were rich and happy; the state had no poor men — it was not that he plowed and wove in their place. He changed their fields and paddies, lightened their taxes and levies, and the people were rich. Above, they served ruler and kin; below, there was no trouble of hunger and cold; then teaching could be completed. The saying goes: 'Having made them rich, what then do you add? He said, Teach them.' Teach them by virtue and align them by rite, and the people will follow rightness and pursue the good; none does not enter filial and go out brotherly. What extravagance, insolence, or roughness could there be? Guanzi says: 'Granaries full, and men know rite and measure; the people sufficient, and they know honor and shame.' So a rich people is easily brought to the rites."

The Lord Grand Secretary said: "The offices toward the people are like a loving father toward his sons: loving them, how could he not teach them? Loving them, yet not make them work? So in spring he plows in person to encourage farming, lends and gives to provide for the insufficient, opens up the dammed waters and releases the light offenders, making the people attend to the season. Receiving grace and covered by favor, to this day they are still poor and straitened — how hard it is to walk the way with them!"

The Worthies said: "In ancient times, in spring they surveyed the plowing to make up the insufficient; in autumn they surveyed the harvest to aid the ungiven. If the people are diligent in wealth, contributions and taxes are light; if the people are diligent in labor, works and buildings are rare. For the people's sake they loved labor-power and did not seize a moment. So the Earl of Shao heard lawsuits under the sweet-pear tree, fearing to hinder the work of farming. Now when the timely rain pours its moisture, the seed hangs ready and cannot be sown; the autumn grains fall in the fields and cannot be gathered. The fields are red-earth empty, yet the stopping-places become markets; after the spring begins, they hang the green banner and whip the clay ox — this is hardly the will of a bright ruler encouraging plowing and sowing, nor what the spring ordinance means."


* * *



Chapter 36 — Flood and Drought （水旱）

The Lord Grand Secretary said: "Yu and Tang were sage rulers; Hou Ji and Yi Yin were worthy ministers; yet there were disasters of flood and drought. Flood and drought are what Heaven does; hunger and abundance are the working of yin and yang — not the labor of men. Therefore in the reckoning of the Grand Year, in yang is drought, in yin is water. In six years one hunger; in twelve years one famine. The way of Heaven is so — hardly the officers' crime alone."

The Worthies said: "In ancient times, when the government had virtue, yin and yang were harmonious, the stars and seasons were in order, wind and rain were timely. So conduct cultivated within made its sound heard without; doing good below made fortune answer in Heaven. When the Duke of Zhou kept the record, the world was at great peace; the state had no untimely wound; the years had no barren harvests. At that time, the wind did not break a clod nor sing on the twig; once in ten days it rained, and always by night. On hills high and low, all ripened. The Odes say: 'Clouds gather thick, and the rain comes in showers.' Now men do not examine the reason for it, but say 'the working of yin and yang' — not what I have heard. Mencius said: 'In the fields there are starved corpses, and men do not know to gather them; dogs and pigs eat men's food, and men do not know to check it. Being a father and mother to the people, when the people hunger and die, to say "It is not I; it is the year" — how is that different from killing a man with a blade and saying "It is not I; it is the weapon"?' The task of the present day lies in removing the trouble of hunger and cold: do away with salt and iron, retire power and profit, divide the land, hasten to the root trades, nourish mulberry and hemp, exhaust the strength of the land. With less work and restrained expense, the people will be rich of themselves. If it is so, then flood and drought cannot trouble, and barren years cannot weary."

The Lord Grand Secretary said: "The debaters prize words that are few and a point that is clear, that may reach the hearing of the many, not reaching ornate text and thick language; much talk harms the officers' plan for transforming the customs, and is family talk. Tao Zhu made his living with root and branch on different paths — a household with several trades, and the way of making a living was complete. Now the offices cast farm tools, making the people attend to the root and not run after the branch; then there is no weariness of hunger and cold. Salt and iron: what harm are they that they should be done away with?"

The Worthies said: "Farming is the world's great work; iron tools are the people's great use. If tools are handy, labor is little and work much, and the farmer delights in his task and is encouraged in his work. If the tools are not complete, the fields are waste and the grain does not grow; labor is small and work halved of itself. Handy and unhandy tools differ tenfold in their work. The offices smelt and cast iron tools; for the most part they make large tools, attending to meeting the quota, unfit for the people's use. The people's tools are blunt and worn, cutting grass without pain; so the farmer works hard and gains little, and the people are bitter about it."

The Lord Grand Secretary said: "Convict laborers and artisans, working daily for the offices on public business, have abundant expense and complete tools. Household workshops, straitened for days and diligent in use, do not melt or refine the iron, and hard and soft do not blend. So the officers ask to hold salt and iron together, unify their use and level their price, to make it convenient for the people, public and private. Even the governance of Yu and Xia could not change this. If the officials make the teaching bright and the workmen perfect their work, then hard and soft blend and the tools are handy. Then what bitterness have the people? What sickness has the farmer?"

The Worthies said: "Convict laborers and artisans! In the former days, when the people could rent, smelt, cast, and boil salt, salt was the same price as the five grains, and the tools were harmonious in profit and fit for use. Now the offices make iron tools, mostly bitter and bad; expense is not saved; the convict labor is troublesome and its work not exhausted. Households combining as one, father and sons striving together, each attending to making good tools; bad tools do not gather. When farm work is urgent, they hurry transport along the paths between fields. The people trade with one another, getting goods with wealth, produce, and new coins; sometimes they lend to the people and do not discard their work. Each sets farm tools and gets what he wants. Corvée remitted and restrained, the offices employ labor again to repair roads and bridges and the various levies, and the people find it convenient. Now gathering the source and unifying the price, the tools are mostly hard; good and bad cannot be chosen. The clerks are often absent; the tools are hard to get. Households cannot store much; storing much, the tools rust. They lose the fat season's days, going far to market for farm tools. Salt and iron are dear in price; the people are inconvenienced. Poor people sometimes plow with wood and hoe by hand, eating coarse and light. The iron offices sell tools that do not sell, or levy uneven assessments on the people. The convict labor does not meet the measure; in season they are ordered to help. Levies are sent without limit; corvée is used to multiply severity; so the people are sick and bitter. In ancient times, in a settlement of a thousand households and a house of a hundred teams, pottery, smelting, crafts, and commerce — the four people's needs — were enough to exchange among themselves. So the farmer did not leave his field-rows and had enough farm tools; the workman did not cut and fell and had enough timber; the potter and smelter did not plow fields and had enough grain; the people each got their convenience, and above them there was no business. Therefore the king attended to the root and did not work the branch, removed showy glitter, discarded carving, steeped the people in rite, and showed the people plainness. Therefore the people attended to the root and did not run after the branch."
