## DEDICATION

**To the memory of John Florio (1553–1625), presiding spirit of this edition.**

Florio was an Italian-born Englishman: lexicographer, maker of dictionaries (*A Worlde of Wordes*, 1598; *Queen Anna's New World of Words*, 1611), and Italian tutor in the households of the English court. In 1603 he published the first English translation of Montaigne's *Essays* — and that translation reached Shakespeare. The proof is on stage: Gonzalo's daydream of an ideal commonwealth in *The Tempest* (Act II) is built, phrase by phrase, out of Florio's rendering of Montaigne's "Of Cannibals" — a borrowing scholars have traced since the editor Edward Capell first flagged it. A translator's English, poured into the ear of the greatest writer in the language, and coming back out as poetry.

Florio is remembered now mostly in footnotes. The Boneyard rule is that the scholar gets named up front and the human story gets told. So this pamphlet — though its working base is a later Englishing — stands under Florio's name: the man who taught England how Montaigne sounds.

# That to philosophize is to learn how to die

## Michel de Montaigne, *Essays*, Book I

Cicero says that studying philosophy is nothing but preparing yourself to die. The reason is that study and reflection pull the soul partway out of the body and set it working on its own — a kind of apprenticeship in death. Or else it is because all the wisdom and reasoning in the world come down in the end to this one point: teaching us not to fear death.

And to tell the truth, either our reason is mocking us, or it has no other goal but our contentment. It should aim at nothing, in the end, but making us live well — and, as Scripture says, at ease. Every school of thought agrees on this: pleasure is our goal, though we take different roads to reach it. Otherwise their teachings would be rejected at first hearing — for who would listen to a man proposing suffering and misery as his goal? The quarrels between the philosophical schools on this point are mere quarrels over words. "Let us skip over those clever trifles" (*Transcurramus solertissimas nugas* — Seneca). There is more stubbornness and contradiction in them than suits so sacred a profession. But whatever role a man takes up, he always mixes something of himself into it.

Let the philosophers say what they like: the thing we all aim at, even in virtue, is pleasure. I enjoy rattling this word in their ears — the word they find so distasteful. And if it means some supreme pleasure and contentment, then it belongs to virtue's assistance more than to any other. This pleasure, for being gayer, stronger, sturdier, and manlier, is only the more seriously delightful. We ought to give the name of pleasure to that which is more gracious, gentle, and natural — not to its cheap rival. The other, lesser pleasure, if it deserves the fine name at all, deserves it only as a competitor, not as the rightful owner. I find it less free of troubles and setbacks than virtue itself. Its enjoyment is momentary, fleeting, fragile — and it has its sleepless nights, its fasts and labors, its sweat and blood. On top of that, it brings its own crowd of sharp, wounding passions, and a dull weariness afterward, that make it equal to the harshest penance.

We are wrong if we think these hardships serve as a spur and seasoning to its sweetness (as in nature one opposite is sharpened by another) — or if we say, when we turn to virtue, that similar hardships and difficulties weigh it down and make it harsh and unreachable. It is just the opposite: far more than in cheap pleasure, they ennoble, sharpen, and heighten the perfect and divine pleasure virtue gives us. Anyone who tries to weigh its cost against its reward makes himself unworthy of it — he neither understands the blessing nor knows how to use it. Those who preach to us that the search for it is rocky, difficult, and painful, but the enjoyment pleasant — what do they mean but to tell us it is always unpleasant? For what human effort will ever fully reach it? The most perfect have had to settle for reaching toward it, approaching it, without ever possessing it. But they are mistaken: of all the pleasures we know, the very pursuit is pleasant. The striving always carries the flavor of the thing it aims at, for it is a good part of the effect, of the same substance as it. The happiness and blessedness that shine in virtue light up everything around it and everything leading to it, right down to the first step and the farthest edge.

Now, of all the gifts virtue gives us, contempt of death is one of the greatest. It is what fits human life with a soft and easy calm, and gives us a pure and pleasant taste of living — without which every other pleasure would die. That is why all the schools' rules center and agree on this one point. And although they all likewise, with one voice, teach us to despise pain, poverty, and the other accidents of human life, they do not press it with the same urgency. For one thing, these accidents are not so unavoidable — most people go through life never knowing poverty, and some never knowing grief or sickness, like Xenophilus the musician, who lived a hundred and six years in perfect, unbroken health. For another, at the worst, death can cut short and end all our other troubles whenever we choose. But death itself is unavoidable:

"We are all driven the same road; sooner or later the lot of all must come out of the urn, and set us sailing into eternal exile." (*Omnes eodem cogimur...* — Horace)

And so, if death frightens us, it is a torment with no relief, and there is no way it cannot reach us. We may turn our heads this way and that, like travelers in dangerous country: "Ever, like the stone of Tantalus, it hangs over us" (*Quae, quasi saxum Tantalo, semper impendet* — Cicero).

Our courts often send condemned criminals to be executed at the place where the crime was committed. But carry them through fine houses on the way; prepare the best entertainment you can: "Sicilian feasts will not tickle their palates, nor will the song of birds and harps bring back sleep" (*Non Siculae dapes / Dulcem elaborabunt saporem...* — Horace). Do you think they can enjoy it — that with the fatal end of their journey always before their eyes, their taste would not be spoiled for such luxuries? "He studies the road, counts the days, measures his life by the length of the journey, and torments himself with the coming blow" (*Audit iter, numeratque dies...* — Claudian).

The end of our race is death. It is the necessary goal we aim at — and if it frightens us, how can we take a step without shaking? The remedy ordinary people use is not to think about it. But what brutish stupidity gives them such gross blindness? They bridle the donkey by the tail: "Who in his folly seeks to walk backward" (*Qui capite ipse suo instituit vestigia retro* — Lucretius). No wonder he often lands in the pit. People frighten each other with the very mention of death, and many cross themselves as if it were the devil's name. And because making a will has to do with dying, no one can be persuaded to pick up a pen for the purpose until the doctor has condemned him and given him up entirely — and then, between fear and terror, God knows what state his understanding is in to do it.

The Romans, finding the poor syllable "death" so harsh to their ears and so ominous, found a way to soften it and spin it out with a roundabout phrase. Instead of saying "such a one is dead," they said "such a one has lived" or "such a one has ceased to live" (Plutarch, *Life of Cicero*). If there was any mention of life in it, even life past, it carried some sound of comfort. From them we borrowed our phrase "the late Mr. So-and-so" (the French *feu Monsieur un tel*). Perhaps, as the saying goes, the phrase "we have lived" is worth what it costs.

I was born between eleven and twelve in the morning on the last day of February 1533, by our reckoning, which begins the year on the first of January. It is now just fifteen days since I turned thirty-nine. I count on living at least as many years more. Meanwhile, troubling yourself over something so far off is folly. But what then? Young and old die on the same terms. No one leaves life any differently than if he had just entered it. There is no man so old and worn out who, having heard of Methuselah, does not think he still has twenty good years ahead. You fool — who promised you your term of life? You trust what the doctors say. Consult facts and experience instead. By the ordinary course of things, you have long been living on borrowed time; you have already outlived the usual span. And to see that it is so, count your acquaintances: how many more died before reaching your age than ever reached it. And among those who made their lives famous, take the tally — I dare bet you will find more who died before thirty-five than after. It is reasonable, and pious too, to take example from the humanity of Jesus Christ himself: he ended his life at thirty-three. The greatest man who was no more than a man, Alexander, died at the same age. How many ways death has to surprise us! "Be as careful as he may, no man can ever guard against the danger that may strike at any hour" (*Quid quisque vitet, nunquam homini satis / Cautum est in horas* — Horace).

To skip fevers and pleurisies — who would have imagined that a Duke of Brittany would be crushed to death in a crowd, as that duke was at the entry of Pope Clement, my neighbor, into Lyons? Have you not seen one of our kings killed at a tournament, and did not one of his ancestors die from being jostled by a hog? Aeschylus, warned of a falling house, took good care to avoid that danger — to no purpose, since he was struck on the head by a tortoise dropped from an eagle's talons in the air. Another choked on a grape pit. An emperor was killed by the scratch of a comb while combing his hair. Aemilius Lepidus died stumbling on his own threshold, and Aufidius from a jostle against the door as he entered the council chamber. And between the very thighs of women died Cornelius Gallus the proctor; Tigillinus, captain of the watch at Rome; Ludovico, son of Guido di Gonzaga, Marquis of Mantua; and — worse example — Speusippus, a Platonic philosopher, and one of our popes. The poor judge Bebius granted an eight-day adjournment in a case; but meanwhile death condemned him, and his own lease on life expired. While Caius Julius the physician was anointing a patient's eyes, death closed his own. And to bring in an example from my own blood: a brother of mine, Captain Saint-Martin, a young man of twenty-three who had already given good proof of his courage, was playing a game of tennis and took a blow from a ball a little above his right ear. It left no sign of wound or bruise, so he thought nothing of it — he did not even sit down to rest. Nevertheless, he died five or six hours later of a stroke brought on by that blow.

With such common examples passing before our eyes every day, how can anyone free himself from the thought of death, or avoid imagining it with its hand on his throat at every moment? What does it matter, you will say, which way it comes, so long as a man does not torment himself expecting it? For my part, that is my own view. If a man could avoid it by any means — even by crawling under a calf's skin — I am one who would not be ashamed of the trick. All I aim at is to pass my time at ease, and I take hold of whatever pastimes contribute most to that, however little glory or example there is in them: "I would rather seem mad and lazy, so long as my faults please me, or at least do not pain me, than be wise and fretting" (*Praetulerim... delirus inersque videri, / Dum mea delectent mala me, vel denique fallant, / Quam sapere, et ringi* — Horace).

But it is folly to think of doing anything that way. They go, they come, they gallop and dance, and not a word of death. All very fine — until it strikes them, their wives, their children, or their friends, catching them unaware and unprepared. Then what torment, what outcries, what madness and despair! Did you ever see anything so crushed, so changed, so undone? A man must make provision for it earlier. And this brutish negligence — if it could possibly lodge in the brain of a sensible man, which I think utterly impossible — sells its goods too dear. If death were an enemy that could be avoided, I would advise borrowing weapons even from cowardice itself. But it is not. It will catch you whether you flee and play the coward or stand your ground like an honest man: "He pursues the fleeing coward, and does not spare the hamstrings of the unwarlike youth who turns his back" (*Nempe et fugacem persequitur virum...* — Horace). And since no armor is proof against him — "Let him hide in fear beneath iron or bronze: death will still drag his head out of its helmet" (*Ille licet ferro cautus, se condat et aere, / Mors tamen inclusum protrahet inde caput* — Propertius) — let us learn to stand our ground bravely and fight him.

To begin stripping him of his greatest advantage over us, let us take a course clean contrary to the common one. Let us disarm him of his novelty and strangeness; let us talk with him, grow familiar with him, and keep nothing so often in our thoughts as death. On every occasion, picture him in every shape: at the stumbling of a horse, at the falling of a tile, at the least prick of a pin, let us at once consider and say to ourselves, "Well — and what if it had been death itself?" And with that, let us take courage and steady ourselves. Even in our feasting and merriment, let us always set the remembrance of our frail condition before our eyes, never letting ourselves be so carried away by pleasure that we do not pause to reflect on how many different ways this merriment of ours leads toward death, and with how many dangers it threatens it. The Egyptians did this: in the height of their feasting and mirth, they had a dried skeleton brought into the room, to remind the guests of themselves. "Believe each day that has dawned on you to be your last; the next day, unexpected, will be all the more welcome" (*Omnem crede diem tibi diluxisse supremum / Grata superveniet, quae non sperabitur, hora* — Horace).

We do not know where death waits for us. Let us expect him everywhere. The premeditation of death is the premeditation of liberty: he who has learned to die has unlearned how to be a slave. There is nothing evil in life for the man who rightly understands that losing life is no evil. Knowing how to die frees us from all subjection and constraint. When the wretched King of Macedon, his prisoner, sent begging Paulus Aemilius not to lead him in his triumph, Aemilius answered, "Let him make that request to himself" (Plutarch, *Life of Paulus Aemilius*; Cicero, *Tusculans*).

Truly, in all things, if nature does not lend a little help, art and effort can do little to any purpose. By my own nature I am not gloomy, but thoughtful — and there is nothing I have entertained myself with more constantly than imaginings of death, even in the most carefree time of my youth: "When my flowering age was enjoying its spring" (*Jucundum quum aetas florida ver ageret* — Catullus). In the company of ladies, at games, some may have thought me troubled by jealousy or some uncertain hope, while I was really entertaining myself with the memory of someone surprised a few days before by a burning fever, of which he died — coming home from an entertainment like this one, his head full of idle fancies of love and merriment, as mine was then — and that, for all I knew, the same fate was waiting for me. "Soon the present will be past, never to be called back" (*Jam fuerit, nec post unquam revocare licebit* — Lucretius).

Yet this thought wrinkled my brow no more than any other. We must feel a sting from such imaginings at first — it is impossible not to. But by turning them over and over in the mind, they at last become so familiar as to be no trouble at all. Otherwise I, for my part, would be in a perpetual fright and frenzy, for no man was ever so distrustful of his life, no man so unsure how long it would last. Neither health — which I have so far enjoyed strong and vigorous, and very seldom interrupted — lengthens my hopes, nor does sickness shorten them. Every minute, it seems to me, I am slipping away, and it is always in my mind that what can be done tomorrow can be done today. Dangers and hazards do little or nothing, in truth, to hasten our end. If we consider how many thousands more hang over our heads besides the accident that threatens us right now, we will find that the healthy and the sick, those at sea and those by the fire, those in battle and those sitting idle at home, are all equally near it. "No man is more fragile than another; no man more certain than another of tomorrow" (*Nemo altero fragilior est; nemo in crastinum sui certior* — Seneca).

For anything I have to do before I die, the longest free time would seem too short, even if it were only an hour's business.

A friend of mine, turning over my notebook the other day, found a memorandum of something I wanted done after my death. I told him — what was really true — that though I was only a league from my own house, and merry and well, yet when the thing came into my head I hurried to write it down there, because I was not certain of living till I got home. As a man forever brooding over his own thoughts and confining them to his own particular concerns, I am at every hour as well prepared as I am ever likely to be, and death, whenever he comes, can bring nothing I did not expect long before. We should always, as nearly as we can, be booted and spurred and ready to go — and above all, at that time, have business with no one but ourselves: "Why, in so short a life, do we aim at so many grand projects?" (*Quid brevi fortes jaculamur aevo / Multa?* — Horace). For we will find work enough to do there, without needing more.

One man complains, more than of death itself, that it robs him of a glorious victory. Another, that he must die before he has married his daughter or raised his children. A third seems troubled only that he must lose his wife's company; a fourth, his son's conversation, as the chief comfort of his life. For my part, I am, thank God, right now in such a condition that I am ready to move out whenever it pleases him, without regret for anything at all. I am disengaging myself entirely from worldly ties; my leave-taking is soon finished, of all but myself. No one ever prepared to say goodbye to the world more completely and without reserve, and to shake hands with every interest in it, than I expect to do. The deadest deaths are the best: "'Wretch that I am,' they cry, 'one fatal day has robbed me of all the prizes of life'" (*'Miser, O miser,' aiunt, 'omnia ademit / Una dies infesta mihi tot praemia vitae'* — Lucretius). And the builder: "The works stand unfinished, the tall towers of the walls unbuilt" (*Manet... opera interrupta, minaeque / Murorum ingentes* — Virgil).

A man should plan nothing that needs a long time to finish — or at least, not with such passionate desire to see it perfected. We are born for action: "When I die, let it be in the middle of the work I had planned" (*Quum moriar, medium solvar et inter opus* — Ovid). I want a man always doing, and, as far as he can, stretching out the duties of life — and then let death take me planting my cabbages, caring nothing for him, and even less for my garden not being finished. I saw a man die who, at his last gasp, complained of nothing so much as that fate was about to cut the thread of a chronicle he was writing, when he had gotten no further than the fifteenth or sixteenth of our kings. "They forget to add this: that in death, we no longer even want the things we wanted" (*Illud in his rebus non addunt: nec tibi earum / jam desiderium rerum super insidet una* — Lucretius).

We must rid ourselves of these common and harmful humors. That is why men first placed burial grounds next to churches, in the busiest parts of the city — to accustom, says Lycurgus, the common people, women, and children not to be startled at the sight of a corpse, and so that the constant sight of bones, graves, and funerals would remind us of our frail condition: "It was once the custom to liven banquets with bloodshed, and to mix with the feast the dreadful sight of men fighting with swords — the dying often falling across the very cups, the tables splashed with blood" (*Quin etiam exhilarare viris convivia caede / Mos olim...* — Silius Italicus). And as the Egyptians after their feasts had a great image of death carried in, with someone crying out, "Drink and be merry, for this is what you will be when you are dead" — so it is my custom to keep death not only in my imagination but constantly on my lips. There is nothing I am so curious about, nothing I so enjoy learning, as how men died — their words, their looks, their bearing. No passages in history hold my attention like these, and it is plain enough, from how I crowd in examples of this kind, that I have a particular liking for the subject. If I were a writer of books, I would put together a register, with notes, of the different deaths of men: whoever taught men to die would at the same time teach them to live. Dicarchus made such a book, and gave it that title — but it was aimed at another, less useful end.

Perhaps someone will object that the pain and terror of dying so far exceed anything we can imagine that the best-prepared fencer will be completely out of his game when it comes to the thrust. Let them say what they like: preparing the mind beforehand is surely a very great advantage. And besides, is it nothing to go that far at least, without panic or flinching? Moreover, nature herself helps and encourages us. If death is sudden and violent, we have no time to fear. If otherwise, I notice that as I sink further into an illness, I naturally slip into a certain weariness and distaste for life. I find I have much more trouble digesting this resolve to die when I am in good health than when I am languishing with a fever. The less I have to do with the comforts of life — as I begin to lose the use and pleasure of them — the less terror death holds for me. Which makes me hope that the further I move from the one and the nearer I approach the other, the more easily I will trade the one for the other. And as I have found in other matters that, as Caesar says, things often look bigger at a distance than up close, I have found that in health I have dreaded illnesses far more than when I was actually suffering them. The strength I have now, the cheerfulness and delight I live in, make the opposite state look so out of proportion to my present condition that in imagination I magnify those hardships by half, and expect them to be much worse than I actually find them when they weigh heaviest on me. I hope to find death the same.

Just observe the ordinary changes and declines we suffer daily, and see how nature robs us of the awareness of our own bodily decay. What remains to an old man of the vigor of his youth and better days? "Alas, what portion of life remains to the old!" (*Heu! senibus vitae portio quanta manet* — Maximian). Caesar, when an old weather-beaten soldier of his guard came asking leave to kill himself, looked at his withered body and shuffling walk and answered pleasantly, "So you fancy you are still alive" (Seneca). If a man fell into that condition all at once, I do not think human nature could bear such a change. But nature leads us by the hand at an easy, almost imperceptible pace, step by step, down to that wretched state — and by that means makes it familiar, so that we do not feel the blow when our youth dies in us, though it is really a harder death than the final dissolving of a worn-out body, than the death of old age. For the fall is not so great from an uneasy existence to nothing at all as it is from a lively and flourishing existence to one that is troublesome and painful. The body, bent and bowed, has less strength to carry a burden — and it is the same with the soul. That is why we must hold her up, firm and upright, against the power of this enemy. For as she can never rest while she fears it, so if she can once make herself sure, she may boast — a thing almost beyond human condition — that no unease, anxiety, fear, or any other disturbance can live or find a place in her: "Not the threatening frown of a tyrant shakes her well-grounded soul, nor the wild south wind, lord of the stormy Adriatic, nor the great hand of thundering Jove" (*Non vultus instantis tyranni / Mente quatit solida, neque Auster / Dux inquieti turbidus Adriae, / Nec fulminantis magna Jovis manus* — Horace).

Then she has become sovereign over all her lusts and passions, mistress of necessity, shame, poverty, and all the other blows of fortune. Let us then, as many of us as can, win this advantage. It is the true and sovereign liberty on this earth: it arms us to defy violence and injustice, and to despise prisons and chains: "I will keep you in handcuffs and fetters, under a cruel guard. — I myself, a god, as soon as I wish, will set me free. This god, I think, is death; death is the final boundary of all things" (*In manicis et / Compedibus saevo te sub custode tenebo. / Ipse Deus, simul atque volam, me solvet. Opinor, / Hoc sentit; moriar; mors ultima linea rerum est* — Horace).

Our very religion has no surer human foundation than contempt of life. Not only does reason argue us into it — for why fear losing a thing which, once lost, cannot be mourned? — but also: since we are threatened by so many kinds of death, is it not infinitely worse to fear them all forever than to undergo one of them once? And what does it matter when it happens, since it is inevitable? When someone told Socrates, "The Thirty Tyrants have sentenced you to death," he answered, "And nature has sentenced them" (Diogenes Laertius — though in fact Socrates was condemned not by the Thirty but by the Athenians). What a ridiculous thing, to trouble ourselves over taking the one step that frees us from all trouble! As our birth brought us the birth of all things, so our death includes the death of all things. To grieve that we will not be alive a hundred years from now is the same folly as grieving that we were not alive a hundred years ago. Death is the beginning of another life. We wept just as much, and it cost us just as much, to enter into this one — and we shed our earlier veil just the same on entering it. Nothing that happens only once can be a grievance. Is it reasonable to fear so long a thing that will be over so soon? Long life and short are made all one by death, for there is no long or short in things that are no more. Aristotle tells us of certain little creatures on the banks of the river Hypanis that live no more than a day: those that die at eight in the morning die in their youth, and those that die at five in the evening die in old age. Who would not laugh to see this moment of life weighed as happiness or misery? The longest and the shortest of our lives, set against eternity — or even against the lifespan of mountains, rivers, stars, trees, and some animals — is no less ridiculous (Seneca, *Consolation to Marcia*).

But nature compels us to it. "Go out of this world," she says, "as you came into it. The same passage you made from death to life, without passion or fear — make it again, in the same way, from life to death. Your death is part of the order of the universe; it is part of the life of the world. Mortals live among themselves by turns, and, like runners in the games, hand the torch of life to the next" (*Inter se mortales mutua vivunt... Et, quasi cursores, vitai lampada tradunt* — Lucretius).

"Would you have me change for you this beautiful order of things? It is the condition of your creation. Death is part of you — and while you try to escape it, you escape yourselves. This very being you now enjoy is divided equally between life and death. The day of your birth is one day's march toward the grave: 'The first hour that gave us life also took an hour away' (*Prima, quae vitam dedit, hora carpsit* — Seneca); 'As we are born we die, and the end hangs on the beginning' (*Nascentes morimur, finisque ab origine pendet* — Manilius).

"All the time you live, you steal from life, and live at life's expense. The perpetual work of your life is only laying the foundation of death. You are in death while you are in life — because you will still exist after death, when you are no longer alive. Or, if you prefer it put this way: you are dead after life, but dying all the while you live. And death handles the dying far more roughly than the dead — more sharply, more truly. If you have made good use of life, you have had enough of it; go your way satisfied. 'Why not leave life like a guest satisfied with the feast?' (*Cur non ut plenus vitae conviva recedis?* — Lucretius).

"If you have not known how to make good use of it — if it was wasted on you — why care about losing it? Why want to keep it longer? 'Why seek to add more life, only to waste it badly again, and die ungrateful for it all?' (*Cur amplius addere quaeris, / Rursum quod pereat male, et ingratum occidat omne?* — Lucretius).

"Life in itself is neither good nor evil; it is the stage of good or evil, as you make it. And if you have lived a day, you have seen it all: one day is equal and like every other day. There is no other light, no other dark; this same sun, this moon, these same stars, this same order of things, is what your ancestors enjoyed, and what your descendants will enjoy: 'Your fathers saw no different world, and your grandchildren will see no different one' (*Non alium videre patres, aliumve nepotes / Aspicient* — Manilius).

"And come the worst that can come — the whole cast and variety of my comedy is performed in a year. If you have watched the turning of my four seasons, they contain the infancy, the youth, the manhood, and the old age of the world. The year has played its part, and knows no art but to begin again; it will always be the same thing: 'We turn in the same circle, forever confined within it' (*Versamur ibidem, atque insumus usque* — Lucretius); 'And the year rolls round on its own footsteps' (*Atque in se sua per vestigia volvitur annus* — Virgil).

"I am not going to invent new amusements for you: 'There is nothing else I can devise or find to please you; it is the same thing over and over' (*Nam tibi praeterea quod machiner, inveniamque / Quod placeat, nihil est; eadem sunt omnia semper* — Lucretius).

"Make room for others, as others made room for you. Equality is the soul of fairness. Who can complain of sharing the fate that includes everyone? Besides, live as long as you can — you will not shorten by one bit the time you will be dead. It is all pointless: you will lie just as long in the state you fear so much as if you had died as a baby: 'Live on, triumphing over as many ages as you will — death will still remain eternal' (*Licet quot vis vivendo vincere secla, / Mors aeterna tamen nihilominus illa manebit* — Lucretius).

"And yet I will put you in a condition you will have no reason to complain of. 'You do not know that in real death there will be no second self left alive to mourn you dead, standing over you where you lie' (*In vera nescis nullum fore morte alium te, / Qui possit vivus tibi te lugere peremptum, / Stansque jacentem* — Lucretius).

"Nor will you even wish for the life you are so anxious about: 'For then no one misses himself or his life... and no longing for ourselves touches us' (*Nec sibi enim quisquam tum se vitamque requirit... Nec desiderium nostri nos afficit ullum* — Lucretius).

"Death is less to be feared than nothing — if anything could be less than nothing: 'We must consider death to be much less a thing to us, if there can be anything less than what we see to be nothing' (*Multo mortem minus ad nos esse putandum, / Si minus esse potest, quam quod nihil esse videmus* — Lucretius).

"It cannot concern you in any way whether you are living or dead: living, because you are still in being; dead, because you are no more. Moreover, no one dies before his hour. The time you leave behind was no more yours than the time that passed before you came into the world — and it concerns you no more. 'Consider how the ages of eternity that passed before us are nothing to us' (*Respice enim, quam nil ad nos anteacta vetustas / Temporis aeterni fuerit* — Lucretius)."

Wherever your life ends, it is all there. The value of living lies not in the length of days but in the use of time. A man may have lived long and yet lived very little. Use the time while you have it. It depends on your will, not on the number of your days, to have lived long enough. Can you really imagine never arriving at the place you are always traveling toward? And yet no journey lacks its end. And if company makes it pleasanter or easier for you — does not the whole world walk the same road? "All things, when your life is over, will follow you" (*Omnia te, vita perfuncta, sequentur* — Lucretius).

Does not the whole world dance the same round as you? Is there anything that does not grow old along with you? A thousand men, a thousand animals, a thousand other creatures die in the same moment that you die: "No night has followed day, no day has followed night, in which sobs and grieving cries were not heard — the companions of death and dark funerals" (*Nam nox nulla diem, neque noctem aurora sequuta est, / Quae non audierit mistos vagitibus aegris / Ploratus, mortis comites et funeris atri* — Lucretius).

"Why try to pull back, if there is no escaping it? You have seen enough examples of people glad to die, as freed from heavy miseries — but have you ever found anyone dissatisfied with dying? It must be utter folly to condemn a thing you have never tried yourself, nor seen tried by anyone else. Why complain of me and of fate? Have we wronged you? Is it for you to govern us, or us to govern you? Though your full age may not be reached, your life is complete: a short man is as much a man as a giant, and neither men nor their lives are measured by the yardstick. Chiron refused immortality when he learned the terms on which he was to have it, from the god of time and duration himself, his father Saturn. Just think seriously how much more unbearable and painful an immortal life would be for man than the one I have given him. If you did not have death, you would curse me forever for depriving you of it. I mixed a little bitterness into it so that, seeing how useful it is, you would not too greedily and recklessly seek and embrace it. And to settle you in this moderation — neither sick of life nor hating death, which I have appointed you to meet once — I blended the two, pleasure and pain. It was I who taught Thales, the greatest of your sages, that living and dying are indifferent — which made him very wisely answer, when asked why he did not die then, 'Because it is indifferent' (Diogenes Laertius).

"Water, earth, air, fire, and the other parts of this creation of mine are no more the instruments of your life than they are of your death. Why fear your last day? It does no more to destroy you than any of the rest. The last step does not cause weariness — it only arrives. Every day travels toward death; the last one only reaches it."

These are the good lessons our mother Nature teaches.

I have often wondered why it is that in war the image of death — whether we see it in ourselves or in others — seems, beyond comparison, less dreadful than at home in our own houses. (If it were not so, an army would be full of doctors and whimpering weaklings.) Death being everywhere the same, why should there be so much more steadiness in peasants and the lower sort of people than in those of better rank? I believe, truly, that it is those terrible ceremonies and preparations we dress it in that frighten us more than the thing itself: a whole new, contrary way of living; the cries of mothers, wives, and children; the visits of stunned and grieving friends; the attendance of pale, blubbering servants; a dark room ringed with burning candles; our bed surrounded by physicians and priests; in short, nothing but gloom and horror all around us. We seem dead and buried already. Children are afraid even of people they know well, when disguised in a mask — and so it is with us. The mask must be taken off things as well as people, and once it is removed we will find underneath nothing but the very same death that a common servant or a poor chambermaid died a day or two ago, without any fear at all. Happy is the death that leaves us no time for preparing such ceremonies.

## OF EXPERIENCE

There is no desire more natural than the desire for knowledge. We try every
road that can lead us to it; where reason fails us, we use experience:

> "Per varios usus artem experientia fecit,
> Exemplo monstrante viam,"

["By various trials experience created art, example showing the
way."—Manilius, i. 59.]

which is a much weaker and cheaper tool; but truth is so great a thing
that we should not turn up our nose at any path that can guide us to it.
Reason comes in so many shapes that we don't know which one to take hold
of; experience has no fewer. The conclusions we try to draw from comparing
events are shaky, because events are never alike. There is no quality so
universal in this picture of things as diversity and variety. Greeks,
Latins, and we ourselves all use eggs as our favorite example of things
being alike; and yet there have been men—one at Delphi in particular—who
could tell eggs apart so well by their markings that he never mistook one
for another, and with many hens to choose from, could say which hen had
laid which egg.

Unlikeness forces its way into our works all by itself; no craft can
achieve perfect likeness. Neither Perrozet nor any other card-maker can
polish and whiten the backs of his playing cards so carefully that some
gamblers won't tell them apart just from watching someone else shuffle
them. Likeness doesn't so much make things one as unlikeness makes them
different. Nature has bound herself to make nothing that wasn't unlike
something else.

And yet I'm not much taken with the idea of the man who thought he could
rein in the power of judges by piling up laws and marking out each judge's
share for him. He didn't realize there is as much freedom and elbow room
in interpreting laws as in making them; and they only fool themselves who
think to cut short our disputes by calling us back to the plain words of
the Bible—as if our minds found any less room to argue over somebody
else's meaning than to deliver our own, and as if there were less heat and
bitterness in commentary than in invention. We can see how wrong he was,
for we have more laws in France than all the rest of the world put
together, more than would be needed to govern all of Epicurus's many
worlds:

> "Ut olim flagitiis, sic nunc legibus, laboramus."

["As we were once weighed down by crimes, so now we are weighed down by
laws."—Tacitus, Annals, iii. 25.]

And yet we have left so much to the opinions and decisions of our judges
that there never was fuller freedom or fuller license. What have our
lawmakers gained by picking out a hundred thousand particular cases and
applying a hundred thousand laws to them? That number bears no proportion
at all to the infinite variety of human actions. The multiplying of our
inventions will never catch up with the variety of examples; add a hundred
times as many more, and it still won't happen that, among future events,
one will be found that matches another one, chosen and recorded out of
that vast number of millions of events, so closely and exactly paired and
fitted to it that there won't remain some circumstance, some difference,
calling for a different judgment. There is little relation between our
actions, which are in perpetual change, and fixed, unchangeable laws. The
laws most to be wished for are the rarest, simplest, and most general; and
I'm even of the opinion that we'd be better off with none at all than with
the monstrous number we have.

Nature always gives us better and happier laws than the ones we make for
ourselves. Look at the poets' picture of the Golden Age, and at the
nations we see living with no laws but nature's. Some of them take for
their only judge the first traveler who comes along their mountain roads
to settle their disputes; others, on market day, pick someone out from
among themselves on the spot to decide their quarrels. What danger would
there be if the wisest among us settled ours the same way—according to
what comes up, on the spot, without being tied to precedent and
consequence? Every foot its own shoe. When King Ferdinand sent colonists
to the Indies, he wisely provided that they should take no law students
with them, for fear lawsuits would get a footing in that new world—law
being a science that by its very nature breeds quarrels and division. He
judged with Plato "that lawyers and doctors are bad institutions for a
country."

How does it happen that our ordinary language, so easy for every other
use, becomes obscure and unintelligible in wills and contracts? How does
it happen that a man who expresses himself so clearly in whatever else he
says or writes can find no way to state himself in these that doesn't fall
into doubt and contradiction—unless it's because the masters of that art,
giving their special attention to picking out impressive-sounding words
and building artificial sentences, have weighed every syllable so
carefully and sifted every kind of tricky connection so thoroughly that
they are now lost and tangled in an infinity of figures and tiny
divisions, and can no longer be brought under any rule or formula, or any
certain meaning:

> "Confusum est, quidquid usque in pulverem sectum est."

["Whatever is ground to powder becomes confused and
indistinguishable."—Seneca, Epistle 89.]

You've seen children trying to divide a blob of quicksilver into a set
number of pieces: the more they press and work it and try to force it to
their will, the more they stir up the freedom of that lively metal; it
slips away from their efforts and scatters into so many separate drops
that all counting is defeated. It's the same here: by splitting these fine
distinctions ever finer, we teach men to multiply their doubts; we put
them on the road to stretching out and diversifying difficulties,
lengthening them and scattering them. By sowing and peddling questions
they make the world fruitful and rich in uncertainties and disputes, the
way the earth is made fertile by being broken up and dug deep.

> "Difficultatem facit doctrina."

["Learning breeds difficulty."—Quintilian, Institutes of Oratory,
x. 3.]

We had our doubts about Ulpian, and now we're even more tangled up with
Bartolus and Baldus. We ought to wipe out the traces of this countless
variety of opinions, not dress ourselves up in it and bequeath a load of
whimsies to those who come after. I don't know what to say about it; but
experience makes it plain that so many interpretations scatter truth and
break it to pieces. Aristotle wrote to be understood; if he couldn't
manage it, much less will some lesser man who isn't as good at it, and
still less a third man trying to express the second man's thoughts. We
open the matter up and spill it in the pouring out; out of one subject we
make a thousand, and in multiplying and subdividing them we fall back into
the infinity of Epicurus's atoms. No two men ever judged the same thing
alike; and it's impossible to find two opinions exactly alike—not only in
different men, but in the same man at different times. I often find matter
for doubt in things the commentators haven't bothered to notice; I'm most
likely to stumble on flat ground, like some horses I've known that trip
most on the smoothest road.

Who won't admit that glosses increase doubts and ignorance, when there's
no book to be found, human or divine, that the world busies itself over,
whose difficulties are cleared up by interpretation? The hundredth
commentator hands it on to the next, knottier and more tangled than he
found it. When did we ever agree among ourselves: "This book is done with;
there's nothing more to be said about it"? It's most obvious in the law:
we give the authority of law to countless learned doctors, countless
decrees, and as many interpretations; and yet do we ever reach the end of
needing interpretation? For all that, is there any progress or advance
toward peace, or do we need any fewer lawyers and judges than when this
great mass of law was still in its infancy? On the contrary, we darken and
bury understanding; we can no longer find it except at the mercy of so
many fences and barriers. Men don't know the natural sickness of the mind:
it does nothing but ferret and pry, and goes round and round forever,
juggling and tangling itself like a silkworm, and then smothers itself in
its own work—"Mus in pice" ["A mouse in a pitch barrel"]. It thinks it
sees, far off, some glimmer of light, some imaginary truth; but as it runs
toward it, so many difficulties, obstacles, and fresh inquiries cross its
path that it loses its way and gets drunk on the motion—much like Aesop's
dogs, who, seeing something like a dead body floating in the sea and not
being able to reach it, set to work drinking the water to lay the channel
dry, and choked themselves on it. To which what a certain Crates said
about the writings of Heraclitus fits well enough: "that they needed a
reader who could swim well," so that the depth and weight of his learning
wouldn't drag him under and drown him. It's nothing but our own weakness
that makes us content with what others or we ourselves have found in this
hunt for knowledge: a better mind won't rest so easily satisfied; there is
always room for someone to follow—even for ourselves—and another road to
take; there is no end to our inquiries; our end is in the other world.
It's a sign the mind has either grown shortsighted, when it's satisfied, or
grown tired. No generous mind can stop at itself; it will always reach
farther, beyond its strength; it makes sallies beyond what it can do; if
it doesn't push forward and press on, pull back, rush and wheel about,
it's only half alive; its pursuits are without bound or method; its food
is wonder, the chase, ambiguity—which Apollo made clear enough by always
speaking to us in double, obscure, sideways meanings: not feeding us, but
amusing and puzzling us. It's an irregular, perpetual motion, with no
pattern and no goal; its inventions heat up, chase, and breed one
another:

Estienne de la Boetie; thus rendered by Cotton:

> "So in a running stream one wave we see
> After another roll incessantly,
> And as they glide, each does successively
> Pursue the other, each the other fly;
> By this that's evermore pushed on, and this
> By that continually preceded is:
> The water still does into water swill,
> Still the same brook, but different water still."

There is more work in interpreting interpretations than in interpreting
things, and more books about books than about any other subject; we do
nothing but comment on one another. Every place swarms with commentaries;
authors are in short supply. Isn't the chief and most honored knowledge of
our later age the understanding of the learned? Isn't that the common,
final end of all study? Our opinions are grafted onto one another: the
first serves as the stock for the second, the second for the third, and so
on; step by step we climb the ladder. Which is why the man perched highest
often has more honor than merit, for he's gotten up only an inch on the
shoulders of the man just below him.

How often—and maybe how foolishly—have I stretched my book out to make it
talk about itself; foolishly, if for no other reason than this: it should
remind me of what I say about others who do the same, that the frequent
loving glances they cast at their work show their hearts panting with
self-love, and that even the scornful severity with which they lash their
work is just the dandling and caressing of a mother's love—like
Aristotle, whose high opinion and low opinion of himself often spring from
the same air of arrogance. My excuse is that I ought to have more freedom
than others in this, since I write expressly about myself and my writings
as I do about my other actions; my subject turns back on itself. But I
don't know whether others will accept this excuse.

I noticed in Germany that Luther has left behind as many divisions and
disputes over doubts about his opinions—more, even—than he himself raised
over the Holy Scriptures. Our quarrel is about words. I ask what nature
is, what pleasure is, what a circle is, what substitution is; the question
is about words, and it's answered with words. A stone is a body; but if a
man presses further—"And what is a body?" "Substance." "And what is
substance?" and so on—he'll drive the man answering to the end of his
Calepin.

> [Calepin (Ambrogio da Calepio), a famous fifteenth-century
> lexicographer. His polyglot dictionary became so famous that "Calepin"
> became the common word for any dictionary.]

We trade one word for another, and often for one less understood. I know
better what a man is than I know what "animal" is, or "mortal," or
"rational." To settle one doubt they give me three; it's the Hydra's head.
Socrates asked Meno what virtue was. "There is," says Meno, "the virtue of
a man and of a woman, of a magistrate and of a private person, of an old
man and of a child." "Very fine," cried Socrates; "we were looking for one
virtue, and you've brought us a whole swarm." We ask one question, and
they hand us back a whole hive. Just as no event and no face entirely
resembles another, so no two entirely differ: nature mixes things
cleverly. If our faces weren't alike, we couldn't tell man from beast; if
they weren't unlike, we couldn't tell one man from another. All things
hold together by some likeness; every example limps, and the conclusions
drawn from experience are always faulty and imperfect. Comparisons are
always hitched at one end or the other: that's how laws serve, fitted to
each of our cases by some twisted, biased, forced interpretation.

Since the laws of conduct—which concern each man's particular duty to
himself—are so hard to frame, as we see they are, it's no wonder the ones
that govern so many particular men are much harder. Just look at the shape
of this justice that governs us; it's a true witness to human weakness, so
full is it of error and contradiction. What we call mercy and severity in
justice—and we find so much of both that I don't know whether the middle
course is found as often—are sickly, unjust members of the very body and
essence of justice. Some country people just brought me news in a hurry:
they'd left in one of my woods a man with a hundred wounds on him, still
breathing, who begged them for pity's sake for water and to be carried to
somewhere he could be helped. They tell me they didn't dare go near him,
but ran off, for fear the officers of justice would catch them there and—as
happens to those found near a murdered man—they'd be questioned about the
matter to their utter ruin, having neither money nor friends to defend
their innocence. What could I have said to these people? It's certain that
this act of humanity would have landed them in trouble.

How many innocent people have we known punished—and that through no fault
of the judge—and how many more that never came to our knowledge? This
happened in my time: certain men were condemned to die for a murder; their
sentence, if not yet pronounced, was at least settled and decided on. Just
in time, the judges are told by the officers of a lower court nearby that
they have some men in custody who have directly confessed the murder and
given an unquestionable account of every detail of it. Yet it was gravely
debated whether or not to suspend the execution of the sentence already
passed on the first accused men. They weighed the novelty of the example
from the legal point of view, and the consequence of overturning
judgments—that the sentence had been passed, and the judges could no
longer go back on it. And in the end, those poor devils were sacrificed to
the forms of justice. Philip—or some other king—guarded against such a
mishap in this way: he had condemned a man to pay a heavy fine to another
by an absolute judgment. The truth coming out some time later, he found he
had passed an unjust sentence. On one side stood the right of the case; on
the other, the right of legal forms. He satisfied both as best he could,
leaving the sentence as it was and paying the condemned man out of his own
purse. But his was a case that could be repaired; my men were hanged past
all repair. How many condemnations have I seen more criminal than the
crimes themselves!

All of which brings to mind the old opinions: "that a man must do wrong in
the small if he's to do right in the large, and be unjust in little things
if he's to arrive at justice in great ones; that human justice is shaped on
the model of medicine, where whatever is useful is also just and right;
and what the Stoics held, that Nature herself goes against justice in most
of her works; and what the Cyrenaics received, that nothing is just in
itself, but customs and laws make justice; and what the Theodorians held,
that theft, sacrilege, and every kind of impurity are just in a wise man
if he finds them profitable to him." There's no remedy for it. I'm in the
same case Alcibiades was in: I will never, if I can help it, put myself in
the hands of a man who can decide about my head—where my life and honor
would depend more on my lawyer's skill and diligence than on my own
innocence. I'd trust myself to a justice that took notice of my good deeds
as well as my bad, where I had as much to hope for as to fear: being let
off isn't pay enough for a man who does better than merely avoid doing
wrong. Our justice shows us only one hand, and the left hand at that; let
a man be who he may, he's sure to come off with a loss.

In China—a kingdom whose government and arts, without any trade with or
knowledge of ours, surpass our examples in several excellent points, and
whose history teaches me how much greater and more various the world is
than either the ancients or we have been able to grasp—the officers sent
by the prince to inspect the state of his provinces punish those who
behave badly in their charge, and in the same way liberally reward those
who have conducted themselves better than the common run and beyond what
their duty required. These men come forward not just to be approved but to
gain; not just to be paid, but to be given a present.

No judge, thank God, has ever yet spoken to me as a judge, on any account
whatever—my own or another's, criminal or civil; and no prison has ever
held me, not even to walk through it. Imagination alone makes the very
outside of a jail hateful to me; I'm so in love with liberty that if I were
forbidden to set foot in some corner of the Indies, I'd live a little less
at my ease; and while I can find earth or air open elsewhere, I'll never
skulk in a place where I have to hide. Good God, how badly I would bear
the condition I see so many people in—nailed to one corner of the kingdom,
deprived of the right to enter the great cities and courts and the freedom
of the public roads, for having quarreled with our laws. If the laws I
live under so much as shook a finger at me in menace, I'd go looking for
others at once, wherever they might be. All my little prudence in the civil
wars we're now in the middle of is spent making sure they don't block my
freedom to come and go.

Now, laws keep up their credit not because they're just, but because
they're laws; that's the mystic foundation of their authority—they have
no other, and it serves their purpose well enough. They're often made by
fools, still more often by men who, hating equality, fail at fairness, but
always by men—vain, irresolute authors. There's nothing so faulty, so
grossly faulty, so ordinarily faulty, as the laws. Whoever obeys them
because they're just isn't obeying them as he should. Our French laws, by
their irregularity and deformity, lend a hand in some sort to the disorder
and corruption so plain in how they're handed down and carried out. The
command is so tangled and shifting

that it in some sort excuses disobedience and failure alike—in the
interpreting, the administering, and the observing of it. Whatever fruit we
may gather from experience, it will do little for our instruction if we
draw it from foreign examples while making so little profit from our own,
which is more familiar to us and surely enough to teach us what we need.
I study myself more than any other subject; that's my metaphysics, my
physics:

> "Quis deus hanc mundi temperet arte domum:
> Qua venit exoriens, qua deficit: unde coactis
> Cornibus in plenum menstrua luna redit
> Unde salo superant venti, quid flamine captet
> Eurus, et in nubes unde perennis aqua;
> Sit ventura dies mundi quae subruat arces...."

["What god governs with skill this dwelling of the world? From where does
the monthly moon rise, where does she wane? How is it that her horns
contract and fill out again? Why do winds rule the sea? What does the east
wind chase with its blasts? And where are the clouds forever supplied with
water? Is a day to come that will undermine the world?"—Propertius,
iii. 5, 26.]

> "Quaerite, quos agitat mundi labor."

["Ask those whom the cares of the world trouble."—Lucan, i. 417.]

In this universal order I let myself be led, ignorantly and carelessly,
by the general law of the world. I'll know it well enough when I feel it;
my learning can't make it change its course; it won't change itself for
me. It's folly to hope so, and a greater folly to trouble myself about it,
since it's necessarily the same for all and common to all. The goodness
and power of the Governor ought to discharge us entirely from caring about
the government. Philosophical inquiries and contemplations serve no other
use but to sharpen our curiosity. The philosophers, with good reason, send
us back to nature's rules; but they have nothing to do with so lofty a
knowledge—they falsify the rules and show us nature's face painted with
too high and too false a coloring, which is why so many different pictures
get made of so uniform a subject. As nature has given us feet to walk
with, so she has given us prudence to guide us through life: not as clever
or strong or showy a prudence as the one of their invention, but an easy,
quiet, wholesome one that does very well what the other only promises—in
the man lucky enough to know how to use it sincerely and steadily, that is
to say, according to nature. The simplest way of trusting yourself to
nature is the wisest way. Oh, what a soft, easy, wholesome pillow is
ignorance and incuriosity, for a well-ordered head to rest on!

I'd rather understand myself well in myself than in Cicero. From the
experience I have of myself, I'd find enough to make me wise, if I were
only a good student of it. Whoever calls to mind the excess of his past
anger, and how far that fever carried him, will see the ugliness of this
passion better than in Aristotle, and conceive a truer hatred of it.
Whoever remembers the ills he's gone through, those that threatened him,
and the small occasions that moved him from one state to another, will by
that prepare himself for future changes and gain knowledge of his
condition. The life of Caesar is no greater an example to us than our own:
it's a life of command and renown, but still a life subject to every human
accident. We have only to listen to our own; we apply to ourselves
whatever we most need. Whoever calls to mind how many times he's been
wrong in his own judgment—isn't he a great fool if he doesn't distrust it
ever after? When I find myself convinced by someone else's reasoning of a
false opinion I held, I don't so much learn the new thing he told me, or
that particular bit of ignorance—that would be no great gain—as I learn,
in general, my own weakness and the treachery of my understanding, and
from that I draw the reform of the whole mass. I do the same with all my
other errors, and I find great use for life in this rule. I don't look at
the kind or the instance, like a stone I've stumbled over; I learn to
suspect my steps everywhere, and take care to set them right. To learn
that a man has said or done a foolish thing is nothing; a man must learn
that he is nothing but a fool—a much fuller and more important lesson.
The false steps my memory has so often made, even when it was most sure
and confident of itself, aren't thrown away on me for nothing: it swears
and assures me in vain— I shake my head; the first objection raised
against its testimony puts me in suspense, and I dare not rely on it in
anything of weight, nor vouch for it in someone else's business. And if it
weren't that what I do for lack of memory, others do more often for lack
of good faith, I'd always, in matters of fact, rather take the truth from
another man's mouth than from my own. If everyone pried into the effects
and circumstances of the passions that sway him, as I've done into the
ones I'm most subject to, he'd see them coming and would break their force
and speed a little. They don't always seize us all at once; there's
warning and degrees:

> "Fluctus uti primo coepit cum albescere vento,
> Paulatim sese tollit mare, et altius undas
> Erigit, inde imo consurgit ad aethera fundo."

["As with the first wind the sea begins to whiten and foam, then swells by
degrees and raises its waves higher and higher, till the ocean rises from
its depths to the sky."—Virgil, Aeneid, vii. 528.]

Judgment holds the master's seat in me—or at least it tries hard to make
it so. It leaves my appetites to go their own way: hatred and friendship,
even the love I bear myself, without change or corruption. If it can't
reform the other parts after its own pattern, at least it won't let itself
be corrupted by them; it plays its game apart.

The advice to everyone "to know himself" should carry great weight, since
that god of wisdom and light had it written on the front of his
temple—[at Delphi]—as summing up all he had to advise us. Plato says too
that prudence is nothing but carrying out this command; and Socrates works
it out in detail in Xenophon. The difficulties and obscurities in any
science are seen only by those who've gotten inside it; a certain degree
of understanding is needed to be able to know that a man doesn't know, and
you have to push against a door to know whether it's bolted against you or
not. That's where this Platonic subtlety comes from: "neither should those
who know inquire, since they know; nor those who don't know, since to
inquire they must know what they're inquiring about." So it is with this
business of "knowing oneself": that every man is seen so settled and
satisfied with himself, that every man thinks himself intelligent enough,
shows that nobody knows anything about the matter—as Socrates lets
Euthydemus understand. I, who profess nothing else, find in it so infinite
a depth and variety that all the fruit I've gathered from my learning only
makes me feel how much I have to learn. To my weakness, so often
confessed, I owe my bent toward modesty, toward the obedience of the
beliefs prescribed to me, toward a constant coolness and moderation in my
opinions, and a hatred of that troublesome, quarrelsome arrogance that
wholly believes and trusts in itself—the capital enemy of learning and
truth. Just listen to them laying down the law: the first foolish things
they utter come out in the style men use to establish religions and laws:

> "Nihil est turpius, quam cognitioni et perceptioni assertionem
> approbationemque praecurrere."

["Nothing is uglier than for assertion and approval to run ahead of
knowledge and perception."—Cicero, Academics, i. 13.]

Aristarchus said that in ancient times there were scarcely seven wise men
to be found in the world, and in his time scarcely so many fools. Don't we
have more reason than he to say so in our age? Dogmatic assertion and
obstinacy are plain signs of a lack of sense. This fellow may have knocked
his nose on the ground a hundred times in a day, yet he'll be at his
"therefores" as firm and sturdy as ever. You'd think some new soul and
vigor of understanding had been poured into him since, and that it had
happened to him as it did to that ancient son of the earth, who took fresh
courage and strength from his fall:

> "Cui cum tetigere parentem,
> jam defecta vigent renovata robore membra:"

["Whose failing limbs, when they touched his mother earth, at once took on
new strength."—Lucan, iv. 599.]

Doesn't this incorrigible blockhead think he's picked up a new
understanding just by picking up a new dispute? It's from my own
experience that I accuse human ignorance, which in my opinion is the surest
lesson in the world's school. Those who won't conclude it in themselves,
from so lowly an example as mine or their own, let them believe it from
Socrates, the master of masters. For the philosopher Antisthenes said to
his pupils, "Let us go hear Socrates; there I will sit as a pupil with
you"—holding to that Stoic teaching that virtue alone is enough to make a
life completely happy, needing nothing else whatever, he added, except the
strength of Socrates.

That long attention I give to studying myself also fits me to judge
others well enough; and there are few things I speak of better or with
better excuse. It very often happens that I see and pick out my friends'
qualities more exactly than they do themselves; I've astonished some of
them with how on the mark my description was, and given them warning about
themselves. Having been used from childhood to study my own life in the
lives of others, I've picked up a studious turn in that line; and once I'm
set on it, few things about me—faces, humors, talk—that serve the purpose
escape me. I study everything, both what I'm to avoid and what I'm to
follow. In my friends, too, I make out their inward leanings from what
they produce—not by sorting this infinite variety of such different,
unconnected actions into certain kinds and chapters, and neatly
distributing my lots and divisions under known heads and classes:

> "Sed neque quam multae species, nec nomina quae sint,
> Est numerus."

["But of how many kinds there are, and what their names are, there is no
counting."—Virgil, Georgics, ii. 103.]

The wise set out their fancies more particularly, piece by piece; I, who
see no further into things than use informs me, present mine in a general
way, without rule, by trial and error. I deliver my opinion in separate,
unconnected articles, as something that can't be spoken all at once and in
the lump. Relation and conformity aren't to be found in low, common souls
like ours. Wisdom is a solid, whole building, where every piece keeps its
place and bears its mark:

> "Sola sapientia in se tota conversa est."

["Wisdom alone is wholly turned inward upon itself."—Cicero, On Ends,
iii. 7.]

I leave it to the professionals—and I don't know whether they'll manage
it—in so tangled, minute, and chancy a business, to marshal this infinite
variety of faces into distinct bodies, to fix our inconstancy and set it
in order. I don't just find it hard to piece our actions together; I find
it hard besides to mark each one by itself with any chief quality, so
ambiguous and many-shaped they are in different lights. What's remarked as
rare in Perseus, king of Macedon—"that his mind, fixing itself to no one
condition, wandered through every way of living, and showed manners so
wild and rambling that neither he himself nor anyone else knew what kind
of man he was"—seems to fit almost everybody; and I've known another man
of his stamp to whom I think this conclusion might more properly be
applied: no moderate settledness, always rushing headlong from one extreme
to the other on occasions no one could guess at, no straight path without
turns and wonderful contradictions, no one quality simple and unmixed—so
that the best guess men could make one day would be that he made a study
of making himself known by being unknowable. A man needs sound ears to
hear himself frankly criticized; and since there are few who can bear to
hear it without smarting, those who risk doing it for us show a singular
proof of friendship—for it is loving sincerely indeed to dare to wound and
offend us for our own good. I think it harsh to judge a man whose bad
qualities outweigh his good ones. Plato requires three things in whoever
would examine another's soul: knowledge, goodwill, and boldness.

I was once asked what I thought myself fit for, if anyone had wanted to
make use of me when I was of a suitable age:

> "Dum melior vires sanguis dabat, aemula necdum
> Temporibus geminis canebat sparsa senectus:"

["While better blood gave me strength, and before envious old age
whitened and thinned my temples."—Virgil, Aeneid, v. 415.]

"For nothing," said I—and I readily excuse myself from knowing anything
that would enslave me to others. But I would have told my master the
truth—[Was this Henri VI.? D.W.]—and would have trained his manners, if he
had pleased, not wholesale by school lessons, which I don't understand and
from which I see no true reform come in those who do, but by watching him
at leisure, on every occasion, and judging him plainly and naturally as an
eyewitness, one point at a time—letting him know how he stood in common
opinion, as against his flatterers. There's none of us who wouldn't be
worse than kings if we were as continually corrupted as they are by that
sort of rabble. What wonder if Alexander, that great king and philosopher,
couldn't defend himself against them!

I should have had loyalty, judgment, and freedom enough for that office.
It would have to be a nameless office, or it would lose its grace and its
effect; and it's a part not fit for all men indifferently. For truth
itself hasn't the privilege of being spoken at all times and to all
comers; its use, noble as it is, has its cautions and limits. It often
happens, as the world goes, that a man slips it into a prince's ear not
only to no purpose but harmfully and unjustly besides; and no man will
make me believe that a virtuous remonstrance can't be viciously applied,
or that the interest of the substance doesn't often have to give way to
the interest of the form.

For such a purpose I'd want a man content with his own fortune:

> "Quod sit, esse velit, nihilque malit,"

["Who is willing to be what he is, and wishes for nothing more."—Martial,
x. ii, 18.]

and of middling station. For on the one hand he wouldn't be afraid to
touch his master's heart to the quick, for fear of losing his preferment
by it; and on the other, being of no high rank, he'd have easier dealings
with all sorts of people. I'd have this office limited to a single man;
for to give the privilege of this freedom and privacy to many would breed
an inconvenient disrespect. And in that one man, above all things, I'd
require the loyalty of silence.

A king isn't to be believed when he boasts of his constancy in standing
the enemy's shock for his glory, if for his profit and improvement he can't
stand a friend's free advice—which has no power but to pinch his ear, the
rest of its effect still lying in his own hands. Now, no condition of men
stands in such great need of true and free advice and warning as kings
do: they lead a public life and have to satisfy the opinion of so many
onlookers that, as those around them hide from them whatever might turn
them from their own way, they find themselves, before they know it,
wrapped in the hatred and loathing of their people—often on occasions they
might have avoided, without any loss even to their pleasures, if they'd
been advised and set right in time. Their favorites commonly think more of
themselves than of their master; and indeed it suits them, for in truth
most offices of real friendship, when offered to a sovereign, run a rough
and dangerous risk—so that there's great need in them not only of very
great affection and freedom, but of courage too.

In short, all this hotchpotch I'm scribbling here is nothing but a record
of the essays of my own life—which, for inward soundness, is exemplary
enough to take lessons from, even against the grain. But as for bodily
health, no man can supply more profitable experience than I, who present
it pure, noway corrupted or changed by art or opinion. Experience is
properly on its own dunghill in the subject of medicine, where reason
wholly gives it place. Tiberius said that whoever had lived twenty years
ought to answer for himself in whatever was harmful or wholesome to him,
and know how to manage himself without medicine;

> [All that Suetonius says in his Life of Tiberius is that he said the man
> who, having reached sixty, held out his pulse to a physician was a
> fool.]

and he might have learned it from Socrates, who, advising his pupils to
make their health their chief study, added that it would be a strange
thing if a sensible man, attending to his exercise and diet, didn't know
better than any physician what was good or bad for him. And medicine
itself always professes to take experience as the test of what it does.
So Plato had reason to say that to be a proper physician, a man who'd
become one would first have to have passed through all the diseases he
claims to cure himself, and through all the accidents and circumstances
he's to judge of. It's only reasonable they should catch the pox if they
want to know how to cure it. For my part, I'd put myself in such hands;
the others only guide us like a man who paints seas and rocks and harbors
sitting at his table, and there draws the model of a ship sailing in
perfect safety—but set him to the work itself and he doesn't know which
end to begin at. They describe our maladies the way a town crier describes
a lost horse or dog—such a color, such a height, such an ear—but bring the
beast to him and he doesn't know it, for all that. If medicine should one
day give me some good, visible relief, then truly I'll cry out in good
earnest:

> "Tandem effcaci do manus scientiae."

["At last I take efficacious science by the hand."—Horace.]

The arts that promise to keep our bodies and souls healthy promise a great
deal; but at the same time there are none that keep their promise less.
And in our time, those who profess these arts among us show the effects
less than any other sort of men. The most you can say of them is that
they sell medicinal drugs; that they're physicians, no man can say.

> [The 1588 edition adds: "Judging by themselves, and by those who are
> ruled by them."]

I've lived long enough to be able to give an account of the custom that's
carried me so far; for anyone who wants to try it, as his taster I've made
the experiment. Here are some of the items, as my memory supplies them—I
have no custom that hasn't varied with circumstances, but I record only
those I've known best and that have so far had the greatest hold on me.

My way of life is the same in sickness as in health: the same bed, the
same hours, the same meat, and even the same drink serve me in both states
alike. I add nothing to them but more or less moderation, according to my
strength and appetite. My health is keeping my usual state undisturbed. I
see that sickness puts me off it on one side, and if I let the physicians
rule me they'll put me off it on the other—so that by fortune and by art
alike I'm thrown off my course. I believe nothing more firmly than this:
that I can't be harmed by the use of things I've been so long used to.
It's for custom to shape a man's life as she pleases; she's everything in
that. She's Circe's potion, changing our nature as she sees fit. How many
nations, only three steps from us, think the fear of the night dew—so
plainly harmful to us—a ridiculous fancy; and our own boatmen and peasants
laugh at it. You make a German sick if you put him on a mattress, as you
do an Italian if you put him on a featherbed, and a Frenchman if you give
him no curtains or fire. A Spanish stomach can't stand eating the way we
do, nor ours drinking the way the Swiss do. A German at Augsburg made me
laugh heartily by finding fault with our fireplaces, using the same
arguments we commonly use to run down their stoves. For to tell the truth,
the smothered heat, and then the smell of the heated stuff the fire is
made of, greatly offends those not used to them—not me. And indeed, the
heat being always equal, constant, and spread everywhere, with no flame,
no smoke, and none of the wind that comes down our chimneys, theirs may in
many ways stand comparison with ours. Why don't we copy the Roman way of
building? They say that in ancient times fires weren't made inside the
houses but outside, at the foot of them, and the heat was carried through
the whole building by pipes built into the wall, winding around the rooms
to be warmed—which I've seen plainly described somewhere in Seneca. This
German, hearing me praise the conveniences and beauties of his city—and it
truly deserves it—began to pity me for having to leave it; and the first
drawback he named was the heaviness of head the chimneys elsewhere would
give me. He'd heard someone make this complaint and pinned it on us, being
by custom unable to notice it at home. All heat that comes from fire
weakens and dulls me. Evenus said fire was the best seasoning of life; I
choose any other way of warming myself.

We're afraid to drink our wines when we get toward the bottom of the
cask; in Portugal those fumes are thought delicious, and it's the drink of
princes. In short, every nation has many customs and ways not only unknown
to other nations but savage and miraculous in their eyes. What are we to
do with people who accept no evidence that isn't in print, who don't
believe men unless they're in a book, nor truth unless it's of a
respectable age? We dignify our follies by committing them to the press:
it carries much more weight to say "I've read such a thing" than just to
say "I've heard such a thing." But I, who no more distrust a man's mouth
than his pen, and who know men write as indiscreetly as they speak, and
who look on this age as one already past, quote a friend as readily as
Aulus Gellius or Macrobius, and what I've seen as readily as what they've
written. And as it's held of virtue that it's no greater for having lasted
longer, so I hold of truth that for being older it's none the wiser. I
often say it's mere folly that makes us run after foreign, book-learned
examples; their fertility is the same now as in the time of Homer and
Plato. But isn't it that we seek more honor from the quotation than from
the truth of the matter in hand—as if it were more to the purpose to
borrow our proofs from the print shops of Vascosan or Plantin than from
what's to be seen in our own village? Or else, indeed, that we haven't the
wit to pick out and make useful what we see before us, and to judge it
clearly enough to turn it into an example. For if we say we need authority
to give weight to our testimony, we're wide of the mark; since in my
opinion, out of the most ordinary, common, well-known things—if we could
only find their light—the greatest miracles of nature could be formed,
and the most wonderful examples, especially on the subject of human
actions.

Now, on this subject—setting aside the examples I've gathered from books,
and what Aristotle tells of Andron the Argive, that he crossed the burning
sands of Libya without drinking—a gentleman who has carried himself very
well in several employments said, in a place where I was, that he'd ridden
from Madrid to Lisbon in the heat of summer without any drink at all. He's
very healthy and vigorous for his age, and there's nothing extraordinary in
his way of life but this: that he sometimes goes two or three months, even
a whole year, as he's told me, without drinking. He's sometimes thirsty,
but he lets it pass; and he holds that it's an appetite that easily goes
away of itself. He drinks more from fancy than from need or pleasure.

Here's another example: not long ago I found one of the most learned men
in France, a man of no small fortune, studying in a corner of a hall
they'd screened off for him with tapestry, with a rabble of servants all
around him making free with their noise. He told me—and Seneca says almost
the same of himself—that he turned this racket to advantage: beaten on by
this noise, he collected and drew himself into himself all the more for
contemplation, and this storm of voices drove his thoughts back inside
him. When he was a student at Padua, his study stood so long amid the
rattle of coaches and the tumult of the square that he not only trained
himself to scorn noise but even to use it in the service of his studies.
Socrates answered Alcibiades, who wondered how he could stand his wife's
perpetual scolding: "Why," said he, "as those do who get used to the
ordinary noise of wheels drawing water." I'm quite the opposite: I have a
tender head, easily upset; when it's bent on something, the least buzzing
of a fly kills it.

Seneca in his youth, having warmly taken up the example of Sextius and
eating nothing that had died, went a whole year without such food and, as
he said, with pleasure; and he gave it up so as not to be suspected of
taking up this rule from some new religion that prescribed it. In the same
way he took from the precepts of Attalus the custom of never lying on any
bedding that sank under his weight, and even in his old age used only
beds that wouldn't yield to any pressure. What the usage of his time made
him count as roughness, ours makes us count as softness.

Just look at the difference between my laborers' way of living and mine;
the Scythians and Indians have nothing more remote, both from what I can
do and from my shape. I've taken on charity boys to serve me who soon
after quit both my kitchen and my livery, just to get back to their old
way of life; and I found one afterward picking mussels out of the gutter
for his dinner, whom neither coaxing nor threats could wean from the
sweetness he found in poverty. Beggars have their splendors and delights
as well as the rich, and, they say, their dignities and governments. These
are the effects of custom: she can mold us not only into whatever shape
she pleases—the wise tell us to apply ourselves to the best, which she'll
soon make easy for us—but also into change and variety, which is the
noblest and most useful lesson she teaches us. The best thing about my
bodily condition is that I'm flexible and not very obstinate: I have
inclinations of my own, more ordinary and more agreeable than others, but
I'm turned aside from them with very little ado and slip easily into the
opposite course. A young man should cross his own rules, to wake up his
vigor and keep it from growing faint and rusty; and there's no way of life
so weak and stupid as one carried on by rule and discipline:

> "Ad primum lapidem vectari quum placet, hora
> Sumitur ex libro; si prurit frictus ocelli
> Angulus, inspecta genesi, collyria quaerit;"

["When he wants to be carried to the first milestone, the hour is picked
from the almanac; if the rubbed corner of his eye itches, his horoscope
examined, he calls for eye-salve."—Juvenal, vi. 576.]

He should often throw himself into excesses too, if he'll take my advice;
otherwise the smallest spree will ruin him and make him troublesome and
disagreeable company. The worst quality in a well-bred man is being
over-particular, tied to one fixed way—and it is fixed, if it isn't
pliable and supple. It's a kind of reproach not to be able, or not to
dare, to do what we see those around us doing; let such men stay home. In
every man it's unbecoming, but in a soldier vicious and intolerable—who,
as Philopoemen said, ought to accustom himself to every variety and
unevenness of life.

Though I was brought up, as much as possible, to liberty and
independence, yet so it is that, growing old and having settled by
indifference on certain forms—my age is now past instruction and has
nothing to do henceforth but keep itself up as best it can—custom has
already, before I knew it, stamped its mark on me in certain things, so
that I count it a kind of excess to give them up. Without doing myself
violence, I can't sleep in the daytime, or eat between meals, or take
breakfast, or go to bed without a good stretch between eating and
sleeping—[Gastroesophogeal Reflux. D.W.]—about three hours after supper.
Nor have children except before I sleep, nor have them standing; nor stand
my own sweat; nor quench my thirst with pure water or pure wine; nor keep
my head long bare; nor have my hair cut after dinner. And I'd be as uneasy
without my gloves as without my shirt, or without washing when I rise from
table or from bed; and I couldn't lie without a canopy and curtains, as if
they were necessities. I could dine without a tablecloth, but not without a
clean napkin—after the German fashion—very comfortably; I dirty them more
than the Germans or Italians do, and make little use of spoon or fork. I'm
sorry they didn't keep up the fashion, started after the example of kings,
of changing our napkin at every course, as they do our plate. We're told
of that hard-working soldier Marius that, growing old, he got particular
about his drink and would drink only out of a cup of his own. In the same
way I've let myself fancy a certain shape of glass, and don't willingly
drink from common glasses, any more than from a strange common hand: all
metal offends me compared with something clear and transparent—let my eyes
taste too, according to their capacity. I owe several other such niceties
to custom. Nature, on the other side, has helped me to some of hers: as,
not being able to stand more than two full meals in one day without
overloading my stomach, nor to skip one of those meals entirely without
filling myself with wind, drying up my mouth, and dulling my appetite; the
great discomfort I get from too much evening air—for in recent years, on
night marches, which often last all night, after five or six hours my
stomach starts to turn, with a violent headache, so that I always throw up
before daybreak. When the others go to breakfast, I go to sleep; and when
I get up, I'm as brisk and gay as before. I'd always been told the night
dew never rises except at the start of the night; but for some years now,
long and familiar dealings with a lord possessed with the notion that the
night dew is sharpest and most dangerous as the sun declines, an hour or
two before it sets—which he carefully avoids, scorning that of the
night—has almost pressed on me, not so much his reasoning as his
experience. What! Shall mere doubt and inquiry strike our imagination so
as to change us? Those who give way to these leanings absolutely and all
at once draw total ruin on themselves. I'm sorry for several gentlemen
who, through their physicians' folly, have in their youth and health shut
themselves up entirely: it would be better to put up with a cough than, by
disuse, to lose forever the company of common life in things of such great
use. Malignant science, to bar us from the pleasantest hours of the day!
Let's keep our possession to the last; for the most part a man hardens
himself by holding out, and corrects his constitution—as Caesar did his
falling sickness—by dint of scorning it. A man should attach himself to
the best rules, but not enslave himself to them—except to such, if any
there are, where obligation and servitude pay.

Both kings and philosophers go to stool, and ladies too; public lives are
tied to ceremony; mine, obscure and private, enjoys every natural freedom.
Soldier and Gascon are also qualities a little given to indiscretion;
so I'll say of this act of relieving nature that it's good to tie it to
certain fixed night hours and force yourself to it by custom, as I've
done—but not to tie yourself, as I've done in my declining years, to one
particular convenience of place and seat for the purpose, and make it
troublesome by long sitting. And yet, in the fouler offices, isn't it in
some measure excusable to ask for more care and cleanliness?

> "Natura homo mundum et elegans animal est."

["Man is by nature a clean and dainty creature."—Seneca, Epistle 92.]

Of all nature's operations, I'm most impatient at being interrupted in
that one. I've seen many soldiers troubled by unruly bellies; mine and I
never miss our punctual appointment, which is on leaping out of bed,
unless some unavoidable business or sickness gets in the way.

I think then, as I said before, that sick men can't place themselves
anywhere more safely than by sitting still in the course of life they've
been bred and trained up in; change, whatever it is, disorders and puts a
man out. Do you think chestnuts can hurt a man of Périgord or of Lucca, or
milk and cheese the mountain folk? We order them not only a new way of
life but a contrary one—a change the healthy couldn't stand. Prescribe
water to a Breton of seventy; shut a sailor up in a hothouse; forbid a
Basque footman to walk: you'll rob them of motion, and in the end of air
and light:

> "An vivere tanti est?
> Cogimur a suetis animum suspendere rebus,
> Atque, ut vivamus, vivere desinimus...
> Hos superesse reor, quibus et spirabilis aer
> Et lux, qua regimur, redditur ipsa gravis."

["Is life worth so much? We're forced to withhold our minds from the
things we're used to; and, to live, we stop living.... Do I think they
still live, for whom the very air we breathe, and the light itself that
governs us, is made oppressive?"—Pseudo-Gallus, Eclogues, i. 155, 247.]

If the physicians do no other good, they do at least this: they prepare
their patients in good time for death, by little and little undermining
and cutting off their use of life.

Both well and sick, I've always willingly let myself obey the appetites
that pressed on me. I give my desires and leanings a long rein; I don't
like curing one disease with another; I hate remedies more troublesome
than the disease itself. To be subject to colic and subject to abstaining
from oysters are two evils instead of one: the disease torments us on one
side and the remedy on the other. Since we're always in danger of being
wrong, let's rather run the risk of a mistake after we've had the
pleasure. The world goes quite the other way, and thinks nothing
profitable that isn't painful; it has great suspicion of what's easy. My
appetite, in various things, has of its own accord happily enough fitted
itself to my stomach's health. Rich, sharp sauces pleased me when I was
young; my stomach having since taken a dislike to them, my taste promptly
followed. Wine is bad for sick people, and it's the first thing my mouth
finds distasteful then, with an unconquerable dislike. Whatever I take
against my liking does me harm; and nothing hurts me that I eat with
appetite and delight. I never took harm from anything very pleasant to me;
and accordingly I've made all medical conclusions give way freely to my
pleasure. And when I was young,

> "Quem circumcursans huc atque huc saepe Cupido
> Fulgebat crocink splendidus in tunic."

["When Cupid, fluttering round me here and there, shone bright in his
saffron tunic."—Catullus, lxvi. 133.]

I gave myself the rein as freely and thoughtlessly to the desire that
ruled in me as anyone whatever:

> "Et militavi non sine gloria;"

["And I have soldiered not without glory."—Horace, Odes, iii. 26, 2.]

yet more in keeping at it and holding out than in sallying:

> "Sex me vix memini sustinuisse vices."

["I can hardly remember holding out for six rounds in one
night."—Ovid, Amores, iii. 7, 26.]

It's certainly a misfortune and a marvel at once to confess at what a
tender age I first came under love's yoke. It was indeed by chance, for it
was long before the years of choice or knowledge; I can't remember myself
so far back. And my fortune may well be matched with Quartilla's, who
couldn't remember when she'd been a maid:

> "Inde tragus, celeresque pili, mirandaque matri
> Barba meae."

["Thence the smell of the armpits, the early hair, and the beard that
astonished my mother."—Martial, xi. 22, 7.]

Physicians bend their rules to the strong cravings that come over sick
people, usually with good success; so great a desire can't be imagined so
strange and wrong but that nature must have a hand in it. And then, how
easy it is to satisfy the fancy! In my opinion this part carries it, at
least, over all the rest. The most grievous and ordinary evils are the
ones fancy loads us with. This Spanish saying pleases me in several ways:

> "Defenda me Dios de me."

["God defend me from myself."]

I'm sorry, when I'm sick, if I haven't some craving that might give me
the pleasure of satisfying it; all the rules of medicine would hardly turn
me from it. I do the same when I'm well; I can see very little more to
hope or wish for. It would be a pity for a man to be so weak and
languishing as not to have even wishing left to him.

The art of medicine isn't so settled that we need lack authority for
whatever we do; it changes with climates and moons, according to Fernel
and according to Scaliger—[physicians to Henri II]. If your physician
doesn't think it good for you to sleep, to drink wine, or to eat such and
such meats, never trouble yourself; I'll find you another who won't be of
his opinion. The diversity of medical arguments and opinions takes in all
sorts and shapes. I saw a poor sick man panting and burning with thirst,
that he might be cured, who was afterward laughed at for his pains by
another physician, who condemned that advice as harmful to him. Hadn't he
tormented himself to good purpose? There lately died of the stone a man of
that profession who'd used extreme abstinence to fight his disease; his
fellow physicians say that, on the contrary, this abstinence had dried him
up and baked the gravel in his kidneys.

I've noticed that, both in wounds and in sicknesses, talking upsets and
hurts me as much as any irregularity I can commit. My voice pains and
tires me, for it's loud and forced—so that when I've gone aside to whisper
with some great persons about important business, they've often asked me
to lower my voice.

This story is worth a digression. Someone in a certain Greek school
speaking as loud as I do, the master of the ceremonies sent word to him to
speak softly. "Tell him, then, he must send me," replied the other, "the
tone he'd have me speak in." To which the other answered, "that he should
take the tone from the ears of the man he's speaking to." It was well
said, if it's to be understood as: "Speak according to the matter you're
speaking of to your hearer." For if it means "'tis enough that he hear
you," or "govern yourself by him," I don't find it reasonable. The tone
and movement of my voice carries a great deal of the expression and
meaning of what I say, and it's for me to govern it, to make myself
understood: there's a voice to instruct, a voice to flatter, and a voice
to scold. I want not only that my voice reach him, but, maybe, that it
strike and pierce him. When I rate my valet in sharp, bitter language, it
would be very pretty for him to say, "Pray, master, speak lower; I hear
you very well":

> "Est quaedam vox ad auditum accommodata,
> non magnitudine, sed proprietate."

["There is a certain voice fitted to the hearing, not by its loudness but
by its propriety."—Quintilian, xi. 3.]

Speaking is half the speaker's and half the hearer's; the hearer ought to
prepare himself to receive it according to its drift—just as with
tennis players, the one who receives the ball shifts and gets ready
according as he sees the striker move, and according to the stroke itself.

Experience has taught me this besides: that we ruin ourselves by
impatience. Evils have their life and their limits, their diseases and
their recovery.

The constitution of maladies is formed on the pattern of the constitution
of animals: they have their fortune and their days limited from birth. He
who tries imperiously to cut them short by force in the middle of their
course lengthens and multiplies them, and provokes instead of calming
them. I'm of Crantor's opinion: that we should neither obstinately and
deafly oppose evils nor give in to them for lack of courage, but
naturally give way to them according to their condition and our own. We
ought to grant free passage to diseases; I find they stay a shorter time
with me, who let them alone. And I've lost some, reckoned the most
tenacious and obstinate, by their own decay, without help and without art,
and contrary to its rules. Let's allow Nature to take her own way a
little; she understands her own business better than we do. "But so-and-so
died of it." And so will you—if not of that disease, of another. And how
many haven't escaped dying who've had three physicians at their heels?
Example is a vague, universal mirror, with many different reflections. If
it's a pleasant medicine, take it: it's always so much present good. I'll
never balk at the name or the color, if it's pleasant and grateful to the
palate: pleasure is one of the chief kinds of profit. I've let colds,
gouty fluxes, looseness, palpitations of the heart, headaches, and other
ailments grow old and die in time a natural death. I've so lost them when
I was half inclined to keep them; they're sooner won over by courtesy
than by bluster. We must patiently bear the laws of our condition; we're
born to grow old, to grow weak, and to be sick, in spite of all medicine.
It's the first lesson the Mexicans teach their children; as soon as
they're born they greet them thus: "You have come into the world, child,
to endure: endure, suffer, and say nothing." It's unjust to lament what
has befallen someone when it may befall everyone:

> "Indignare, si quid in te inique proprio constitutum est."

["Be angry, then, only when something is unjustly decreed against you
alone."—Seneca, Epistle 91.]

Look at an old man begging God to keep his health strong and whole—that is
to say, to give him back his youth:

> "Stulte, quid haec frustra votis puerilibus optas?"

["Fool, why do you vainly wish these childish wishes?"—Ovid., Trist.,
111. 8, II.]

Isn't it folly? His condition can't bear it. Gout, the stone, and
indigestion are the marks of long years, as heat, rains, and winds are of
long journeys. Plato doesn't believe Aesculapius bothered to prescribe a
regimen to spin out life in a weak, worn-out body, useless to its country
and its calling, or to beget healthy, sturdy children; and he doesn't
think this care suits the divine justice and prudence that directs all
things to usefulness. My good friend, your business is done; nobody can
set you up again. At most they can patch you up and prop you a little,
and by that stretch your misery out an hour or two:

> "Non secus instantem cupiens fulcire ruinam,
> Diversis contra nititur obicibus;
> Donec certa dies, omni compage soluta,
> Ipsum cum rebus subruat auxilium."

["Like one who, wanting to prop up a threatening ruin, sets various
props against it, till, one fixed day, every joint giving way, the very
props are buried with the house."—Pseudo-Gallus, i. 171.]

We must learn to suffer what we can't escape; our life, like the harmony
of the world, is made up of contrary things—of different tones, sweet and
harsh, sharp and flat, lively and solemn. The musician who'd use only some
of these—what could he do? He must know how to use them all and mix them;
and so we should mix the goods and evils that belong to our life with each
other. Our being can't go on without this mixture, and one part is no less
necessary to it than the other. To try to fight natural necessity is to
play the folly of Ctesiphon, who undertook to kick back at his
mule—[Plutarch, How to Restrain Anger, c. 8].

I consult very little about the changes I feel in myself: for these
doctors take advantage—once they have you at their mercy, they stuff your
ears with their forecasts. Once before, catching me weakened by sickness,
they handled me roughly with their dogmas and schoolmasterly follies—one
minute threatening me with great pains, the next with approaching death.
I was indeed moved and shaken by it, but not beaten or knocked off my
stand; and though my judgment was neither changed nor distracted, it was
at least disturbed. It's always agitation and combat.

Now I use my imagination as gently as I can, and would rid it, if I
could, of all trouble and strife; a man must help, flatter, and coax it,
if he can. My mind is fit for that office; it doesn't need appearances
all through: if it could persuade as it preaches, it would relieve me
successfully. Want an example? It tells me "that it's for my good to have
the stone; that the structure of my age must naturally suffer some decay,
and it's now time it began to come apart and own to a breach; it's a
common necessity, and there's nothing miraculous or new in it; in it I
pay what's due to old age, and I can't expect a better bargain; that
company should comfort me, having fallen into the most common infirmity of
my age—I see men everywhere tormented with the same disease, and I'm
honored by the fellowship, since men of the best quality are most often
afflicted with it; it's a noble, dignified disease; that of those struck
with it, few have it with less pain; that these others are put to the
trouble of a strict diet and the daily taking of nauseous potions, whereas
I owe my better state purely to my good fortune—for some ordinary broths
of eryngo or burstwort that I've taken two or three times to oblige the
ladies, who with more kindness than my pain was sharp insisted on giving
me half of theirs, seemed to me as easy to take as they were fruitless in
working; the others have to pay a thousand vows to Aesculapius and as
many crowns to their physicians for voiding a little gravel, which I
often do with nature's help alone. Even the composure of my face isn't
disturbed in company; and I can hold my water ten hours, as long as any
man in health. The fear of this disease," says my mind, "once frightened
you when it was unknown to you; the cries and despairing groans of those
who make it worse by their impatience bred horror in you. It's an
infirmity that punishes the members you've most offended with. You're a
conscientious fellow";

> "Quae venit indigne poena, dolenda venit:"

["A punishment we haven't deserved may well be grieved
over."—Ovid, Heroides, v. 8.]

"consider this chastisement: it's very light compared with others, and
laid on with a father's tenderness. Just see how late it comes; it seizes
and troubles only that part of your life which is, one way or another,
barren and lost—having, as it were by agreement, allowed time for the
license and pleasures of your youth. The fear and pity people feel for
this disease serve you as matter for glory—a quality of which, if you've
purified your judgment of it and your reason has somewhat cured it, your
friends nonetheless make out some trace in your looks. It's a pleasure to
hear it said of oneself: what strength of mind, what patience! You're seen
sweating with pain, turning pale and red, trembling, vomiting blood,
suffering strange contractions and convulsions, at times letting great
tears fall from your eyes, passing thick, black, dreadful water, or having
it stopped by some sharp, jagged stone that cruelly pricks and tears the
neck of the bladder—while all the while you entertain the company with an
ordinary face; droning along at intervals with your people; taking your
part in unbroken talk, now and then excusing your pain and making your
suffering out less than it is. Do you remember the men of past times who
so eagerly sought out diseases to keep their virtue in breath and
exercise? Suppose nature sets you on and drives you into this glorious
school, which you'd never have entered of your own free will. If you tell
me it's a dangerous, mortal disease—what others aren't? For it's a
doctor's trick to make you expect any they say don't go straight to death.
What does it matter if they get there by accident, or if they easily slide
and slip into the path that leads us to it? But you don't die because
you're sick; you die because you're alive. Death kills you without
sickness's help; and sickness has put death off in some, who've lived
longer because they always thought themselves dying. To which may be
added that, as with wounds, so with diseases: some are medicinal and
wholesome. The stone is often no less long-lived than you; we see men it's
stayed with from childhood to extreme old age—and if they hadn't parted
company, it would have stayed longer still. You kill it more often than it
kills you. And though it shows you the picture of approaching death, isn't
it doing a good office to a man of your age, to remind him of his end?
And what's worse, you've no longer anything that should make you want to
be cured. Whether or no, common necessity will soon call you away. Just
see how skillfully and gently she puts you out of conceit with life, and
weans you from the world—not forcing you with tyrannical subjection, like
so many other infirmities you see old men afflicted with, that hold them
in continual torment and keep them in perpetual, unbroken weakness and
pain, but by warnings and lessons at intervals, mixing in long pauses of
rest, as if to give you time to meditate and chew over your lesson at
your own ease and leisure. To give you means to judge rightly and take on
the resolve of a man of courage, she shows you the state of your whole
condition, in good and evil alike; and one while a very cheerful, another
an unbearable life, in one and the same day. If you don't embrace death,
at least you shake hands with it once a month; so you have more reason to
hope it'll one day catch you unawares, without threatening; and that,
being so often led to the water's edge but still thinking yourself on the
usual terms, you and your confidence will one time or another be
unexpectedly ferried across. A man can't reasonably complain of diseases
that fairly divide the time

with health."

I'm obliged to Fortune for having so often attacked me with the same kind
of weapons: she shapes and fashions me by use, hardens and habituates me,
so that I can tell within a little what it'll cost me to be quit of it.
For lack of a natural memory, I make one of paper; and whenever any new
symptom shows up in my disease, I write it down. So it falls out that,
having now run through almost every kind of example, if anything alarming
threatens me, I turn over these little loose notes like the Sibyl's
leaves, and never fail to find matter of comfort in some favorable
forecast from my past experience. Custom also makes me hope better of the
time to come: for the working of this clearing-out having gone on so
long, it's to be believed nature won't change her course, and no worse
accident will happen than what I already feel. And besides, this disease's
condition suits my quick, sudden temperament: when it attacks me gently
I'm afraid, for then it's in for a long stay; but naturally it has brisk,
vigorous fits—it claws me properly for a day or two. My kidneys held out
a lifetime without change; and I've now lived almost another lifetime
since they changed their state. Evils have their periods, like blessings:
maybe the infirmity is drawing toward its end. Age weakens the heat of my
stomach, and, its digestion being less perfect, sends this raw matter to
my kidneys. Why, at some turn, may not the heat of my kidneys abate too,
so they can no longer bake my phlegm into stone, and nature find some
other way of purging? Years have plainly helped me drain off certain
rheums; why not these excrements that supply matter for gravel? But is
there anything delightful to compare with this sudden change—when from
extreme pain, by voiding a stone, I recover, like a flash of lightning,
the beautiful light of health, so free and full, as happens in our sudden,
sharpest colics? Is there anything in the pain suffered that can balance
the pleasure of so sudden a mending? Oh, how much pleasanter health seems
to me after a sickness so near and so close that I can tell them apart
face to face, in their fullest show—when they stand against each other as
if to make head and dispute it! As the Stoics say vices are usefully
brought in to give value to virtue and set it off, we can with better
reason and less rash guessing say that nature has given us pain for the
honor and service of pleasure and ease. When Socrates, after his fetters
were knocked off, felt the pleasure of the itching the weight of them had
caused in his legs, he rejoiced to consider the close alliance between
pain and pleasure—how they're linked by a necessary tie, so that by turns
they follow and beget each other—and cried out to good Aesop that he
ought, out of this thought, to have made matter for a fine fable.

The worst I see in other diseases is that they're not so grievous in their
attack as they are in their going: a man spends a whole year recovering,
all the while full of weakness and fear. There's so much risk, and so many
steps to reach safety, that there's no end of it before they've unwrapped
you from a kerchief, and then from a cap, before they let you walk out and
take the air, drink wine, lie with your wife, eat melons—it's odds you
fall back into some new distemper. The stone has this privilege: it takes
itself clean off. Whereas the other maladies always leave some impression
and change behind that lays the body open to a new disease, and they lend
a hand to one another. Those are excusable that content themselves with
possessing us, without spreading further and bringing in their followers;
but courteous and kind are those whose passing brings us some profitable
issue. Since I've been troubled with the stone, I find myself freed from
all other accidents, much more, I think, than before, and I've never had a
fever since. I argue that the extreme, frequent vomitings I'm subject to
purge me; and, on the other hand, my distastes for this and that, and the
strange fasts I'm forced to keep, digest my bad humors, and nature, with
those stones, voids whatever's superfluous and harmful in me. Let them
never tell me it's a medicine bought too dear: for what good are so many
stinking draughts, so many caustics, incisions, sweats, setons, diets, and
so many other ways of cure which often—because we can't stand their
violence and insistence—bring us to our graves? So that when I have the
stone, I look on it as medicine; when I'm free of it, as an absolute
deliverance.

And here's another particular benefit of my disease: it almost plays its
game by itself, and lets me play mine, if I only have the courage to do
it. For in its greatest fury I've endured it ten hours together on
horseback. Just endure; you need no other regimen. Play, run, dine, do
this and that, if you can; your spree will do you more good than harm.
Say as much to a man with the pox, the gout, or a hernia! The other
diseases lay more universal obligations on us; they rack our actions in
another fashion, upset our whole order, and engage our whole state of life
in attending to them. This one only pinches the skin; it leaves
understanding and will wholly at our own disposal, and tongue, hands, and
feet. It wakes you up rather than stupefies you. The soul is struck down
by the heat of a fever, overwhelmed by an epileptic fit, thrown off its
seat by a sharp headache, and in short astounded by all the diseases that
hurt the whole mass and the noblest parts. This one never meddles with the
soul; if anything goes wrong with her, it's her own fault—she betrays,
unhorses, and abandons herself. Only fools let themselves be persuaded
that this hard, massive body baked in our kidneys can be dissolved by
drinks. So once it's on the move, there's nothing to do but give it
passage; and for that matter, it'll make one for itself.

I notice this particular convenience in it besides: that it's a disease
where we have little to guess at. We're spared the trouble other diseases
throw us into by the uncertainty of their causes, conditions, and
course—a trouble infinitely painful. We've no need of consultations and
doctoral interpretations; our senses tell us well enough both what it is
and where it is.

With such arguments, weak and strong—like Cicero with the disease of his
old age—I try to lull and amuse my imagination, and to dress its wounds.
If I find them worse tomorrow, I'll come up with new stratagems. That this
is true: I've lately come to such a pass that the least movement forces
pure blood out of my kidneys. What of it? I move about nonetheless as
before, and ride after my hounds with a youthful, reckless eagerness; and
I count it very good satisfaction for so weighty an accident when it costs
me nothing more than a dull heaviness and uneasiness in that part. It's
some great stone wasting and consuming the substance of my kidneys and my
life, which I evacuate little by little, not without some natural
pleasure, as an excrement henceforth superfluous and troublesome. Now if I
feel anything stirring, don't imagine I trouble myself to consult my
pulse or my urine, to set myself some irksome prevention; I shall feel the
pain soon enough, without making it more and longer by the disease of
fear. He who fears he will suffer already suffers what he fears. To which
may be added that the doubts and ignorance of those who take on themselves
to lay out nature's designs and her inward workings, and the many false
forecasts of their art, should make us understand that her ways are
inscrutable and utterly unknown; there's great uncertainty, variety, and
obscurity in what she either promises or threatens. Old age excepted,
which is an unmistakable sign of death's approach, in all other accidents
I see few signs of the future we can ground our divining on. I judge
myself only by what I actually feel, not by reasoning—to what end, since
I'm resolved to bring nothing to it but expectation and patience? Want to
know how much I gain by this? Look at those who do otherwise, who lean on
so many different persuasions and counsels—how often imagination presses
on them with no bodily pain at all. I've many times amused myself, when
well and safe and quite free from these dangerous attacks, by reporting
them to the physicians as then just beginning to show themselves in me; I
underwent the sentence of their dreadful conclusions, all the while quite
at my ease—and so much the more obliged to God's favor, and the better
satisfied of the vanity of this art.

There's nothing that ought so much to be pressed on youth as activity and
watchfulness: our life is nothing but movement. I bestir myself with great
difficulty, and I'm slow in everything—whether rising, going to bed, or
eating. Seven o'clock in the morning is early for me, and where I'm
master, I never dine before eleven, nor sup till after six. I used to lay
the fevers and other diseases I fell into to the heaviness long sleeping
brought on me, and I've always been sorry I went back to sleep in the
morning. Plato is angrier at too much sleeping than at too much drinking.
I like to lie hard and alone—even without my wife, as kings do—pretty
well covered with clothes. They never warm my bed; but since I've grown
old they give me, when needed, cloths to lay to my feet and stomach. They
found fault with the great Scipio for being a great sleeper—not, in my
opinion, for any other reason than that men were annoyed that he alone
should have nothing in him to find fault with. If I'm at all particular
in my way of living, it's rather in my bed than anything else; but in
general I give way and fit myself to necessity as well as anyone. Sleeping
has taken up a great part of my life, and even now, at my age, I still
sleep eight or nine hours at a stretch. I wean myself from this bent
toward sloth, to good purpose, and am plainly the better for it. I find
the change a little hard, indeed, but in three days it's over; and I see
few men who can do with less sleep when need calls, who take more constant
exercise, or to whom long journeys are less trouble. My body can stand a
steady strain, but not a violent or sudden one. Lately I've given up
violent exercises, and such as make me sweat: my limbs tire before they're
warm. I can stand a whole day together, and never tire of walking; but
from my youth I've always preferred to ride on paved roads. On foot I get
up to the haunches in dirt, and little fellow as I am I'm liable in the
streets to be elbowed and jostled for lack of presence. I've always loved
to rest myself, sitting or lying, with my heels as high as my seat or
higher.

There's no calling as pleasant as the soldier's—a calling noble in its
doing, for valor is the stoutest, proudest, and most generous of all
virtues, and noble in its cause: there's no usefulness either more
universal or more just than protecting the peace and greatness of one's
country. The company of so many noble, young, active men delights you; the
everyday sight of so many tragic scenes; the freedom of talk, without art;
a manly, unceremonious way of living, please you; the variety of a
thousand different actions; the stirring harmony of martial music that
ravishes and fires both ears and soul; the honor of this employment—even
its hardships and difficulties, which Plato holds so light that in his
Republic he makes women and children share them—are delightful to you. You
put yourself forward of your own will on particular bold deeds and risks,
according as you judge their splendor and importance; and as a volunteer,
find even life itself worthily spent:

> "Pulchrumque mori succurrit in armis."

["And he remembers that it is fine to die in arms."—Virgil, Aeneid,
ii. 317.]

To fear common dangers shared by so great a multitude of men; not to dare
do what so many kinds of souls, what a whole people dare—is for a heart
poor and mean beyond all measure. Company heartens even children. If
others outdo you in knowledge, in grace, in strength, or in fortune, you
have other resources to fall back on; but to give way to them in steadiness
of mind—you can blame no one for that but yourself. Death is more abject,
more pining and troublesome, in bed than in a fight: fevers and colds as
painful and mortal as a musket shot. Whoever has stoutly fortified himself
to bear the accidents of common life needn't screw his courage up to be a
soldier:

> "Vivere, mi Lucili, militare est."

["To live, my Lucilius, is to be a soldier."—Seneca, Epistle 96.]

I don't remember ever having the itch, and yet scratching is one of
nature's sweetest pleasures, and so close at hand; but repentance follows
too close behind. I use it most in my ears, which are apt to itch at
times.

I came into the world with all my senses whole, even to perfection. My
stomach is comfortably good, and so are my head and my breath; and for the
most part they hold up even at the height of fevers. I've passed the age
to which some nations, not without reason, have set so just a limit of
life that they wouldn't let men go beyond it; and yet I have respites,
though short and uncertain, so clean and sound as to be little short of
the health and pleasantness of my youth. I don't speak of vigor and
liveliness; it's not reasonable they should follow me past their limits:

> "Non hoc amplius est liminis, aut aquae
> Coelestis, patiens latus."

["My side can no longer stand waiting at a door in the rain."—Horace,
Odes, iii. 10, 9.]

My face and eyes soon show my condition; all my changes begin there, and
show somewhat worse than they really are. My friends often pity me before
I feel the cause in myself. My looking glass doesn't frighten me; for even
in my youth it's happened to me more than once to have a sickly, ill-
boding complexion with no great consequence, so that the physicians,
finding no inward cause to match that outward change, laid it to my mind
and some secret passion tormenting me within. But they were wrong. If my
body would govern itself as well by my rule as my mind does, we'd move a
little more at our ease. My mind was then not only free from trouble but
full of joy and contentment besides, as it commonly is—half by its temper,
half by its design:

> "Nec vitiant artus aegrae contagia mentis."

["Nor do the troubles of a sick mind infect the limbs."—Ovid, Tristia,
iii. 8, 25.]

I'm of the opinion that this temper of my soul has often lifted my body
out of its lapses. It's often cast down; if the other isn't brisk and gay,
it's at least calm and at rest. I had a quartan ague four or five months
that made me look wretchedly ill; my mind was always, if not calm, at
least cheerful. If the pain is outside me, weakness and languor don't much
afflict me. I see various bodily faintings that fill me with horror only
to name, which yet I'd fear less than a thousand passions and agitations
of mind I see around me. I've made up my mind to run no more; it's enough
that I can crawl along. Nor do I complain any more of the natural decay I
feel in myself:

> "Quis tumidum guttur miratur in Alpibus?"

["Who is surprised to see a swollen throat in the Alps?"—Juvenal,
xiii. 162.]

any more than I grieve that my life won't be as long and whole as an oak's.

I've no reason to complain of my imagination; I've had few thoughts in my
life that have even broken my sleep, except those of desire, which have
waked me without afflicting me. I seldom dream, and then of chimeras and
fantastic things, commonly born of pleasant thoughts, and more laughable
than sad; and I believe it's true that dreams are faithful interpreters
of our leanings. But it takes art to sort and understand them:

> "Res, quae in vita usurpant homines, cogitant, curant, vident,
> Quaeque agunt vigilantes, agitantque, ea si cui in somno accidunt,
> Minus mirandum est."

["It's the less to be wondered at if the things men practice, think of,
care for, see, and do awake—and which stir their feelings—happen to a man
in sleep."—Attius, quoted in Cicero, On Divination, i. 22.]

Plato says besides that it's prudence's office to draw lessons for
divining future things from dreams. I don't know about that, but there
are wonderful instances of it that Socrates, Xenophon, and Aristotle—men
of unquestionable authority—tell of. Historians say the Atlantes never
dream; they also, they say, never eat animal food—which I add because it's
perhaps the reason they never dream, for Pythagoras prescribed a certain
diet to produce fitting dreams. Mine are very gentle, with no tossing of
the body or crying out. I've seen several men of my time wonderfully upset
by theirs. Theon the philosopher walked in his sleep, and so did
Pericles's servant, and that on the tiles and the roof of the house.

I hardly ever pick my dish at table, but take the next at hand, and change
it for another unwillingly. A muddle of meats and a clatter of dishes
displeases me as much as any other muddle. I'm easily content with few
dishes, and I'm an enemy to Favorinus's opinion that in a

feast they should snatch away the meat you like and set a plate of another
sort before you—and that it's a poor supper if you don't stuff your guests
with the rumps of different fowls, the beccafico alone deserving to be
eaten whole. I usually eat salt meats, yet I prefer bread with no salt in
it; and my baker never sends any other to my table, contrary to the custom
of the country. In my childhood, what they had most to correct in me was
my refusing the things children commonly love best—sugar, sweetmeats, and
marchpane. My tutor fought this dislike of dainty things as a kind of
over-nicety; and indeed it's nothing but a difficulty of taste, in
whatever it fastens on. Whoever cures a child of an obstinate liking for
brown bread, bacon, or garlic cures him of pampering his palate too. There
are some who make a show of temperance and plainness by calling for beef
and ham in the middle of the partridges. It's all very fine: this is the
daintiness of the dainty; it's the taste of a spoiled fortune that turns
up its nose at ordinary, familiar things.

> "Per quae luxuria divitiarum taedio ludit."

["Things by which the luxury of wealth plays at weariness."—Seneca,
Epistle 18.]

Not to make good cheer over what another man is enjoying, and to be fussy
about what a man eats, is the essence of this vice:

> "Si modica coenare times olus omne patella."

["If you're afraid to sup on plain greens from a modest dish."—Horace,
Epistles, i. 5, 2.]

There's indeed this difference: that it's better to train your appetite to
the things easiest to come by. But it's always a vice to tie yourself
down. I once said a kinsman of mine was overnice who, from serving in our
galleys, had unlearned the use of beds and of undressing to sleep.

If I had any sons, I'd gladly wish them my fortune. The good father God
gave me—who has nothing of me but the acknowledgment of his goodness, but
truly it's a very hearty one—sent me from my cradle to be brought up in a
poor village of his, and kept me there all the time I was at nurse, and
longer still, raising me to the meanest, most common way of living:

> "Magna pars libertatis est bene moratus venter."

["A well-governed stomach is a great part of liberty."—Seneca,
Epistle 123.]

Never take on yourselves—and much less hand over to your wives—the care
of their upbringing; leave their shaping to fortune, under the common,
natural laws; leave it to custom to train them to frugality and hardship,
so they may rather come down from strictness than climb up to it. This
bent of his aimed at another end besides: to make me at home with the
people and the condition of men who most need our help—reckoning that I
should think more of those who stretch out their arms to me than of those
who turn their backs on me. And for this reason he had me held at the
christening font by people of the meanest fortune, to oblige and bind me
to them.

Nor has his design turned out altogether badly; for whether from the
greater honor in such condescension, or from a natural pity that has very
great power over me, I lean toward the humbler sort of people. The side
I'd condemn in our wars, I'd condemn more sharply when it's flourishing
and winning; it will partly win me over when I see it wretched and
crushed. How gladly I admire the fine spirit of Cheilonis, daughter and
wife of kings of Sparta. While her husband Cleombrotus, in the upheaval of
her city, had the upper hand over Leonidas her father, she, like a good
daughter, stuck close to her father in all his misery and exile, against
the conqueror. But as soon as the fortune of war turned, she changed her
will with the change of fortune, and bravely went over to her husband's
side, following him wherever his ruin carried him—admitting, as it seems
to me, no choice but to cling to the side that most needed her, and where
she could best show her compassion. I'm naturally readier to follow the
example of Flaminius, who gave his help rather to those who most needed
him than to those who could do him good, than that of Pyrrhus, whose bent
was to cringe to the great and lord it over the poor.

Long sittings at table both trouble me and do me harm; for, whether
because I was used to it as a child, I eat the whole time I sit. So at my
own house, though the meals there are among the shortest, I usually sit
down a little after the rest, after Augustus's manner—but I don't copy
him in rising before the rest too. On the contrary, I love to sit on a
long time after, and listen to the talk, provided I'm not one of the
talkers: for I tire and hurt myself talking on a full stomach, as much as
I find it very wholesome and pleasant to argue and strain my voice before
dinner.

The ancient Greeks and Romans had more reason than we have in setting
apart for eating—which is a principal business of life, if they weren't
kept off by some extraordinary affair—many hours, and the greatest part
of the night: eating and drinking more deliberately than we do, who do
everything posthaste; and in stretching this natural pleasure to more
leisure and better use, mixing it with profitable talk.

Those whose business it is to take care of me can very easily keep me
from eating anything they think will do me harm; for in such matters I
never crave or miss what I don't see. But at the same time, once it comes
in my sight, it's vain to persuade me to hold off. So when I plan to
fast, I must be kept away from the suppers, and have only so much given
me as a set light meal requires; for if I sit down to table, I forget my
resolve. When I tell my cook to change the way of dressing some dish, all
my household know what it means: that my stomach is out of order, and I
won't touch it.

I like all meats that can stand it to be very little boiled or roasted,
and I prefer them very high, and several of them quite gone. Nothing but
hardness generally offends me—of any other quality I'm as patient and
easy as any man I know—so that, contrary to the common taste, even with
fish it often happens I find them both too fresh and too firm. Not for
lack of teeth, which I always had good, even excellent, and which age
doesn't yet begin to threaten; I've always been used every morning to rub
them with a napkin, and before and after dinner. God is kind to those He
makes die by degrees; it's the one benefit of old age. The last death
will be that much less painful: it will kill only half a man, or a
quarter. One tooth lately fell out by itself, without drawing and without
pain; it was the natural end of its term. In that part of my being, and
several others, some are already dead, others half dead—of those that
were most active and in the front rank in my vigorous years. That's how I
melt and steal away from myself. What folly it would be in my
understanding to dread the height of this fall, already so far advanced,
as if it were from the very top! I hope I won't. In truth, I draw a chief
comfort from meditating on my death: that it will be just and natural,
and that henceforth I can neither ask nor hope from Destiny for anything
but an unlawful favor. Men persuade themselves that we used to have
longer lives as well as greater stature. But they fool themselves; and
Solon, who lived in those elder times, sets the span of life at seventy
years. I, who have so much and so everywhere worshipped that "The mean is
best" of the time past, and who have concluded the most moderate measures
to be the most perfect—shall I lay claim to an unmeasured, monstrous old
age? Whatever happens against the course of nature may be troublesome;
but whatever comes according to her should always be pleasant:

> "Omnia, quae secundum naturam fiunt, sunt habenda in bonis."

["All things done according to nature are to be counted
good."—Cicero, On Old Age, c. 19.]

And so, says Plato, the death that comes by wounds and diseases is
violent; but the one that comes on us with old age leading us to it is,
of all others, the easiest, and in some sort delicious:

> "Vitam adolescentibus vis aufert, senibus maturitas."

["Young men are carried off by violence, old men by ripeness."—Cicero,
ibid.]

Death mixes and mingles itself all through life; decay comes before its
hour, and shoulders its way even into the course of our advance. I have
portraits of myself taken at twenty-five and at thirty-five. I compare
them with the one lately drawn: how many times over is it no longer me!
How much more unlike is my present image to the former than to my dying
one! It's too much to abuse nature—to make her trot so far that she has
to be forced to leave us, and abandon our bearing, our eyes, teeth, legs,
and all the rest to the mercy of a strange, worn-out face, and hand us
over to art, being tired of following us herself.

I'm not very fond either of salads or of fruits, except melons. My father
hated all kinds of sauces; I love them all. Eating too much hurts me; but
as to the quality of what I eat, I don't yet know for certain that any
kind of meat disagrees with me; nor have I noticed that full moon or
waning, autumn or spring, has any effect on me. We have motions in us that
are changeable and unknown: for example, I first found radishes agreeable
to my stomach, then sickening, and now agreeable again. In several other
things I find my stomach and appetite change the same way; I've gone back
and forth again and again from white wine to claret, from claret to white.

I'm a great lover of fish, and so I make my fast days feasts and my feast
days fasts; and I believe what some people say, that fish is easier to
digest than flesh. As I make it a point of conscience not to eat flesh on
fish days, so my taste makes it a point of conscience not to mix fish and
flesh; the difference between them seems to me too wide.

From my youth I've sometimes stayed away from meals: either to sharpen my
appetite for the next morning—for as Epicurus fasted and ate lean meals to
get his pleasure used to doing without plenty, I, on the contrary, do it
to get my pleasure ready to make better and merrier use of plenty—or else
I fasted to keep my vigor fresh for the service of some action of body or
mind, for both the one and the other are cruelly dulled in me by
fullness. And above all I hate that silly pairing of so healthy and
lively a goddess with that little belching god, bloated with the fumes of
his liquor—[Montaigne did not approve of coupling Bacchus with
Venus]—or to settle my sick stomach, or for lack of fit company. For I
say, as Epicurus did, that you shouldn't so much consider what you eat as
whom you eat with; and I commend Chilo, who wouldn't promise to dine with
Periander till he'd first been told who the other guests were to be. No
dish is so welcome to me, no sauce so appetizing, as the one drawn from
company. I think it's more wholesome to eat more slowly and less, and to
eat more often; but I'd have appetite and hunger paid attention to. I'd
take no pleasure in being fed three or four measly, stinted meals a day,
in medicinal fashion. Who'll assure me that if I have a good appetite in
the morning I'll have the same at supper? But we old fellows especially—
let's seize the first fit time for eating, and leave hopes and forecasts
to the almanac makers. The utmost fruit of my health is pleasure; let's
lay hold of the present and known. I steer clear of fixed rules in these
laws of fasting; let whoever wants one pattern to serve him avoid keeping
to it. We harden ourselves in it; our strength is stupefied and put to
sleep there. Six months later you'll find your stomach so used to it that
all you've gained is the loss of your freedom to do otherwise except to
your hurt.

I never keep my legs and thighs warmer in winter than in summer; one
simple pair of silk stockings is all. I've let myself, to ease my colds,
keep my head warmer, and my belly on account of my colic; in a few days
my diseases got used to it and scorned my ordinary provisions. We soon go
from a nightcap to a kerchief over it, from a plain cap to a quilted hat;
the trimmings of the doublet mustn't just serve for show—there must be a
hare's skin or a vulture's skin added, and a cap under the hat. Follow
that ladder and you'll go a fine way to work. I'll do nothing of the
sort, and would gladly drop what I've started. If you fall into some new
trouble, all this is labor lost; you're used to it—find something else.
That's how they ruin themselves who let themselves be pestered with these
forced, superstitious rules: they must add something more, and something
more after that. There's no end to it.

For our business and pleasures, it's much more convenient, as the
ancients did, to skip dinner and put off making good cheer till the hour
of retiring and rest, without breaking up the day; and that's how I used
to do. As for health, I've since found by experience, on the contrary,
that it's better to dine, and that digestion goes better while awake. I'm
not much given to thirst, well or sick; my mouth is indeed apt to be dry,
but without thirst. And commonly I never drink except with the thirst
that eating brings on, well into the meal. I drink pretty well for a man
of my size: in summer, and at a savory meal, I not only go beyond
Augustus's limits, who drank exactly three times, but—so as not to offend
Democritus's rule, which forbade stopping at four as an unlucky
number—I go on at need to the fifth glass, about three half-pints. For
the little glasses are my favorites, and I like to drink them off, which
other people avoid as an unbecoming thing. I mix my wine sometimes with
half water, sometimes with a third; and when I'm at home, by an old custom
my father's physician prescribed for him and for himself, they mix what
I'm to have down in the buttery, two or three hours before it's brought
in. They say Cranabs, king of Attica, invented this custom of watering
wine; whether it's useful or not, I've heard disputed. I think it's more
decent and wholesome for children to drink no wine till after sixteen or
eighteen. The most usual, common way of living is the most becoming; all
singularity, in my opinion, is to be avoided. And I'd as soon hate a
German for mixing water with his wine as a Frenchman for drinking it
straight. Public custom gives the law in these things.

I'm afraid of mist, and run from smoke as from the plague. The first
repairs I made in my own house were to the chimneys and the privies, the
common, unbearable faults of all old buildings; and among the hardships
of war I count the choking dust they made us ride in a whole day
together. I breathe free and easy, and my colds for the most part go off
without troubling my lungs and without a cough.

Summer heat is more an enemy to me than winter cold; for besides the
discomfort of heat, less remediable than cold, and besides the force of
the sunbeams striking my head, all glittering light hurts my eyes, so
that I couldn't now sit at dinner facing a blazing fire.

To soften the whiteness of paper, back when I used to read more, I laid a
piece of glass on my book, and found my eyes much eased by it. To this
hour—at fifty-four—I'm still a stranger to spectacles; and I can see as
far as ever I did, or anyone else. True, in the evening I begin to feel a
little dimness and weakness in my sight if I read—an exercise I've always
found troublesome, especially at night. There's one step back, and a very
plain one; I'll take another: from the second to the third, and so to the
fourth, so gently that I'll be stone blind before I'm aware of the age
and decay of my sight. That's how artfully the Fatal Sisters unwind our
lives. And so I doubt whether my hearing is beginning to grow dull; and
you'll see I'll have half lost it while I'm still blaming the voices of
those who speak to me. A man must screw his soul up to a high pitch to
feel how it's ebbing away.

My walk is quick and firm; and I don't know which of the two, my mind or
my body, I have most trouble keeping in the same state. That preacher is
very much my friend who can hold my attention through a whole sermon. In
places of ceremony, where everyone's face is so starched, where I've seen
the ladies keep even their eyes so fixed, I could never manage it so that
some part or other of me didn't break loose; so that though I was seated,
I was never settled. And as for gesturing, I'm never without a switch in
my hand, walking or riding. As the philosopher Chrysippus's maid said of
her master, that he was drunk only in his legs, for it was his way to be
forever kicking them about wherever he sat—and she said it when the wine
had made all his companions drunk and he'd found no change in himself at
all—it might have been said of me from my childhood that I had either
folly or quicksilver in my feet, so much stirring and restlessness there
is in them, wherever they're placed.

It's unseemly—besides the harm it does my health, and even to the pleasure
of eating—to eat greedily as I do; I often bite my tongue, and sometimes
my fingers, in my haste. Diogenes, meeting a boy eating that way, boxed
his tutor's ear! There were men at Rome

that taught people to chew, as well as to walk, with a good grace. I lose
by it the chance of talking, which gives great relish to the table,
provided the talk is suitable—that is, pleasant and short.

There's jealousy and envy among our pleasures; they cross and hinder one
another. Alcibiades, a man who well understood how to make good cheer,
banished even music from the table, so it wouldn't disturb the
entertainment of talk—for the reason, as Plato tells us, "that it's the
custom of ordinary people to call in fiddlers and singers to feasts, for
lack of good talk and pleasant conversation, with which men of
understanding know how to entertain one another." Varro asks all this in
entertainments: "people of graceful presence and agreeable talk, neither
silent nor chattering; neatness and daintiness, both of meat and place;
and fair weather." The art of dining well is no slight art, the pleasure
no slight pleasure; neither the greatest captains nor the greatest
philosophers have scorned the use or knowledge of eating well. My
imagination has handed three repasts over to my memory's keeping, which
fortune made sovereignly sweet to me on several occasions in my more
flourishing age; my present state shuts me out—for everyone, according to
the good temper of body and mind he's then in, supplies for his own share
a particular grace and savor. I, who only crawl on the earth, hate this
inhuman wisdom that would have us despise and hate all care of the body;
I count it as great an injustice to loathe natural pleasures as to be too
much in love with them. Xerxes was a blockhead who, ringed with every
human delight, offered a reward to whoever could find him new ones; but
he's not much less one who cuts off any of the pleasures nature has
provided for him. A man should neither chase pleasures nor run from them,
but receive them. I receive them, I confess, a little too warmly and
kindly, and easily let myself follow my natural leanings. We've no need to
exaggerate their emptiness; they'll make us feel it well enough
themselves, thanks to our sick, wet-blanket mind, which puts us out of
taste with them as with itself. It treats both itself and all it takes in,
now better, now worse, according to its insatiable, roving, changeable
essence:

> "Sincerum est nisi vas, quodcunque infundis, acescit."

["Unless the vessel is clean, whatever you pour into it turns
sour."—Horace, Epistles, i. 2, 54.]

I, who boast that I so carefully and particularly embrace the comforts of
life, find them, when I look at them closest, very little more than wind.
But what of it? We're all wind through and through; and what's more, the
wind itself, more discreet than we, loves to bluster and shift from corner
to corner, and contents itself with its proper business without wanting
steadiness and solidity—qualities not its own.

The pure pleasures, like the pure pains, of the imagination are the
greatest, say some—as was expressed by the balance of Critolaiis. No wonder: it
makes them to its own liking, and cuts them out of whole cloth. Of this I
see notable examples every day, and perhaps to be wished for. But I, who
am of a mixed and heavy make, can't pounce so quickly on this one simple
object; instead I carelessly let myself be carried along with the present
pleasures of the general human law—intellectually sensible, and sensibly
intellectual. The Cyrenaic philosophers hold that bodily pains, like
bodily pleasures, are more powerful—both as being double and as being more
just. There are some, as Aristotle says, who from a savage kind of
stupidity dislike them; and I know others who do the same from ambition.
Why don't they forswear breathing too? Why don't they live off
themselves? Why not refuse light, because it's free and costs them neither
contriving nor labor? Let Mars, Pallas, or Mercury give them light to see
by, instead of Venus, Ceres, and Bacchus. These boastful humors may fake
some contentment—for what won't fancy do? But as for wisdom, there's no
touch of it. Won't these men seek the squaring of the circle even when
they're with their wives? I hate being told to keep my mind in the clouds
when my body's at table; I wouldn't have the mind nailed there, nor
wallowing there. I'd have it take its place there and sit—but not lie
down. Aristippus stood up for nothing but the body, as if we had no soul;
Zeno took in only the soul, as if we had no body: both were wrong.
Pythagoras, they say, followed a philosophy all of contemplation, Socrates
one all of conduct and action; Plato found a middle way between the two.
But they say this just for the sake of talking. The true temperament is
found in Socrates; and Plato is much more Socratic than Pythagorean, and
it suits him better. When I dance, I dance; when I sleep, I sleep. Even
when I walk alone in a beautiful orchard, if my thoughts are some of the
time taken up with outward things, some of the time I call them back
again—to my walk, to the orchard, to the sweetness of that solitude, and
to myself.

Nature has mother-like seen to it that the actions she's laid on us from
necessity should be pleasurable to us too; and she invites us to them not
only by reason but by appetite as well. It's unjust to break her laws.
When I see Caesar—and Alexander too—in the middle of his greatest
business, so fully enjoying human, bodily pleasures, I don't say he let
his mind go slack: I say he braced it, by vigor of courage bringing those
violent employments and laborious thoughts under the ordinary usage of
life. Wise, had he believed the last was his ordinary calling, the first
his extraordinary one. We're great fools. "He's spent his life in
idleness," we say. "I've done nothing today." What? Haven't you lived?
That's not only the fundamental but the most glorious of your callings.
"Had I been set to manage great affairs, I'd have shown what I could do."
"Have you known how to think through and manage your life? You've done
the greatest work of all." To show and unfold herself, nature needs only
fortune; she shows herself equally at every stage, behind a curtain as
well as before one. Have you known how to order your conduct? You've done
a great deal more than the man who's written books. Have you known how to
take your rest? You've done more than the man who's taken empires and
cities.

The glorious masterpiece of man is to live to the purpose; all other
things—to reign, to heap up treasure, to build—are but little appendages
and props. I love to see a general, at the foot of a breach he's about to
storm, give himself up wholly and freely at dinner to talking and making
merry with his friends. And Brutus, when heaven and earth were leagued
against him and Roman liberty, stealing some hour of the night from his
rounds to read and pore over Polybius in all security. It's for little
souls, buried under the weight of business, never to know how to get
clear of it—never to know how to lay it down and take it up again:

> "O fortes, pejoraque passi
> Mecum saepe viri! nunc vino pellite curas
> Cras ingens iterabimus aequor."

["O brave hearts, who have often suffered worse with me! Now drive away
care with wine; tomorrow we sail again over the vast sea."—Horace, Odes,
i. 7, 30.]

Whether in joke or in earnest that the theologians' and Sorbonne men's
wine and their feasts have become a proverb, I find it reasonable they
should dine all the more comfortably and pleasantly, as they've spent the
morning profitably and seriously in the exercises of their schools. The
consciousness of having well spent the other hours is the just and savory
sauce of the dinner table. The sages lived that way; and that inimitable
striving after virtue, which amazes us in the one Cato and the other—that
humor of theirs, so strict as to be almost tiresome—gently gives way and
yields to the laws of the human condition, of Venus and Bacchus,
according to the precepts of their school, which require the perfect sage
to be as skilled and knowing in the use of natural pleasures as in all
the other duties of life:

> "Cui cor sapiat, ei et sapiat palatus."

["Let him whose heart is wise have a wise palate too."]

Ease and relaxation, I think, wonderfully honor and become a strong,
generous soul. Epaminondas didn't think that joining in heartily with the
young men of his city in songs and sports and dances took anything from
the honor of his glorious victories and the perfect purity of his conduct.
And among so many admirable deeds of Scipio the elder—a man worthy to be
thought of heavenly stock—there's nothing that gives him a greater grace
than to see him carelessly and childishly pottering about gathering and
picking cockle shells, and playing at quoits,

> [This game, as the Dictionnaire de Trévoux describes it, is one in which
> two people vie over which of them will soonest pick up some object.]

amusing and tickling himself by writing comedies that act out the
meanest, most popular doings of men. And with his head full of that
wonderful enterprise against Hannibal and Africa, visiting the schools in
Sicily and sitting through philosophy lectures—to the point of arming the
blind envy of his enemies at Rome. Nor is there anything more remarkable
in Socrates than that, old as he was, he found time to have himself taught
dancing and the lute, and thought it time well spent. This same man was
seen in a trance, standing on his feet a whole day and night together,
before the whole Greek army, startled and swallowed up by some profound
thought. He was the first, among so many brave men of the army, to run to
the rescue of Alcibiades, hard pressed by the enemy—to shield him with his
own body and drag him clear of the throng by sheer force of arms. It was
he who, in the battle at Delium, picked up Xenophon when he'd been thrown
from his horse and saved him; and who, among all the people of Athens,
enraged as they were at so shameful a sight, was the first to step forward
to rescue Theramenes, whom the thirty tyrants were leading off to
execution with their guards—and didn't give up his bold attempt till
Theramenes himself begged him to, though only two others followed him in
all. He was seen, when courted by a beauty he was in love with, to keep
up a strict abstinence when need was. He was seen always going to the wars
and walking on ice with bare feet; wearing the same cloak winter and
summer; outdoing all his comrades in bearing hardships patiently; and
eating no more at a feast than at his own private dinner. He was seen, for
twenty-seven years together, putting up with hunger, poverty, his
children's unruliness, and his wife's claws, with the same face. And in
the end, slander, tyranny, prison, chains, and poison. But was this man
bound by any rule of good manners to drink full bumpers? He was also the
man in the whole army who came off best in drinking. And he never refused
to play at cobnuts, nor to ride the hobbyhorse with children—and it suited
him well. For all actions, says philosophy, equally become and equally
honor a wise man. We've material enough for it, and we should never tire
of holding up the image of this great man in every pattern and shape of
perfection. There are very few examples of a full, pure life; and every
day we wrong our teaching by setting before ourselves those that are weak
and flawed—hardly good for any one service, and pulling us back
rather—corrupters of conduct rather than correctors. People fool
themselves: a man goes much more easily, indeed, by the extremes, where
the far end serves for a bound, a stop, and a guide, than by the middle
way, broad and open—and according to art more than according to
nature—but at the same time much less nobly and commendably.

Greatness of soul lies not so much in climbing and pressing forward as in
knowing how to govern and bound itself; it counts everything great that is
enough, and shows itself in preferring moderate things to eminent ones.
There's nothing so fine and right as to play the man well and duly; nor
any science so hard as to know well and naturally how to live this life;
and of all the infirmities we have, the most barbarous is to despise our
own being.

Whoever wants to cut his spirit off, when his body's ill at ease, to keep
it from the infection—let him do it by all means, if he can. But
otherwise, let him on the contrary favor and help his body, and not refuse
to share its natural pleasures with wedded contentment—bringing to them,
if he's the wiser, moderation, lest by indiscretion they get mixed up with
displeasure. Excess is the plague of pleasure; and temperance isn't its
scourge, but its seasoning. Euxodus, who set up pleasure as the sovereign
good, and his companions, who set so high a value on it, tasted it in its
most charming sweetness by means of temperance—which in them was singular
and exemplary.

I bid my soul look on pain and pleasure with an eye equally steady:

> "Eodem enim vitio est effusio animi in laetitia
> quo in dolore contractio,"

["For the mind's overflowing in joy comes from the same fault as its
shrinking in sorrow."—Cicero, Tusculan Disputations, iv. 31.]

and equally firm—but gaily on the one, severely on the other—and, as far
as it can, to be as careful to quench the one as to feed the other.
Judging rightly of good brings with it judging soundly of evil: pain has
something of the unavoidable in its tender beginnings, and pleasure
something of the avoidable in its excessive end. Plato yokes them
together, and holds it to be equally fortitude's business to fight against
pain and against the immoderate, charming coaxings of pleasure. They are
two fountains; whoever draws from them, when and as much as he needs—be
he city, man, or beast—is very fortunate. The first is to be taken as
medicine, and on necessity, and more sparingly; the other to quench
thirst, but not to drunkenness. Pain, pleasure, love, and hatred are the
first things a child feels: if, when reason comes, they apply reason to
these, that is virtue.

I have a vocabulary of my own: I "pass away time" when it's bad and
uneasy; but when it's good I don't pass it away—I "taste it over again
and cling to it." We should run over the bad and settle on the good. This
common phrase—pastime, passing away the time—shows the way of those wise
folk who think they can't do better with their lives than let them run
out and slip away, pass them over and balk them, and, as much as they can,
ignore and shun them as something troublesome and contemptible. But I know
it for another kind of thing, and find it both precious and serviceable,
even in its latest decay, in which I now enjoy it; and nature has put it
in our hands in such favorable circumstances that we have only ourselves
to blame if it's troublesome to us or slips away unprofitably:

> "Stulti vita ingrata est, trepida est, tota in futurum fertur."

["The fool's life is thankless, fearful, wholly bent on the
future."—Seneca, Epistle 15.]

Still, I make myself ready to lose mine without regret—but as a thing
perishable by its condition, not as one that troubles or annoys me. Nor
does it properly become anyone not to mind dying, except those who are
glad to live. There's good husbandry in enjoying life: I enjoy it doubly
what others do, for the measure of its enjoyment depends on our greater or
lesser application to it. Chiefly, since I feel mine to be so short in
time, I want to stretch it out in weight; I'll check the swiftness of its
flight by the swiftness of my grasp, and make up by vigorous use for the
speed of its running away. In proportion as my possession of life is
shorter, I must make it so much the deeper and fuller.

Others feel the pleasure of contentment and prosperity; I feel it too, as
much as they—but not as it passes and slips by. We should study it,
taste it, and chew it over, to give fit thanks to Him who grants it to
us. They enjoy the other pleasures the way they do sleep—without knowing
it. So that even sleep itself shouldn't slip away from me so stupidly, I
once had myself woken in my sleep, so I might relish and taste it better
and more sensibly. I ponder over contentment; I don't skim it, I sound
it; and I bend my reason, now grown wayward and peevish, to entertain it.
Do I find myself in some calm, settled state? Is there some pleasure
tickling me? I don't let it trifle with my senses only; I bring my soul
into it too—not to get entangled, but to take delight; not to lose
itself, but to be present. And I set it, for its part, to look at itself
in this prosperous state, to weigh and prize its happiness and swell it.
It counts up how much it owes to God: that its conscience and inward
passions are at rest; that it has its body in its natural state, orderly
and duly enjoying the soft, soothing functions by which He, of His grace,
is pleased to make up to us the sufferings His justice at His pleasure
lays on us. It reflects what a great blessing it is to be so sheltered
that whichever way it turns its eyes the heavens are calm around it. No
desire, no fear, no doubt troubles the air; no difficulty, past, present,
or to come, that its imagination can't pass over without offense. This
thought takes on great brightness from comparing different conditions. So
I set before my mind, in a thousand lights, those whom fortune or their
own fault carries off and torments. And then again those who, more like
me, take their good fortune so carelessly and incuriously. Those are
people who truly spend their time; they skip over the present and what
they have, to wait on hope—on shadows and vain images that fancy sets
before them:

> "Morte obita quales fama est volitare figuras,
> Aut quae sopitos deludunt somnia sensus:"

["Such shapes as are said to hover about after death, or dreams that
mock the sleeping senses."—Virgil, Aeneid, x. 641.]

which quicken and stretch out their flight the more they're chased. The
fruit and end of their chase is to chase—as Alexander said, the end of
his labor was to labor:

> "Nil actum credens, cum quid superesset agendum."

["Thinking nothing done while anything remained to do."—Lucan,
ii. 657.]

For my part, then, I love life and tend it, such as it has pleased God
to give it to us. I don't want it without the necessity of eating and
drinking; and I'd call it no less pardonable a fault to wish it had been
twice as long:

> "Sapiens divitiarum naturalium quaesitor acerrimus:"

["The wise man is the keenest seeker after nature's riches."—Seneca,
Epistle 119.]

Nor that we should keep ourselves alive by putting into our mouths just
a little of that drug with which Epimenides took away his appetite and
kept himself going; nor that we should stupidly beget children with our
fingers or our heels—but rather, with reverence be it spoken, that we
might delightfully beget them with our fingers and heels; nor that the
body should be without desire and without thrill. These are ungrateful,
wicked complaints. I accept, kindly and gratefully, what nature has done
for me; I'm well pleased with it, and proud of it. A man wrongs that
great, all-powerful Giver when he refuses, cancels, or disfigures his
gift: all goodness himself, he has made everything good:

> "Omnia quae secundum naturam sunt, aestimatione digna sunt."

["All things that are according to nature are worthy of esteem."—Cicero,
On Ends, iii. 6.]

Of philosophical opinions, I take most to the solidest—that is to say,
the most human and most our own. My talk is, like my conduct, low and
humble. Philosophy plays the child, to my thinking, when it gets up on
its "therefores" to preach to us that it's a barbarous match to wed the
divine to the earthly, the reasonable to the unreasonable, the stern to
the indulgent, the honorable to the dishonorable. That pleasure is a
beastly quality, unworthy to be tasted by a wise man; that the only
pleasure he gets from enjoying a fair young wife is the pleasure his
conscience takes in doing an act according to order—like putting on his
boots for a profitable journey. Oh, if its followers had no more right,
nerves, or vigor in taking their wives' maidenheads than they show in
their lesson!

This isn't what Socrates says, who is philosophy's master and ours: he
values bodily pleasure as he should; but he puts the mind's first, as
having more strength, steadiness, ease, variety, and dignity. This one,
according to him, by no means goes alone—he's not so fanciful—but only
it goes first. Temperance with him is pleasure's moderator, not its
enemy. Nature is a gentle guide—but no more sweet and gentle than prudent
and just.

> "Intrandum est in rerum naturam, et penitus,
> quid ea postulet, pervidendum."

["We must search into the nature of things, and look deep into what she
asks of us."—Cicero, On Ends, v. 16.]

I hunt for her footprint everywhere: we've muddled it with artificial
tracks. And that Academic and Peripatetic good, which is "to live
according to nature," gets hard on that account to bound and explain—as
does the Stoics', next door to it, which is "to consent to nature." Isn't
it a mistake to count any actions less worthy because they're necessary?
And yet they won't talk me out of it: that it's a very happy marriage of
pleasure with necessity, with which, says an ancient, the gods always
conspire. To what end do we tear apart by divorce a building joined by so
close and brotherly a fellowship? Let's rather strengthen it by mutual
services: let the mind rouse and quicken the heaviness of the body, and
the body check and steady the lightness of the soul:

> "Qui, velut summum bonum, laudat animac naturam, et, tanquam malum,
> naturam carnis accusat, profecto et animam carnaliter appetit, et
> carnem carnaliter fugit; quoniam id vanitate sentit humana, non
> veritate divina."

["He who praises the soul's nature as the supreme good, and blames the
flesh's nature as evil, at once lusts after the soul carnally and flees
the flesh carnally—since he feels this from human vanity, not from divine
truth."—St. Augustine, City of God, xiv. 5.]

In this present God has made us, there's nothing unworthy of our care; we
stand accountable for it down to a hair. And isn't it man's commission to
conduct man according to his condition? It's express, plain, and the very
first of all—and the Creator has laid it on us seriously and strictly.
Authority carries weight only in matters of common judgment, and counts
for more in a foreign tongue; so let's charge at it once more in this
place:

> "Stultitiae proprium quis non dixerit, ignave et contumaciter
> facere, quae facienda sunt; et alio corpus impellere, alio animum;
> distrahique inter diversissimos motus?"

["Who wouldn't say it's folly's mark to do what must be done lazily and
stubbornly—to push the body one way and the mind another, and be torn
between wholly different motions?"—Seneca, Epistle 74.]

To make this plain, ask anyone some day to tell you what whims and fancies
he's been turning over in his head, on account of which he's drawn his
thoughts away from a good meal, and grudges the time he spends eating.
You'll find there's nothing on all the dishes at your table so tasteless
as this wise meditation of his—for the most part we'd do better to sleep
than to wake to the purpose we wake for—and that his discourses and
notions aren't worth the worst dish there. Though they were the raptures
of Archimedes himself—what then? I don't here speak of, or mix with the
rabble of us ordinary men and the vanity of the thoughts and desires that
distract us, those venerable souls lifted by the fire of devotion and
religion to a constant, conscientious meditation on divine things—who, by
the force of a lively, burning hope, tasting beforehand the eternal food,
the final aim and last step of Christian desires, the one constant,
incorruptible pleasure, scorn to give themselves to our needy, fleeting,
doubtful comforts, and easily leave to the body the care and use of
sensual, temporal fodder. That's a privileged study. Between ourselves,
I've always found lofty opinions and low conduct to be remarkably good
company.

Aesop, that great man, saw his master make water as he walked. "What
then," said he, "must we drop it as we run?" Let's manage our time; there's
still a great deal of it lying idle and ill spent. The mind hasn't other
hours enough, willingly, to do its business in, without cutting itself
loose from the body in the little space it needs for its necessity. They
want to get out of themselves and escape from being men. It's folly:
instead of turning themselves into angels, they turn themselves into
beasts; instead of lifting themselves, they lay themselves low. These
high-flown humors frighten me, like high, unreachable places; and nothing
is hard for me to take in the life of Socrates except his trances and his
dealings with spirits; nothing is so human in Plato as the thing they say
got him called divine; and of our sciences, the ones that fly highest seem
to me the most earthbound and low; and I find nothing so humble and mortal
in Alexander's life as his fancies about his own immortalizing. Philotas
neatly teased him in his answer: he congratulated him by letter on the
oracle of Jupiter Ammon, which had set him among the gods. "On your
account I'm glad of it," he wrote, "but the men are to be pitied who have
to live with a man, and obey him, who goes beyond and won't be content
with the measure of a man":

> "Diis to minorem quod geris, imperas."

["Because you carry yourself lower than the gods, you rule."—Horace,
Odes, iii. 6, 5.]

The pretty inscription with which the Athenians honored Pompey's entry
into their city matches my feeling: "By so much as you own yourself a
man, you are a god." It's an absolute, and as it were divine, perfection
for a man to know how to enjoy his being faithfully. We chase after other
conditions because we don't understand the use of our own; and we go out
of ourselves because we don't know how to live there. It's little use
going on stilts, for on stilts we must still walk with our legs; and when
seated on the highest throne in the world, we're still seated on our
backsides. The finest lives, in my opinion, are those that fit themselves
regularly to the common, human pattern—without miracle, without
extravagance. Old age stands in a little need of gentler handling. Let's
commend that to God, the protector of health and wisdom—but let it be gay
and sociable:

> "Frui paratis et valido mihi
> Latoe, dones, et precor, integra
> Cum mente; nec turpem senectam
> Degere, nec Cithara carentem."

["Grant me, Apollo, to enjoy what I have in good health; and sound, I
pray, in mind; to spend no shameful old age, nor one without the
lyre."—Horace, Odes, i. 31, 17.]

Or:

> ["Grant me, Apollo, to enjoy what I have in good health; let me be sound
> in body and mind; let me live honorably in old age, and let music not be
> wanting."]
