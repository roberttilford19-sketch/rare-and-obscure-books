## Dedication

To **François de La Rochefoucauld (1613–1680)** — French duke, Fronde rebel,
soldier who watched the aristocratic world he was born into tear itself apart,
then spent his salon years compressing what he had learned about human vanity
into sentences sharp enough to cut.

He drew his maxims from the life he had lived among the great, not from books
alone; the fault he described, he insisted, was in mankind.

## Method statement

This is a **draft** — a plain-English rendering, not a finished book.

- **Base.** Every maxim here was rendered from the public-domain English
  translation by **J. W. Willis Bund, M.A. LL.B., and J. Hain Friswell**,
  *Reflections; or, Sentences and Moral Maxims* — imprint **Simpson Low, Son,

  notes, and some account of the author and his times." The imprint and year
  were verified against the archived scan of this same translation on
  archive.org (identifier `reflectionsorsen00laro`, 1898 reprint by
  Sampson Low, Marston & Co., whose title page reads "Translated from the
  Editions of 1678 and 1827"), cross-checked with Project Gutenberg eBook
  #9105, which transcribes the 1871 title page in full.
- **French source.** The French text behind the base is the **1678 5th
  edition** — the last edition the author saw through the press, holding
  **504 maxims**.
- **Numbering.** Maxim numbers 1–252 in this file follow the **1678 edition's
  standard numbering**, carried by the base translation. Maxim numbering varies
  between editions: the 1665 1st edition carried fewer maxims in a different
  order, and later editors have shuffled them. Where numbering differs, it is
  noted — nothing here has been silently renumbered. (The base also prints, as
  a separate supplement, maxims suppressed from the 1665/1666 editions,
  numbered I–CXXV in Roman numerals, and prose "Reflections on Various
  Subjects"; both are outside this file's scope.)
- **What was done.** Each maxim was rewritten in plain, modern English —
  clear enough for a humble peasant to read — keeping it as short and sharp
  as the original. No "thee," "thou," or "thy." The translators' Victorian
  footnotes, parallel quotations, and commentary were not carried over; only
  the maxims themselves are rendered. Nothing was invented: every sentence
  traces to the base text, with the French-checked exceptions noted in the
  inline marks and the spot-check note below.

- **French spot-check.** The base is Victorian and occasionally misses the 1678
  French (e.g., rendering *l'intérêt* as "self," *l'ennui* as "indifference,"
  *la vertu des femmes* as "women chaste"). Fifteen maxims in this file were
  corrected to follow the 1678 French, each marked with an inline note naming
  the base's reading and the French reading. The 1678 wording was verified
  against the archive.org scan of the 5th edition (identifier
  `bub_gb_R9brb0SKTbgC`, Claude Barbin, Paris, 1678), checked maxim by maxim
  against its OCR. Where Bund/Friswell and the 1678 French agree, the base was
  kept. Numbers themselves were also re-checked against the 1678 sequence; no
  renumbering was needed in the 1–252 range, and no transcriber digit-typos
  (like the "4ll." seen at 411 in the companion's lane) occur here.
- **What was not done.** This draft makes no claim of being the first,
  oldest, definitive, or complete English rendering. Many translations of
  these maxims exist, and this is simply one more plain-English pass for the
  shelf. It stops at draft; Robert Tilford's G4 read-through gate has not been
  crossed, and nothing has been published, emailed, uploaded, or pushed.

# Reflections; or, Sentences and Moral Maxims

**François, Duc de La Rochefoucauld (1613–1680)**

**Maxims 1–252, in clear modern English — DRAFT**

*Numbering follows the 1678 5th edition (504 maxims in all), via the base translation.*

---

**1.** What we call virtue is often just a mix of different deeds and different interests, arranged by luck or by our own effort; and it is not always courage that makes men brave, or chastity that makes women chaste.

**2.** Self-love is the greatest of flatterers.

**3.** Whatever has been discovered in the country of self-love, there are still many lands there that no one has explored.

**4.** Self-love is more cunning than the most cunning man in the world.

**5.** How long our passions last is no more in our power than how long our lives last.

**6.** Passion often turns the cleverest man into a fool, and sometimes turns the biggest fool into a clever man.

**7.** Great, dazzling deeds are painted by politicians as the result of great plans, when they are usually caused by mood and passion. The war between Augustus and Antony, put down to their ambition to rule the world, was probably just jealousy.

**8.** The passions are the only advocates that always persuade. They are a natural art with rules that never fail; the plainest man with passion will persuade better than the most eloquent man without it.

**9.** The passions have a kind of unfairness and self-interest in them that makes it dangerous to follow them; we should distrust them even when they look most trustworthy.

**10.** In the human heart there is a constant making of new passions; the ruin of one is almost always the foundation of another.

**11.** Passions often produce their opposites: miserliness sometimes leads to wastefulness, and wastefulness to miserliness; we are often stubborn from weakness and bold from fear.

**12.** However carefully we hide our passions behind looks of piety and honor, they can always be seen through those veils.

**13.** Our self-love bears the condemnation of our tastes more impatiently than the condemnation of our opinions.

**14.** Men not only tend to forget favors and injuries; they even hate those who have done them a favor, and stop hating those who have injured them. Having to repay a favor or take revenge feels like a slavery they refuse to bear.

**15.** The mercy of princes is often just policy to win the people's love.

**16.** This mercy they make a virtue of often comes from vanity, sometimes from laziness, often from fear, and almost always from all three together.

**17.** The moderation of happy people comes from the calm that good fortune gives their temper.

**18.** Moderation comes from fear of stirring up the envy and contempt deserved by those drunk on good fortune; it is a vain show of strength of mind, and in short, the moderation of men at their highest is only a wish to seem greater than their fortune.

**19.** We all have strength enough to bear the misfortunes of others.

**20.** The steadiness of the wise is only the skill of hiding the turmoil in their hearts.

**21.** Those condemned to death sometimes put on a steadiness and contempt of death that is only fear of facing it; you could say this steadiness and contempt are to their minds what the blindfold is to their eyes.

**22.** Philosophy easily masters evils past and evils to come; but present evils master it.

**23.** Few people know death; we only endure it, usually out of determination, and even out of stupidity and habit; and most men only die because they do not know how to stop themselves dying.

**24.** When great men let themselves be crushed by long misfortune, they show us they were held up only by ambition, not by strength of mind; so that, vanity aside, heroes are made like other men.

**25.** It takes greater virtues to bear good fortune than bad.

**26.** Neither the sun nor death can be stared at without blinking.

**27.** People are often proud of their passions, even the worst of them; but envy is a passion so timid and shamefaced that no one ever dares admit to it.

**28.** Jealousy is in a way just and reasonable, since it aims to keep a good that is ours, or that we believe is ours; envy, on the other hand, is a rage that cannot bear the happiness of others.

**29.** The harm we do does not draw as much persecution and hatred on us as our good qualities do.

**30.** We have more strength than will; and it is often only as an excuse that we say things are impossible.

**31.** If we had no faults, we would not take such pleasure in pointing out those of others.

**32.** Jealousy feeds on doubt; the moment doubt turns to certainty, it ends or turns to rage.

**33.** Pride loses nothing even when it throws vanity away; it always pays itself back.

**34.** If we had no pride ourselves, we would not complain of the pride of others.

**35.** Pride is much the same in all men; the only difference is the manner of showing it.

**36.** It seems that nature, which so wisely fitted the organs of our body for our happiness, also gave us pride to spare us the pain of knowing our faults.

**37.** Pride has a bigger share than kindness in our lectures to those who commit faults; we correct them not so much to mend them as to convince them that we ourselves are free of faults.

**38.** We promise according to our hopes; we perform according to our fears.

**39.** Self-interest speaks every language and plays every part, even the part of being unselfish.

**40.** Self-interest blinds some and opens the eyes of others.

**41.** Those who busy themselves too much with little things often become incapable of great ones.

**42.** We do not have strength enough to follow all our reason.

**43.** A man often thinks himself the leader when he is being led; while his mind aims at one goal, his heart quietly drags him toward another.

**44.** Strength and weakness of mind are wrongly named; they are really only the good or lucky arrangement of our bodily organs.

**45.** The whims of our temper are even more capricious than those of fortune.

**46.** The attachment or indifference that philosophers have shown toward life is only the style of their self-love, which is no more open to argument than a taste in food or in colors.

**47.** Our temper sets the price on every gift we receive from fortune.

**48.** Happiness lies in the taste, not in the things; we are happy from having what we like, not from having what others like.

**49.** We are never as happy or as unhappy as we imagine.

**50.** Those who think they have merit convince themselves they are honored by being unhappy, so as to persuade others and themselves that they are worthy targets of fortune.

**51.** Nothing should take away our satisfaction with ourselves so much as seeing that we disapprove at one time of what we approved at another.

**52.** However different our fortunes look, there is a certain balancing of good and evil that makes them equal.

**53.** Whatever great gifts nature gives, it is not she alone but fortune also that makes the hero.

**54.** The contempt of riches among philosophers was only a hidden desire to get even with fortune for the injustice done to their merit, by despising the very goods fortune had denied them; it was a way to protect themselves from the disgrace of poverty, and a back road to the distinction they could not win by riches.

**55.** Hatred of favorites is only love of favor. Envy at not having it consoles itself and softens its regret by despising those who have it; we refuse them our homage because we cannot take away from them what draws the homage of everyone else.

**56.** To get established in the world, we do everything to look as if we were established.

**57.** Although men pride themselves on their great deeds, those deeds come less often from a great plan than from chance.

**58.** It seems our actions have lucky or unlucky stars, to which they owe much of the blame or praise given them.

**59.** There are no misfortunes so bad that clever men cannot draw some advantage from them, nor good luck so good that fools cannot turn it to their harm.

**60.** Fortune turns everything to the advantage of those she smiles on.

**61.** The happiness or unhappiness of men depends as much on their dispositions as on their fortunes.

**62.** Sincerity is openness of heart; it is found in very few people, and what we usually see is only a clever pretense to win the confidence of others.

**63.** The dislike of lying is often a hidden ambition to make our words carry weight, and to give our conversation an air of religion.

**64.** Truth does not do as much good in the world as its counterfeits do harm.

**65.** There is no praise we have not heaped on prudence, and yet she cannot guarantee us even the smallest event.

**66.** A clever man should manage his interests so that each comes in its turn. Our greed so often troubles us, making us chase so many things at once, that while we eagerly chase the least we miss the greatest.

**67.** What grace is to the body, good sense is to the mind.

**68.** Love is hard to define; all we can say is that in the soul it is a desire to rule, in the mind a sympathy, and in the body a hidden, delicate wish to possess what we love — plus many mysteries.

**69.** If there is a pure love, free of mixture with our other passions, it is the one hidden at the bottom of the heart, of which we ourselves are unaware.

**70.** No disguise can long hide love where it exists, or fake it where it does not.

**71.** Few people would not be ashamed of being loved when they no longer love.

**72.** If we judge love by most of its results, it looks more like hatred than friendship.

**73.** We may find women who have never had an intrigue, but it is rare to find those who have had only one.

**74.** There is only one kind of love, but there are a thousand different copies of it.

**75.** Neither love nor fire can survive without constant movement; both stop living the moment they stop hoping or fearing.

**76.** True love is like real ghosts: everyone talks about it, few have seen it.

**77.** Love lends its name to countless dealings attributed to it, with which it has no more to do than the Doge of Venice has with everything done in Venice.

**78.** The love of justice in most men is simply the fear of suffering injustice.

**79.** Silence is the safest course for a man who distrusts himself.

**80.** What makes us so changeable in our friendships is that the qualities of the soul are hard to know, while those of the mind are easy.

**81.** We can love nothing that does not suit us, and we only follow our taste or pleasure when we put our friends before ourselves; yet only by that putting-first can friendship be true and perfect.

**82.** Making peace with our enemies is only a wish to better our position, weariness of war, and fear of some unlucky accident.

**83.** What men call friendship is merely a partnership of shared interests and an exchange of favors — in fact it is a trade in which self-love always expects to gain something.

**84.** It is more shameful to distrust our friends than to be deceived by them.

**85.** We often talk ourselves into loving people more powerful than we are, yet it is interest alone that makes our friendship; we do not give our hearts for the good we wish to do, but for the good we expect to receive.

**86.** Our distrust of another man gives him license to deceive us.

**87.** Men would not live long together in society if they were not each other's dupes.

**88.** Self-love magnifies or shrinks the good qualities of our friends according to how satisfied we are with them, and we judge their merit by how they treat us.

**89.** Everyone complains of his memory; no one complains of his judgment.

**90.** In daily life we please more by our faults than by our good qualities.

**91.** The biggest ambition looks least like ambition when it meets something utterly impossible.

**92.** To undeceive a man who is full of his own merit does him as bad a turn as was done to the mad Athenian who believed all the ships coming into the harbor were his.
*[The 1678 French reads "Détromper un homme préoccupé de son mérite" (to undeceive a man full of his own merit); the base's "deceived about his own merit" is looser. Followed the French.]*

**93.** Old men love giving good advice as consolation for no longer being able to set bad examples.

**94.** Great names drag down rather than lift those who do not know how to live up to them.

**95.** The test of extraordinary merit is to see those who envy it most forced to praise it.

**96.** A man may be ungrateful, but often the fault lies less with him than with his benefactor.

**97.** We are wrong to think mind and judgment are two different things: judgment is only the reach of the mind's light. This light gets to the bottom of things; it notices all that can be noticed, and sees what looks invisible. So we must agree that it is the reach of the mind's light that produces all the effects we credit to judgment.

**98.** Everyone praises his own heart; no one dares praise his own understanding.

**99.** Politeness of mind is thinking decent and delicate thoughts.
*[The 1678 French reads "des choses honnêtes & délicates" (decent things); the base says "chaste." Followed the French.]*

**100.** Gallantry of mind is saying flattering things in an agreeable way.
*[The 1678 French reads "dire des choses flatteuses" (saying flattering things); the base says "the most empty things." Followed the French.]*

**101.** Ideas often flash into our minds more fully formed than we could make them with much labor.

**102.** The head is always the dupe of the heart.

**103.** Knowing your own mind does not mean knowing your own heart.

**104.** Men and things each have their proper distance; to judge some rightly you must see them close, others you can only judge rightly from afar.

**105.** A man whom chance has given sense is not a reasonable being. Only he is reasonable who understands, who distinguishes, who tests.

**106.** To understand things rightly we must understand their details, and since that knowledge is almost endless, our knowledge is always shallow and imperfect.

**107.** One kind of flirting is to boast that you never flirt.

**108.** The head cannot long play the heart's part.

**109.** Youth changes its tastes with the heat of its blood; age keeps its tastes from habit.

**110.** Nothing is handed out so freely as advice.

**111.** The more we love a woman, the readier we are to hate her.

**112.** The blemishes of the mind, like those of the face, grow with age.

**113.** There may be good marriages, but there are no delightful ones.

**114.** We cannot bear being deceived by our enemies and betrayed by our friends, yet we are often content to be served so by ourselves.

**115.** It is as easy to deceive ourselves without knowing it as to deceive others.

**116.** Nothing is less sincere than the asking and giving of advice. The asker seems to honor his friend's opinion while really thinking of getting his friend to approve his own opinion and take responsibility for his conduct. The giver returns the trust placed in him with eager, unselfish zeal, though he is usually guided only by his own interest or reputation.

**117.** The subtlest thing we do is to pretend blindness to traps we know are set for us. We are never so easily deceived as when we are trying to deceive.

**118.** The resolve never to deceive often leaves us open to deception.

**119.** We grow so used to disguising ourselves to others that at last we are disguised to ourselves.

**120.** We often act treacherously more from weakness than from a settled plan.

**121.** We often do good so that we may do evil without punishment.

**122.** If we conquer our passions, it is more from their weakness than from our strength.

**123.** If we never flattered ourselves, we would have little pleasure.

**124.** The most deceitful people spend their lives denouncing deceit, so that they may use it on some great occasion for some great interest.

**125.** Daily scheming marks a small mind; those who use it to protect themselves on one side usually leave themselves open on another.

**126.** Cunning and treachery are the children of incapacity.

**127.** The surest way to be deceived is to think yourself cleverer than others.

**128.** Too much subtlety is false delicacy; true delicacy is solid subtlety.
*[The 1678 French reads "La trop grande subtilité est une fausse délicatesse: & la véritable délicatesse est une solide subtilité"; the base twice renders "subtilité" as "cleverness." Followed the French.]*

**129.** Sometimes it is enough to be blunt not to be fooled by a clever man.
*[The 1678 French reads "Il suffit quelquefois d'être grossier" (to be crude/blunt); the base says "play the fool." Followed the French.]*

**130.** Weakness is the only fault that cannot be cured.

**131.** The least fault of women who give themselves to love is to love.

**132.** It is far easier to be wise for others than to be wise for yourself.

**133.** The only good copies are those that show us the ridiculousness of bad originals.
*[The 1678 French reads "Les seules bonnes copies"; the base says "examples." Followed the French.]*

**134.** We are never so ridiculous for the qualities we have as for those we pretend to have.
*[The 1678 French reads "par les qualitez que l'on a" (by the qualities we have); the base says "habits." Followed the French.]*

**135.** We sometimes differ from ourselves more widely than we differ from others.

**136.** Some people would never have loved if they had never heard love talked about.

**137.** When vanity is not prompting us, we say little.

**138.** A man would rather speak ill of himself than say nothing.

**139.** One reason we find so few people sensible and agreeable in conversation is that hardly anyone thinks more about answering what is said than about what he wants to say himself. The cleverest and politest are content to seem attentive, while you can see in their eyes and minds that they are wandering from what is being said and longing to get back to what they want to say. Instead of seeing that the worst way to persuade or please others is to try so hard to please ourselves, and that listening well and answering well are among the greatest charms in conversation.

**140.** If it were not for the company of fools, a witty man would often be at a loss.

**141.** We often boast that we are never bored, yet we are so conceited we do not see how often we bore others.

**142.** As great minds are known by saying much in few words, so small minds are known by using many words to say nothing.

**143.** It is more often our own feelings than their merit that makes us overpraise others, and when we praise them we want to draw their praise.

**144.** We dislike praising, and we never praise without a motive. Praise is flattery — artful, hidden, delicate — which pleases the giver and the receiver differently. The one takes it as the reward of merit; the other gives it to show his fairness and knowledge.

**145.** We often choose poisoned praise which, bouncing back on those we praise, exposes faults we could not have exposed otherwise.

**146.** Usually we only praise to be praised.

**147.** Few are wise enough to prefer useful blame to treacherous praise.

**148.** Some blame is praise; some praise is blame.

**149.** Refusing praise is only wanting to be praised twice.

**150.** The wish to deserve praise strengthens our good qualities, and praise of wit, courage, and beauty tends to increase them.

**151.** It is easier to govern others than to keep from being governed.

**152.** If we never flattered ourselves, the flattery of others would not harm us.

**153.** Nature makes merit, but fortune puts it to work.

**154.** Fortune cures us of many faults that reason could not.

**155.** There are people who disgust us with their abilities, and people who please us even with their faults.

**156.** There are people whose whole merit is saying and doing silly things at the right time, who ruin everything if they change their manners.

**157.** The fame of great men should always be judged by the means used to win it.

**158.** Flattery is base coin, and only our vanity gives it currency.

**159.** It is not enough to have great qualities; we must also know how to manage them.

**160.** However brilliant an action, it should not be counted great unless it is the result of a great design.
*[The 1678 French reads "l'effet d'un grand dessein" (the result of a great design/plan); the base says "great motive." Followed the French.]*

**161.** A certain harmony should be kept between actions and ideas if we want to judge the effects they produce.

**162.** The art of making the most of modest abilities wins praise, and often earns more reputation than real brilliance.

**163.** Countless crafts look foolish whose secret motives are most wise and weighty.

**164.** It is much easier to look fit for offices we do not hold than for those we do.

**165.** Ability wins us the respect of good judges; luck wins us that of the crowd.

**166.** The world rewards the appearance of merit more often than merit itself.

**167.** Miserliness is more opposed to thrift than to generosity.

**168.** However deceitful hope may be, she carries us pleasantly to the end of life.

**169.** Laziness and fear keep us on the path of duty, but our virtue gets the praise.

**170.** If a man acts rightly and honestly, it is hard to say whether it comes from integrity or from skill.

**171.** As rivers are lost in the sea, so virtues are lost in self-interest.
*[The 1678 French reads "Les vertus se perdent dans l'intérêt" (virtues are lost in self-interest); the base says "in self." Followed the French.]*

**172.** If we look closely at the effects of boredom, we find it makes us miss more duties than self-interest does.
*[The 1678 French reads "les divers effets de l'ennui" (the effects of boredom); the base says "indifference." Followed the French.]*

**173.** There are different kinds of curiosity: one comes from interest, which makes us want to know everything that may profit us; another comes from pride, from wanting to know what others do not.

**174.** It is far better to train our minds to bear the ills we have than to brood over those that may come.

**175.** Constancy in love is a constant inconstancy, which fastens our heart on all the qualities of the person we love in turn, preferring now one, now another. This constancy is only inconstancy fixed and confined to the same person.

**176.** There are two kinds of constancy in love: one comes from constantly finding new things to love in the beloved; the other from making constancy a point of honor.

**177.** Perseverance deserves neither blame nor praise, since it is only the continuance of tastes and feelings we can neither make nor destroy.

**178.** What makes us like new studies is not so much weariness of the old or love of change as the wish to be admired by those who know more than we do, and the hope of advantage over those who know less.

**179.** We sometimes complain of our friends' fickleness to excuse our own in advance.

**180.** Our repentance is not so much sorrow for the harm we have done as fear of the harm that may come to us.

**181.** One kind of inconstancy comes from lightness or weakness of mind and makes us take up everyone's opinion; another, more excusable kind comes from being sick of things.

**182.** Vices go into the making of virtues as poison goes into medicines. Prudence gathers and blends the two and makes them useful against the ills of life.

**183.** To do virtue credit, we must admit that the worst misfortunes of men are those they fall into through their crimes.

**184.** We confess our faults to repair, by our sincerity, the damage they have done us in the opinion of others.

**185.** There are heroes of evil as well as heroes of good.

**186.** We do not despise all who have vices, but we do despise all who have no virtues.

**187.** The name of virtue is as useful to our interest as the name of vice.

**188.** The health of the mind is no more certain than that of the body; when the passions seem farthest away we are in as much danger of infection as of falling ill when we are well.

**189.** Nature seems to have fixed, at a man's birth, the limits of his virtues and his vices.

**190.** Great men should not have great faults.

**191.** We may say vices lodge with us through life like innkeepers we stop with in turn, and if we traveled the road twice I doubt our experience would make us avoid them.

**192.** When our vices leave us, we flatter ourselves that we have left them.

**193.** There are relapses in diseases of the mind as in those of the body; what we call a cure is often only a pause or a change of disease.

**194.** Defects of the mind are like wounds of the body. Whatever care we take to heal them, the scars remain, and there is always danger of their reopening.

**195.** The reason we so often fail to give up a single vice is that we have so many.

**196.** We easily forget the faults known only to ourselves.

**197.** There are men of whom we can never believe evil until we have seen it; yet there are very few in whom we would be surprised to see it.

**198.** We exaggerate the glory of some men to lessen that of others; we would praise Conde and Turenne far less if we did not want to blame them both.

**199.** The wish to look clever often keeps us from being so.

**200.** Virtue would not go far if vanity did not walk beside her.

**201.** He who thinks he can satisfy the world deceives himself greatly, but he who thinks the world cannot be satisfied with him deceives himself even more.

**202.** Falsely honest men are those who hide their faults from themselves and others; truly honest men are those who know their faults perfectly and confess them.

**203.** The true gentleman is the man who is ruffled by nothing.
*[The 1678 French reads "Le vray honneste homme est celuy qui ne se pique de rien" (the true gentleman); the base says "He is really wise." Followed the French.]*

**204.** The sternness of women is a dress and a paint they add to their beauty.
*[The 1678 French reads "La sévérité des femmes est un ajustement & un fard" (severity — an adornment and a paint); the base says "coldness... a balance and burden." Followed the French.]*

**205.** Virtue in women is often the love of reputation and of a quiet life.

**206.** He is a truly good man who always wants to bear the inspection of good men.

**207.** Folly follows us at every stage of life. If a man looks wise, it is only because his folly suits his age and fortune.

**208.** There are foolish people who know their folly and use it skillfully.

**209.** Whoever lives without folly is not as wise as he thinks.

**210.** Growing old, we become more foolish — and more wise.

**211.** Some people are like farces: praised for a time, however foolish and tasteless.

**212.** Most people judge men only by success or by fortune.

**213.** Love of glory, fear of shame, the plan of making a fortune, the wish to make life pleasant, and the wish to run others down are often the causes of that bravery so praised among men.
*[The 1678 French reads "le dessein de faire fortune" (the plan to make a fortune); the base says "greed of fortune." Followed the French.]*

**214.** Courage in common soldiers is a dangerous way of earning a living.

**215.** Perfect bravery and sheer cowardice are two extremes rarely found. Between them lies a vast space holding every other kind of courage, as different as faces and tempers. Men will expose themselves freely at the start of a fight and slacken if it lasts. Some are content to satisfy honor and do little more. Some are not always masters of their fear. Some are seized by panic; others charge because they dare not stay at their posts. Some grow bolder in small dangers and are readied for greater ones. Some will face a sword cut and flinch from a bullet; others fear bullets little and dread crossing swords. All these kinds of courage agree in one thing: night, by increasing fear and hiding brave or cowardly deeds, lets men spare themselves. And more generally, we meet no man who does all he would have done if he were sure of escaping unhurt; so the fear of death always takes something from courage.

**216.** Perfect courage is doing without witnesses what you would do before all the world.

**217.** Fearlessness is an extraordinary strength of soul that lifts it above the troubles, disorders, and feelings that the sight of great dangers can stir in it; by this strength heroes keep a calm face and keep their reason and freedom in the most startling and terrible accidents.

**218.** Hypocrisy is the homage vice pays to virtue.

**219.** Most men expose themselves in battle enough to save their honor; few want to do more than enough, or more than is needed to make their plan succeed.

**220.** Vanity, shame, and above all temperament often make the courage of men and the virtue of women.
*[The 1678 French reads "font souvent la valeur des hommes & la vertu des femmes" (the courage of men and the virtue of women); the base says "men brave and women chaste." Followed the French.]*

**221.** We do not want to lose our lives; we do want to gain glory, and this makes brave men show more skill and cleverness in avoiding death than rogues show in saving their fortunes.

**222.** Few people reach the first approach of old age without showing where body or mind is beginning to fail.

**223.** Gratitude is like the good faith of merchants: it keeps trade going, and we do not pay because it is just to pay our debts, but because it makes it easier to find people who will lend.

**224.** Not all who pay the debts of gratitude can flatter themselves that they are grateful.

**225.** What makes our reckoning of gratitude go wrong is that the pride of the giver and the pride of the receiver cannot agree on the value of the favor.

**226.** Too much hurry to pay off an obligation is a kind of ingratitude.

**227.** Lucky people are bad at correcting their faults; they always think they are right when fortune backs up their vice or folly.

**228.** Pride will not owe; self-love will not pay.

**229.** The good we have received from a man should make us excuse the wrong he does us.

**230.** Nothing is so catching as example, and we never do great good or evil without producing more of the same. We copy good deeds from rivalry, and bad ones from the evil in our nature, which shame keeps locked up until example sets it free.

**231.** It is great folly to want only to be wise.

**232.** Whatever excuse we give for our griefs, it is often only interest or vanity that causes them.
*[The 1678 French reads "ce n'est souvent que l'intérêt & la vanité" (it is often only self-interest and vanity); the base drops "often." Followed the French.]*

**233.** There are different kinds of hypocrisy in grief. In one, under the pretext of weeping for someone dear to us, we weep for ourselves; we miss her good opinion of us, we mourn the loss of our comfort, our pleasure, our standing. So the dead get the credit for tears shed for the living. I call this a kind of hypocrisy that deceives itself. There is another kind, less innocent because it deceives the whole world: the grief of those who aspire to the glory of a noble and endless sorrow. After time, which swallows all, has worn away the sorrow they had, they still stubbornly display their tears, sighs, and groans, wear a solemn face, and try to convince everyone by all they do that their grief will end only with their life. This sad and painful vanity is commonly found in ambitious women. Since their sex shuts them out of every road to glory, they try to make themselves famous by showing an inconsolable grief. There is yet another kind of tears, from small causes, that flow easily and stop as easily. One weeps to earn a name for tenderness, weeps to be pitied, weeps to be wept for — in short, one weeps to avoid the disgrace of not weeping.

**234.** It is more often from pride than from ignorance that we stubbornly oppose the opinions of the day; we find the first places taken, and we do not want to be last.

**235.** We are easily comforted for our friends' misfortunes when they let us show our tenderness for them.

**236.** It seems even self-love can be the dupe of kindness and forget itself when we work for others. And yet it is only taking the shortest road to its goal — taking interest under the pretense of giving, in fact winning everyone over in a subtle and delicate way.

**237.** No one should be praised for his goodness who has not strength enough to be bad. All other goodness is too often only laziness or weakness of will.

**238.** It is less dangerous to do most men wrong than to do them too much good.

**239.** Nothing flatters our pride so much as the confidence of the great, because we take it as the fruit of our worth, forgetting that it is usually just vanity, or the inability to keep a secret.

**240.** We may say of good looks, as distinct from beauty, that they are a symmetry with no rules, a secret harmony of features with each other and with the color and appearance of the person.

**241.** Flirting is at the bottom of woman's nature, though not all practice it; some are held back by fear, others by sense.

**242.** We often bore others when we think we could not possibly bore them.

**243.** Few things are impossible in themselves; what fails us more often than the means is the will to apply them.

**244.** The highest ability is knowing the value of things.

**245.** There is great ability in knowing how to hide your ability.

**246.** What looks like generosity is often ambition in disguise, scorning small gains to chase bigger ones.

**247.** The loyalty of most men is merely an invention of self-love to win trust: a way to rise above others and become the keeper of important secrets.

**248.** Magnanimity despises all to win all.

**249.** There is as much eloquence in a speaker's voice, eyes, and bearing as in his choice of words.

**250.** True eloquence is saying all that should be said, not all that could be said.

**251.** Some people's faults become them; other people's very virtues disgrace them.

**252.** It is as common to change your tastes as it is rare to change your inclinations.

**253.** Self-interest puts every kind of virtue and vice to work.

**254.** Humility is often a fake submission, used to shove others aside. It is
one of pride's tricks: bring yourself low to raise yourself high. Pride changes
its shape a thousand ways, and never deceives better than when it hides under
humility.

**255.** Every feeling has its own tone of voice, its gestures and its looks.
That fit — good or bad, pleasant or unpleasant — is what makes people agreeable
or disagreeable.

**256.** In every trade people act a part and put on a look to seem what they
want to be. The world is nothing but a cast of actors.

**257.** Solemn gravity is a mysterious bearing of the body, invented to hide an
empty mind.

**258.** Good taste comes more from judgment than from cleverness.

**259.** The joy of love is in loving: we are happier in the passion we feel than
in the one we inspire.

**260.** Politeness is just a wish to get politeness back, and to be thought
polite.

**261.** The usual raising of young people is a second self-love planted in them.

**262.** No passion is ruled by self-love as powerfully as love — and we are
always readier to wreck the peace of the one we love than our own.

**263.** What we call generosity is often just the vanity of giving: we like the
giving more than what we give.

**264.** Pity is often our own troubles seen in other people's troubles. It is a
careful preview of the troubles we may fall into ourselves. We help others so
others will help us when it happens to us — and the favors we do are really
favors we give ourselves in advance.

**265.** A narrow mind breeds stubbornness, and we don't easily believe what we
can't see.

**266.** We fool ourselves if we think strong passions like ambition and love
can beat the rest. Idleness, faint as she looks, rarely fails to become the
mistress of the house: she quietly takes over every plan and action of life,
and wears away both passions and virtues without being noticed.

**267.** Jumping to believe the worst without looking into it properly is pride
and laziness at work. We want to find someone guilty, and we don't want the
trouble of examining the crime.

**268.** We credit judges with the lowest motives, and yet we stake our name and
reputation on the judgment of men — men who, from jealousy, bias, or lack of

**271.** Youth is a constant intoxication — the fever of reason.

**273.** There are people the world approves of whose only merit is the vices

**274.** Novelty is to love what the blossom is to the fruit: it gives a shine

**275.** Natural goodness, for all its show of being plain to see, is often

**276.** Absence kills small passions and feeds big ones — like wind blowing out
a candle and blowing up a fire.

**277.** Women often think they love when they don't love. The bustle of a love

**278.** What makes us so often fed up with the people who do our business for

**279.** When we play up how tender our friends are toward us, it's often less

**280.** The praise we give newcomers comes from the envy we bear those already
established.

**281.** Pride, which stirs envy up, often serves to cool it down.

**283.** Sometimes knowing how to use good advice takes no less skill than

**284.** There are wicked people who would be much less dangerous if they had

**286.** You can't love a second time those you have truly stopped loving.

**288.** There are troubles and sicknesses that remedies only make worse at

**289.** Studied simplicity is polished fraud.

**294.** We always like those who admire us; we don't always like those we

**300.** There are follies as catching as fevers.

**375.** Ordinary men usually condemn whatever is beyond them.

**384.** The only thing we should be surprised at is that we can still be

**387.** A fool doesn't have what it takes to be good.

**391.** Fortune looks blind to no one so much as to those she's done nothing

**399.** There's a kind of greatness that doesn't depend on fortune: a certain

**400.** There can be talent without a position, but no position without some
kind of talent.

**401.** Rank is to merit what clothes are to a pretty woman.

**402.** What you find least in flirtation is love.

**404.** Nature seems to have hidden talents and abilities at the bottom of our
hearts that we don't know about. Only our passions can bring them to light —
and sometimes they show us things truer and finer than any skill could.

**405.** We come to each stage of life as beginners, and often, for all our

**406.** Flirts make it a point of honor to be jealous of their lovers — to hide

**409.** We'd often be ashamed of our very best actions if the world could see

**410.** The hardest work of friendship isn't showing our faults to a friend —

**411.** Few of our faults are as bad as the tricks we use to hide them.

**413.** A man with only one kind of wit can't please for long.

**421.** Conceit causes more talk than wit.

**422.** Every passion makes us do wrong things; love alone makes us ridiculous.

**424.** We often take credit for the opposite of the vices we have — when weak,

**504.** On the hollowness of despising death. The pagans boasted a contempt of
death drawn from reason alone, with no hope of a life to come — but meeting
death bravely is common enough; truly despising it is always faked. Everything
that could be written has been written to convince us death is no evil, and the
weakest men have given as many noble examples as the bravest — yet I don't
think any man of sense ever believed it. The trouble we take to convince
others, and ourselves, shows how far from easy the job is. We may be sick of
life for many reasons, but we can despise it for none. Not even suicides take
it lightly — they're as startled as anyone when death comes in a different
shape than the one they chose. The difference we see in brave men's courage
comes from meeting death differently than they pictured it, nearer one day
than another: having scorned death when they didn't know it, they fear it once
they do. If we could see it stripped of its trappings, we might believe it
wasn't the greatest of evils. The wisest and bravest are those who best avoid
thinking about it — anyone who sees it in its true light finds it dreadful.
The need to die made all the philosophers' fine constancy: they thought it
right to go gracefully when go they must, and, unable to live forever, set
about building an undying name and saving what could be saved from the wreck.
To put a brave face on it, say less than you think, and trust nature more than
shaky reason — reason that tells us we can face death without a qualm. The
glory of dying bravely, the hope of being mourned, the wish to leave a good
name, the certainty of escaping life's miseries and being free of fortune's
tricks — these are resources not to be scorned. But don't trust them too far:
they're like a lone shelter to soldiers storming a fort — from far off it looks
like cover, up close it's flimsy. It's self-deception to think death up close
will look the same as from far off, or that our feelings — mere weaknesses —
are strong enough to stand the roughest trial of all. It's just as foolish to
lean on self-esteem and think it will let us count as nothing what must
destroy it. And the mind we trust to arm us will prove too weak in the fight —
it betrays us constantly, and instead of filling us with contempt for death,
only shows us everything frightful in it. The most it can do is get us to look
away and fix our eyes elsewhere. Cato and Brutus each chose noble things to
look at; a lackey not long ago settled for dancing on the scaffold on his way
to the wheel. Different motives, same result. And whatever gap there is
between peer and peasant, we've always seen both meet death with the same calm.
With this difference: the peer's contempt for death is love of fame hiding
death from his eyes; the peasant's is narrow vision hiding from him how great
the evil is, and leaving him free to think of other things.
