## Method Statement

What you are reading is **not** a new translation from Greek. It is a
plain-English modernization of Thomas Taylor's 1793 English translation of
Sallustius's *On the Gods and the World*, verified on Wikisource as
"Sallust On the Gods and the World (1793) translated by Thomas Taylor" —
a work in the public domain worldwide (Taylor died in 1835).

Taylor's English is deliberately archaic: long periodic sentences, thee/thou
forms of address, and 18th-century philosophical vocabulary. The job here was
to render that English into language a humble peasant could understand —
shorter sentences, ordinary words, no archaisms — while keeping Taylor's
meaning faithfully, chapter by chapter, with nothing added and nothing taken
away. Where Taylor used terms that have no plain modern equivalent, they are
kept and explained once, in the note below.

Taylor's footnotes to his 1793 edition are omitted in this draft. The
accompanying pieces in Taylor's volume — the Pythagoric Sentences of
Demophilus and the Hymns of Proclus — are not part of this book.

No claim is made that this is the first, oldest, definitive, or complete
English version of anything. It is one clear rendering, honestly labeled, of a
public-domain text.

## What This Text Is

*On the Gods and the World* (Greek: *Peri theōn kai kosmou*) is a short
treatise of 4th-century Hellenic pagan theology — a kind of pagan catechism,
written while Christianity was becoming the power of the empire. Its author,
the Sallustius named by the ancient writer Photius, is generally identified
as a friend and philosophical ally of the Emperor Julian — the last pagan
emperor of Rome. It is Neoplatonist in outlook: one first cause beyond being,
ranks of gods below it, an unmade and unending world, the immortality of the
soul, and an account of why the old myths (fables) are not lies but veils
over truth.

Say it plainly: this is a pagan book, written by a pagan, for pagans, in the
fourth century. Read it as what it is.

## A Note on Terms

- **Gods (lowercase g is Taylor's, kept as "gods").** Taylor follows the
  Greek in speaking of many gods under one first cause. Left as written.
- **Super-mundane / mundane gods.** "Above the world" and "of the world."
  Where the sentence allows, rendered as "gods above the world" and "gods of
  the world."
- **Daemons.** Taylor's "dæmons" are *not* devils. They are the middle beings
  between gods and men — guardians, avengers, purifiers. Rendered here as
  "daemons" on first use in each chapter with the sense given in context,
  so no reader mistakes them for the Christian devil.
- **Intellect, soul, essence.** The three great rungs of Taylor's (and
  Sallustius's) ladder: *being* at the bottom, *soul* above it, *intellect*
  above that, and the first cause above them all. Kept as technical terms and
  explained where they first appear.
- **Polity.** A city's form of government. Kept.
- **Providence, fate, fortune.** Three different divine workings, carefully
  distinguished in chapter 9: providence (the gods' effortless care), fate
  (that care working through bodies and the stars), fortune (the gods'
  unexpected turns toward good ends).

# ON THE GODS AND THE WORLD

*Sallustius — modernized from Thomas Taylor's 1793 English*

## 1. What a hearer about the gods must bring, and of common conceptions

Those who want to hear about the gods must have been raised well from
childhood, and not fed on foolish opinions. They must also be naturally
sensible and good, so they can take in what they hear and understand it
rightly.

They must also know the common conceptions. Common conceptions are things
that all men, when asked, admit are certainly true — such as that every god
is good, cannot be acted upon, and is free from all change. For anything that
changes is changed either into something better or into something worse. If
into something worse, it becomes corrupted; but if into something better, it
must have been evil to begin with.

## 2. That a god is unchanging, ungenerated, eternal, without body, and not in any place

Such are the qualifications for a hearer about the gods. The teaching itself
runs as follows.

The essences of the gods are not generated. Eternal natures are without
generation, and those beings are eternal who hold a first power and are by
nature free from being acted upon. Their essences are not put together out of
bodies — for even the powers of bodies are without body. They are not
contained in any place — that is a property of bodies. They are not cut off
from the first cause, nor from one another — just as thoughts are not cut off
from the mind, nor branches of knowledge from the soul.

## 3. Of fables — that they are divine, and why

Why, then, did the ancients set aside teachings like these and use fables?
The question is worth looking into.

The first good that comes from fables is this: they stir us to inquiry and
will not let our thinking power lie idle and asleep. It is not hard to show
that fables are divine, from the hands that use them: they are used by poets
moved by divinity, by the best of philosophers, and by those who reveal the
mystery rites. In oracles, too, the gods themselves use fables. But *why*
fables are divine is a question for philosophy.

Since all beings delight in likeness and turn away from unlikeness, discourses
about the gods must be made as like the gods as possible — so they may be
worthy of the gods' nature, and make the gods favorable to those who speak of
them. Only fables can do this. Fables imitate the gods in being partly
speakable and partly unspeakable, partly seen and partly unseen, partly wise
and partly foolish. This extends to the goodness of the gods as well: as the
gods give the goods of the senses to all in common, but the goods of the mind
to the wise alone, so fables tell all men that there are gods — but *who*
they are, and of what kind, they show only to those able to receive so high a
knowledge.

Fables also imitate the workings of the gods. The world itself may rightly be
called a fable: its bodies and bodily goods are seen, but souls and minds are
hidden and invisible.

Besides, to tell all men the plain truth about the gods breeds contempt in
the unwise — who cannot learn — and laziness in the studious. But hiding the
truth in fables keeps the first from contempt and forces the second to
philosophize.

But you will ask: why are adulteries, thefts, the binding of fathers, and
other unworthy deeds celebrated in fables? There is nothing strange in this.
Where there is an obvious absurdity, the soul at once takes these tales for
coverings, and understands that the truth inside them is to be kept in deep
and guarded silence.

## 4. That there are five kinds of fables, with examples of each

Of fables, some are theological, some physical, some about the soul, some
material, and some mixed from these.

**Theological** fables use nothing bodily, but contemplate the very essences
of the gods — like the fable that says Saturn devoured his children. It hints
darkly at the nature of an intellectual god, since every intellect returns
into itself.

We treat fables **physically** when we speak of the workings of the gods in
the world — as when we take Saturn for Time, call the parts of time the
children of the universe, and say the children are devoured by their parent.

We use fables in the **soul's** manner when we contemplate the workings of
the soul — because our thoughts, though they go out into other things by a
step-by-step working, still stay rooted in their source.

**Material** fables are such as the Egyptians use in their ignorance, taking
bodily things for gods: Isis for earth, Osiris for moisture, Typhon for heat;
or again Saturn for water, Adonis for crops, Bacchus for wine. To say these
things are *dedicated* to the gods — as herbs, stones, and animals are — is
the part of wise men. To call them gods is the part of madmen — unless we
speak as custom speaks, when it calls the orb of the sun and its rays "the
sun" itself.

The **mixed** kind may be seen in many places, and in the fable that tells
how Discord threw a golden apple at a banquet of the gods, how a quarrel over
it rose among the goddesses, and how they were sent by Jupiter to the
judgment of Paris — who, charmed by the beauty of Venus, gave her the apple
above the rest. In this fable the banquet means the above-world powers of the
gods, and so they dwell together with one another. The golden apple means the
world, which — being put together from contrary natures — is fitly said to
be thrown by Discord, or strife. And since different gods give different gifts
to the world, they seem to quarrel over the apple. And a soul living by the
senses — for this is Paris — seeing no other powers in the universe, declares
that the contested apple comes from the beauty of Venus alone.

Of these kinds of fables, the theological belong to philosophers; the physical
and those of the soul, to poets; the mixed, to the mystery rites — since the
aim of all mystic ceremonies is to join us to the world and the gods.

If another fable must be told, this one may serve. They say the mother of the
gods, seeing Attis by the river Gallus, fell in love with him, set a starry
cap on his head, and lived with him ever after in close union. But Attis fell
in love with a Nymph, left the mother of the gods, and joined himself to the
Nymph. At this the mother of the gods drove Attis mad; he cut off his manhood,
left it with the Nymph, and returned to his former union with the goddess.

The mother of the gods is the life-giving goddess, and so is called mother.
Attis is the maker of things that come to be and pass away — hence he is
found by the river Gallus, for Gallus means the Galaxy, the milky circle, from
which a passive body comes down to earth. And since the first gods perfect
those that come after, the mother of the gods, falling in love with Attis,
gives him heavenly powers — that is the meaning of the starry cap. But Attis
loves a nymph, and nymphs rule over generation, for everything in generation
flows. Yet the flowing nature of generation must be stopped, or something
worse than the lowest things would be produced. So the maker of things that
come to be and pass away, sending his fruitful powers into the realms of
generation, is joined again to the gods.

But these things never happened at any one time, because they are forever.
The mind sees all things as existing together; speech puts one thing first
and another second in the order of being.

Since a fable fits the world so well, how could we — who imitate the world —
be better adorned than by the help of fable? Through it we keep a holy day.
First, we ourselves, fallen from the heavens and joined to a nymph — the
symbol of generation — live sunk in sorrow, fasting from corn and other gross
and sordid foods, since all such things are against the soul. Then come the
cutting of a tree and fasting, as if we would cut off from our nature all
further advance of generation. At last we take milk for food, as if passing
into a state of rebirth; and finally come joy and crowns, and a rising again,
as it were, to the gods.

The time of these rites confirms their truth: they are held about spring, at
the equinox, when things in generation cease to generate, and the days grow
longer than the nights — for this season suits souls on the way up. The
carrying-off of Proserpine is fabled to have happened at the opposite
equinox, and it stands for the descent of souls.

So much for the way fables are to be read. To this discourse on them, may
both the gods and the souls of the fable-writers be favorable.

## 5. Of the first cause

Next we must know the first cause, and the ranks of gods below the first;
the nature of the world, of intellect, of soul, and of essence; also
providence, fate, and fortune; virtue and vice; and the good and evil forms of
government that come from these; and lastly, where evil crept into the world.
Each of these needs many long discourses, but there is no reason we may not
treat them briefly, so mankind is not left wholly without this knowledge.

The first cause must be one. The One stands over all multitude, excelling all
things in power and goodness, and so all things must share in its nature:
nothing can block its workings through power, and it will not cut itself off
from anything through the goodness it has.

If the first cause were soul, all things would be alive. If it were intellect,
all things would be intellectual. If it were essence, all things would share
in essence — and some, seeing essence in all things, have taken occasion to
call it essence. If things had nothing but being, and did not also have
goodness, this would be true. But beings exist through goodness and share in
*the good* — so the first cause must be beyond essence, and *the good* itself.

This is best proved by souls endowed with virtue, who neglect the care of
their own being and face the greatest dangers for their country, their
friends, or virtue's sake.

After this unspeakable power come the ranks of the gods.

## 6. Of the gods above the world and the gods of the world

Of the gods, some belong to the world and some are above the world. I call
those of the world who make the world. Of those above the world, some produce
essences, others intellect, and others soul — and so they fall into three
ranks, and in treating of these ranks all the gods are easily found.

But of the gods of the world, some are the causes of the world's existence,
others give the world life, others harmonize it though it is put together
from different natures, and others guard and keep it once it is harmonized.
Since these ranks are four, and each is made of first, middle, and last, their
rulers must be twelve: Jupiter, Neptune, and Vulcan make the world; Ceres,
Juno, and Diana give it life; Mercury, Venus, and Apollo harmonize it; and
Vesta, Minerva, and Mars watch over it with guardian power.

The truth of this may be seen in statues as in riddles: Apollo tunes the lyre,
Pallas is armed, Venus is naked — since harmony produces beauty, and beauty
is not hidden from the eyes. But since these gods hold the world first, the
other gods must be considered as dwelling in these: Bacchus in Jupiter,
Aesculapius in Apollo, the Graces in Venus. We may also see the spheres
joined to them: Vesta with earth, Neptune with water, Juno with air, Vulcan
with fire. The six higher gods we name by custom: we take Apollo and Diana
for the sun and moon, give the sphere of Saturn to Ceres and the upper air to
Pallas, and say heaven is common to them all.

The ranks, powers, and spheres of the twelve gods are thus laid open, and
celebrated as in a sacred hymn.

## 7. Of the nature and everlastingness of the world

The world must be incorruptible and unmade. Incorruptible: if it were
corrupted, it must become either better, or worse, or mere confusion. If
worse, its maker must be evil, changing it from better to worse. If better,
its maker must have lacked power, since he did not make it better at first. If
it changed into the same state as before, he labors in vain. And it is not
permitted to say he changes it into nothing but disorder and confusion. From
all this it is plain the world is unmade as well: if it cannot be corrupted,
it is unmade — since everything made is also corrupted.

Add this: since the world exists through the goodness of divinity, divinity
must always be good and the world must endure forever — just as light exists
along with the sun and fire, and the shadow with the body that casts it.

Of the bodies in the world, some imitate intellect and move in a circle;
others imitate soul and move in a straight line. Of those moved in a straight
line, fire and air are driven upward, water and earth downward. Of those moved
in a circle, the starry sphere begins its motion from the east, but the seven
planets are carried in their courses from the west. There are many causes of
this, not least the following: if there were but one swift circling of the
spheres, generation would be imperfect; but since motion differs, the nature
of bodies must differ too. A heavenly body must neither burn nor chill, nor
produce anything else belonging to the four elements.

Since the world is a sphere — as the zodiac shows — and in every sphere the
lower part is the middle, being everywhere farthest from the surface, heavy
bodies are driven downward to the earth. All these things the gods make, the
intellect arranges in order, and soul keeps ever in motion.

So much concerning the gods.

## 8. Of intellect and soul, and that the soul is immortal

There is a certain power below essence but before soul. From essence it draws
its being, and it perfects soul — as the sun perfects the sight of the body.
Of souls, some are rational and immortal, others irrational and immortal; the
first come from the first gods, the second from the second ranks of gods.

First let us find the definition of soul. Soul is that by which living things
differ from lifeless things. They differ by motion, sense, imagination, and
thought. The irrational soul is therefore sensitive and imaginative life; the
rational soul is that which rules sense and imagination and uses reason in its
workings. The irrational soul serves the passions of the body: it desires
without reason and burns with anger. The rational soul, with reason's help,
despises the body, and fighting the irrational soul, when it conquers produces
virtue, when it is conquered produces vice.

The rational soul must be immortal, because it knows the gods — and nothing
mortal knows the immortal. It despises human concerns as foreign to its
nature, and stands opposed to bodies, being itself without body. Add this:
when the body it is joined to is beautiful and young, the soul is weighed
down and its strength lessened; but when the body grows old, the soul revives
and grows in strength and vigor.

Every worthy soul uses intellect. But intellect is not generated by body — for
how can things without intellect generate intellect? Using the body as an
instrument, it does not dwell in the body — as no maker of machines dwells in
his machines, though many machines move from place to place with no one
touching them. We should not wonder if the rational soul is often led astray
by the body: even the arts cannot work when their instruments are damaged.

## 9. Of providence, fate, and fortune

From this we may also see the providence of the gods. How could order be put
into the world if no one set it in order? Whence could all things be made for
a purpose — the irrational soul, that there might be sense; the rational,
that the earth might be adorned? We may see providence working in natural
effects too: it made the eyes of a clear nature for seeing, but set the
nostrils above the mouth so we may tell foul smells; of the teeth, the middle
ones are made sharp for cutting, but those set deeper in the mouth are broad
for grinding food. So in all things we may see that nothing is made without
reason and design.

Since so much providence shows in the lowest things, it cannot be absent from
the highest. Divinations and the healing of bodies also come from the kindly
providence of the gods.

We must believe the gods care for the world in like manner — expecting no
reward, and suffering no labor in the caring. As bodies endowed with power
produce by their very essence what they produce — as the sun lights and heats
by what it alone is — so the providence of the gods, with far greater reason,
gives good to those under its care without labor or strain. This dissolves the
objections of the Epicureans against providence, who say that what is divine
causes trouble neither to itself nor to others.

Such is the bodiless providence of the gods over bodies and souls. But the
kindly working of the gods that comes through bodies and dwells in bodies is
different from this, and is called *fate* — because its chain shows more
plainly in bodies, and for its sake the mathematical art was devised. That
human affairs, and especially our bodily nature, are ruled not only by the
gods but also by divine bodies, agrees highly with reason and truth; and
reason tells us that health and sickness, good and bad fortune, come from
these according to each man's deserts.

But to blame fate for injustice and crimes of lust leaves us good and makes
the gods evil and base — unless one answers thus: everything in the world,
and whatever exists by nature, is good; but a nature badly raised, or of
weaker condition, turns the good that comes from fate into something worse —
just as the sun, good in itself, becomes harmful to bleary eyes and the
feverish.

For on what account do the Massagetae eat their parents, the Hebrews practice
circumcision, and the Persians keep their nobility pure? And how can
astrologers call Saturn and Mars harmful, yet praise these same planets as
kindly, saying philosophy, kingdoms, and military command are their gifts? If
they name triangles and squares as the cause, it is absurd that human virtue
should stay the same everywhere while the gods change with differences of
place. That the nobility or lowliness of parents may be foretold from the
stars shows the stars do not *produce* all things, but only *point to* some,
by their different positions and aspects — for how can things that existed
before birth be produced by birth?

As providence and fate govern nations and cities, and every single man, so
also does *fortune* — of which we must now speak. Fortune must be seen as a
power of the gods, disposing things this way and that, happening beyond
expectation, toward kindly ends. So it is fitting that cities honor this
goddess in common, since every city is put together from different parts. This
goddess rules below the moon, since nothing by chance is found above the
moon.

If the wicked enjoy good fortune and the worthy are crushed by want, there is
nothing strange in it: the first count riches as everything, the second
despise them. And besides, good luck does not lessen the wickedness of the
wicked — but virtue alone is enough for the good.

## 10. Of virtue and vice

In speaking of the soul we must speak of virtue and vice. The irrational soul,
entering bodies, at once produces anger and desire; the rational soul, ruling
these, makes the whole soul threefold: reason, anger, and desire.

The virtue of reason is wisdom; of anger, courage; of desire, temperance; of
the whole soul, justice. Reason must judge what is fitting and becoming;
anger, listening to reason's persuasion, must despise things that seem
terrible; desire must pursue what reason approves, not what seems pleasant.
When the parts of the soul stand thus, a just life follows — for justice
about possessions is but a small part of virtue.

In well-raised men you will see all these joined in friendship; in the
uneducated, one is bold but unjust, another prudent but intemperate. You
cannot call these virtues, for they lack reason, are imperfect, and belong to
certain irrational animals.

Vice is seen from the opposites: the vice of reason is folly; of anger,
fear; of desire, intemperance; of the whole soul, injustice. Virtues come from
an upright government and from well-ordered teaching and training; vices from
the opposite course.

## 11. Of a good government and a corrupt one

The forms of government come from the threefold division of the soul: the
rulers are like reason, the soldiers like anger, the common people like
desire.

When all things are managed by reason, and the best of all men holds power, a
*kingdom* is produced. When reason and anger join, and more than one hold the
reins, an *aristocracy* is produced. Where government runs through desire, and
honors are sought for the sake of wealth, it is called a *timocracy*.

The government opposed to a kingdom is called a *tyranny* — for the kingdom
manages everything by reason, the tyranny nothing. *Oligarchy*, the rule of
the few, is opposed to aristocracy — because in it not the best, but a few,
and those the worst, govern the city. Lastly, *democracy* is opposed to
timocracy — because in it not the rich, but the multitude alone, rules all
things.

## 12. Whence evils come, and that there is no nature of evil

But how did evil come into the world, since the gods are good and the makers
of all things? First we must hold that, since the gods are good and the
authors of all things, there is no *nature* of evil. Evil is produced by the
absence of good — just as darkness is in itself nothing, but is produced by
the absence of light.

But if evil had any real being, it must dwell either in the gods, or in
intellects, in souls, or in bodies. It cannot dwell in the gods, since every
god is good. If anyone said intellect is evil, he must also say intellect is
without intellect. If soul, he must say soul is worse than body — for every
body, taken by itself, is without evil. If they say evil comes from soul and
body joined, it is surely absurd that things not evil apart should become evil
by joining.

If anyone says the daemons are evil, we answer: if they hold their power from
the gods, they will not be evil; but if from something else, then the gods
are not the authors of all things. And if the gods do not make all things,
either they are willing but unable, or able but unwilling — and neither can
rightly be said of a god.

From this it is plain that nothing in the world is naturally evil. Evil
appears around the doings of men — and not all of them, nor always. Indeed,
if men sinned through evil itself, nature herself would be evil. But if the
adulterer counts the adultery as evil and the pleasure in it as good; if the
murderer counts the killing as evil and the riches from the deed as good; if
he who destroys his enemies counts the destruction as evil and revenge on an
enemy as good — and souls sin in this way — then evils are produced *through
goodness*, just as darkness is produced while light is absent, though it has
no being in the nature of things.

The soul sins because it desires good, but it wanders about the good because
it is not the first essence. That it may not wander — and that when it does,
proper remedies may be applied and it may be restored — the gods have given
many things: arts and sciences, virtues and prayers, sacrifices and
initiations, laws and governments, judgments and punishments were devised to
keep souls from falling into guilt; and even when they leave this body,
purifying gods and daemons cleanse them from guilt.

## 13. In what sense everlasting things are said to be "made"

What has been said of the gods, the world, and human affairs may be enough
for those who cannot be led upward by philosophy, yet whose souls are not
incurable.

It remains to speak of natures that were never generated nor separated from
one another — for we have already seen that secondary natures come from
primary ones. Everything generated is generated either by art, by nature, or
by power. What works by nature or by art must come before the things it
produces; but what works by power has its products existing along with
itself, since it holds an inseparable power — just as the sun produces light
existing along with itself, fire produces heat, and snow cold.

If the gods made the world by art, they would cause it not simply to *be*,
but to be *in some particular way* — for all art produces form. Whence then
does the world draw its being? If by nature: nature in making gives something
of itself to its products, and the gods are without body — so the world,
their offspring, must be without body. But if anyone says the gods are
bodily: whence comes the power of bodiless things? And besides, if this were
granted, then if the world were corrupted its maker must be corrupted too —
on the supposition that he works by nature.

It remains, then, that the gods made the world by power alone. But everything
generated by power exists along with the cause that holds this power, and
products of this kind cannot be destroyed unless the producing cause is
stripped of power. So those who make the world corruptible plainly deny there
are gods — or, if they say there are gods, they strip divinity of power.

He who made all things through power caused all things to exist along with
himself. And since this power is the greatest possible, not only men and
animals were produced, but gods and daemons as well. And as much as the first
god stands above our nature, by so much must there be more powers set between
him and us — for natures far apart from each other have many connecting
middles.

## 14. How the unchanging gods are said to be angry and appeased

But if anyone, rightly holding that the gods are unchanging, wonders how they
delight in the good but turn from the evil, how they grow angry with the
guilty but are made favorable by due worship — we answer:

Divinity does not delight — for what delights also grieves. It is not angry —
for anger is a passion. It is not appeased by gifts — for then it would be
moved by pleasure. A divine nature cannot rightly be made well or ill
disposed by human affairs. The gods are forever good and kindly, never
harmful, and ever dwell in the same unvarying state of being.

But we, when we are good, are joined to the gods through likeness; when evil,
we are cut off from them through unlikeness. While we live by virtue we share
in the gods; when we become evil we make them our enemies — not that they
are angry, but because guilt keeps us from receiving the light of the gods
and puts us under the power of avenging daemons. But if we gain pardon for
our guilt through prayers and sacrifices, we neither appease the gods nor work
any change in them. By such means, turning back to the divine nature, we heal
our vices and again share in the goodness of the gods.

To say divinity turns away from the evil is the same as saying the sun is
hidden from the blind.

## 15. Why we honor the gods, who need nothing

From this we have the answer to doubts about sacrifices and other matters of
divine worship. What is divine needs nothing. The honors we pay the gods are
performed for *our* benefit.

Since the providence of the gods reaches everywhere, only a certain fitness
is needed to receive their kindly gifts. All fitness comes through imitation
and likeness. So temples imitate the heavens, but altars the earth; statues
resemble life, and so are like animals; prayers imitate what is intellectual;
sacred characters imitate higher unspeakable powers; herbs and stones resemble
matter; and the animals sacrificed resemble the irrational life of our souls.

From all this nothing comes to the gods beyond what they already have — for
what addition can be made to a divine nature? But a union of our souls with
the gods is produced by this means.

## 16. Of sacrifices and other honors — no gain to the gods, gain to men

It will be proper to add a few things about sacrifices. First, since we hold
everything from the gods, it is only just to offer the first fruits of gifts
to the givers: of our possessions through offerings, of our bodies through
adornment, and of our life through sacrifices.

Besides, without sacrifices prayers are only words; with sacrifices they
become *living* words — words strengthen life, and life gives words life. Add
too that the happiness of everything is its proper perfection, and its proper
perfection joins it with its cause — and so we pray to be united with the
gods. Since life dwells first in the gods, and there is also a human life,
and the human life longs to be united with the divine, a middle is needed —
for natures far apart cannot be joined without a middle, and the middle must
be like the natures it joins. Life must therefore be the middle, by means of
life. Hence the happy men of our day, and all the ancients, have sacrificed
animals — and not rashly, but in a manner fitted to each god, with many other
rites of divine worship.

So much concerning sacrifices and the worship of the gods.

## 17. That the world is by nature incorruptible

That the gods will never destroy the world has been said already. The order
of our discourse now requires us to prove that it is by nature
incorruptible.

Whatever is corrupted is corrupted either from itself or from another nature.
If the world were corrupted from itself, fire must burn itself and water dry
itself up. If it may be corrupted by another, it must be either by body or by
what is without body. It cannot be by what is without body: bodiless things,
like nature and soul, *preserve* bodily substances, and nothing is destroyed
by what by nature preserves it.

But if the world may be corrupted by body, it must be either by the bodies
now in it or by others. If by the bodies now in it: either those moving in a
circle must destroy those moving in a straight line, or those moving in a
straight line must destroy the circling ones. But nothing moving in a circle
has a corruptible nature — for why do we never see anything of that kind
corrupted? And things moving in a straight line cannot reach those circling
in an orb — for if they could, why have they never done it to this day?
Neither can natures moving in a straight line destroy one another: the
corruption of one is the generation of another, and this is not destruction
but change alone.

But if the world may be corrupted by other bodies than those it holds, no one
can say where these bodies were generated or where they now exist.

Besides, whatever is corrupted is corrupted either in form or in matter — and
form is shape, matter is body. When forms are corrupted but matter remains,
we see something else generated. But if matter may be corrupted, how is it it
has not failed in so many years? If instead of the corrupted natures others
are produced, they are generated either from beings or from non-beings. If
from beings: since these remain forever, matter must be eternal too. But if
beings suffer corruption, then not only the world but all things will be
corrupted. If matter is generated from non-beings: first, nothing can be
generated from non-being; and even if it could, then as long as non-being
lasted, matter would keep existing — and non-beings can never be destroyed.

If they say matter is without form: first, why does this happen to the whole
world and not to a part? And second, bodies themselves would not be
destroyed, only their beauty.

Besides, whatever is corrupted either dissolves into the natures it is made
of, or vanishes into nothing. If it dissolves into its natures, others will
again be produced — for why was it produced at first? But if beings pass into
nothing, what should stop this happening to divinity itself? If power
prevents it — it is not the property of power to preserve itself alone. By
the same reasoning, being cannot be generated from non-beings, nor vanish
into nothing.

Likewise, the world, if it may be corrupted, must be corrupted either by
nature or against nature. If by nature: then, given its past and present
continuance in being, it would have held what is *against* nature before what
is *according to* nature. If against nature: then some other nature must
exist that can change the nature of the world — and none is anywhere to be
seen.

Besides, whatever can by nature be corrupted, we can also destroy. But no one
has ever destroyed or changed the circling body of the world — while we can
change an earthly body, but cannot destroy it.

Lastly, whatever may be corrupted is changed and aged by time. But through so
long a train of ages the world has stayed without change.

Having said this much for those who need stronger proofs on this matter, we
earnestly beg the world to look kindly on our undertaking.

## 18. Why sacrifices are kept up, and that divinity cannot be harmed

Impiety, which overruns some places of the earth and will often exist in the
future, should not trouble the worthy mind. Things of this kind do not touch
the gods, nor can religious honors profit the gods. And the soul, from its
middle nature, is not always able to pursue the right.

Nor can the whole world share in the providence of the gods in the same way:
some parts enjoy it forever, others in time; some firsthand, others at
secondhand — just as the head perceives by all the senses, but the whole body
by one alone. So, as it seems to me, those who founded the holy days also
appointed unlucky ones: during these some rites are left out and others are
shut away, and things that atone for the weakness of our nature strip certain
rites of their ornament.

It is not unlikely that impiety is itself a kind of punishment. Those who
have known the gods and despised them, we may reasonably suppose, will in
another life be deprived of the knowledge of their nature. And those who have
honored their own earthly rulers as gods shall be cut off from the divinities,
as the punishment of their impiety.

## 19. Why offenders are not punished at once

We should not wonder that offenders of this kind — and others too — are not
punished for their guilt at once. There are not only daemons who punish
offending souls; souls also punish themselves. And those whose guilt fits
them to suffer for all time should not be punished in a small part of time.

Besides, there must be such a thing as human virtue. If the guilty were
punished at once, men would be just from fear and would no longer be
virtuous.

Souls are punished when they leave this body: some by wandering about this
earth, others in certain of its hot and cold regions, others tormented by
avenging daemons. In every case the rational soul suffers punishment together
with the irrational soul, the partner of its guilt — and through this the
shadowy body comes to be, which is seen about graves, and especially about
the tombs of those who lived abandoned lives.

## 20. Of the soul's transmigration, and how rational souls are said to dwell in irrational natures

As for the soul's passing into other bodies: if into rational ones, they
become the souls of particular bodies; if into irrational ones, they attend
them from outside — as our guardian daemons attend us in their kindly
workings. For the rational part never becomes the soul of an irrational
nature.

The truth of this rebirth is shown by what happens at birth: why are some
born blind, others feeble, others with a vicious soul? And since souls are by
nature fitted to do their proper work in bodies, they should not lie idle
forever once they have left them. For if souls did not return into bodies,
either they must be infinite in number, or divinity must keep making new
ones. But nothing actually infinite can exist in the world — for the infinite
can never exist in the finite. Nor can new ones be made: everything in which
something new may be generated is necessarily imperfect — but the world must
be perfect, since it is produced from a perfect nature.

## 21. That the good will be happy, in this life and when they leave it

Souls that live by virtue shall in other respects be happy; and when parted
from the irrational nature and purified from all body, they shall be joined
with the gods and govern the whole world together with the deities who made
it.

And indeed, though none of this should happen to the soul, yet virtue herself
— and the pleasure and honor that come from virtue, together with a life free
from sorrow and from being subject to others — would be enough to make happy
those who choose, and are able to pursue, a life wholly shaped by virtue
itself.
