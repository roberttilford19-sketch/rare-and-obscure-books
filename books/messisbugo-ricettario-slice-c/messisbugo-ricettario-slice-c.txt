## Dedication

**Cristoforo di Messisbugo (d. 1548)** — who he was: steward
(*scalco*) of the House of Este at Ferrara, and a cook of the
Italian Renaissance. The verifiable record gives it plain: from
1524 to 1548 he served at the courts of Alfonso I d'Este and his
son Ercole II at Ferrara, where he ordered many lavish banquets;
greatly esteemed as a master of ceremonies, he was made count
palatine on 20 January 1533 by the Emperor Charles V.

The circumstances of his death: he died in 1548, and his book —
*Banchetti, compositioni di vivande e apparecchio generale* — was
published posthumously at Ferrara in 1549. He is buried at
Sant'Antonio in Polesine.

Why his work is vital — proved, not praised: the record gives one
hard fact that belongs to food history. The first known Italian
description of Beluga caviar — the roe of the great sturgeon of
the Po, which abounded in his century — is in Messisbugo's books:
he described serving it and preserving it. A master who wrote down
how a Renaissance court actually ate — and whose caviar passage is
the first of its kind in his country — needs no praise; the facts
are the monument.

Its relation to this rescue: the Boneyard rescues the working
knowledge of dead masters, and a master cook's working knowledge
is among the most perishable of all — it dies with the kitchen.
The series sets out to give the master himself back his voice,
recipe by recipe, in plain English a working cook can read. This
slice — the pastries, the pies, and the great batches that close
the recipe book — is the last serving: with it, the whole recipe
collection stands rendered.

Where a reader goes next: the 1549 Ferrara edition itself, in the
Internet Archive's digitization (identifier `bub_gb_9yA8AAAAcAAJ`)
— the source of this rendering; the Wikipedia article "Cristoforo
di Messisbugo" for the life in brief; and Slices A and B of this
series, which render the introduction, the table, and the earlier
runs of recipes from the same source.

## How to read this book

Messisbugo writes for a steward's kitchen. His words are kept in
Italian in parentheses at first use:

- *libbra* — the pound (about 12 ounces in Ferrara's measure);
  *oncia* — the ounce; *meza* — half; *scropulo* — scruple;
  *dramma* — dram; *grano* — grain; *ottavo* — an eighth.
- *pignatta, tiella, patella, catino, flamegna* — pot, shallow pan,
  frying pan, basin, baking dish.
- *testo* — the domed cover set over a pan with coals on top: the
  period's oven-lid.
- *buttiero* — butter; *dileguito/strutto* — rendered fat, lard;
  *zucchero* — sugar; *mele* — honey (not apples); *agrefto* —
  verjuice, the sour juice of unripe grapes; *acqua rosata* — rose
  water.
- *pasta reale* — "royal paste": the enriched dough; *sfoglia* — a
  sheet of paste; *pastume* — the filling-mixture; *battuto* — the
  beaten/minced mixture, the forcemeat.
- *speronella* — the spur-wheel pastry cutter; *buffolo* — the
  round cutter; *rotello* — the little edge-wheel for crimping;
  *stampa* — a stamp or mold; *ferri* — the irons (shaped molds
  for frying).
- *inrosellare* — to glaze (with yolk); *pizzicare* — to pinch the
  edge shut.
- *da grasso / da magro* — "of fat" / "of lean": feast-day food
  (meat, butter, eggs) and fast-day food (fish, oil, vegetables).
- *imbandire* — to dish up, to set on the board.

Bracketed notes in the form [Editor's note] are the editor's, not
Messisbugo's. Where the scan defeats the reading, the English
marks it [obscured in the scan] rather than guessing.

## Note on the Italian text

The source is the raw OCR of a 1549 printing, and it shows its age:
the long *s* prints as *f*, *u* and *v* interchange, and the
digitization mangles letters throughout (*zucchero* as *Zuccharo*,
*pevere* as *Peuere*, *battuto* as *battume*). The translation was
made by reading through the noise to the Italian beneath; uncertain
readings are flagged, never smoothed over.

Three transmission scars are left visible. First, the slice opens
mid-book and several recipe-headings are lost where the scan
fails: those passages are rendered as fragments, as found, under a
plain description of what the heading seems to have been. Second,
the pages of the scan are out of order in the closing section: the
method-tail of the *brazzatelle* (poach, then bake) appears pages
before the *brazzatelle* heading and its ingredients, and the
*offelle* recipe breaks off into what reads as a separate spiced
composition for the little pastry boxes. The order of the lines is
kept as the scan gives it, and the scrambling is said out loud
where it matters. Third, the slice ends on a duplicate: the last
thirty-odd lines repeat the master's introduction to the recipes
— the same passage rendered whole in Slice A — a repeated page in
the scan, not a new recipe. It is described, not translated twice.
The known duplicate blocks at OCR lines 1555–1983 and 6872–7283
fall outside this slice and are not rendered here.

## The recipes

### To make ten dishes of *maricondi* [the heading prints as MARICON* — the tail of the word lost]

Take three pounds of fat cheese, and of hard cheese one
[measure — obscured], grated, and [obscured — reads like raisins]
two pounds, and thirty egg yolks, and [obscured], one [pound] of
sugar, one pound of butter, fresh [cream (?) — obscured], and one
ounce of fine cinnamon, ground (?), and a grated white loaf; pound
everything well together in the mortar. And take the said
*pastume*, put it in a baking dish (*flamegna*), and press it in a
little press (*torchietto*) as much as possible; and according as
it shall be well pressed, the juice out — not being able to have
[the press — obscured], what remains inside you shall cut the
baking dish around (?), and then the said *pastume* you shall cut
in slices, large or small, as seems good to you; then fry them in
three pounds of fresh butter; then, dishing them, put on top six
ounces of sugar. Of this dish you may serve yourself in place of a
pastry (*pasta*) — and it is good for filling little boxes
(*casse*), St. James's [shells (?)], fleur-de-lis, or such things
of paste.

[Editor's note — in plain words: A pressed cheese composition —
fat cheese, hard cheese, raisins, thirty yolks, sugar, butter,
cinnamon, grated white bread — pounded, pressed till the juice
runs, sliced, fried in butter, and sugared: served as a pastry in
its own right, and good for filling little pastry boxes and
shell-shaped cases. The Ferrarese *mariconda* by another spelling;
it returns below as a filling for the German paste.]

### To make ten dishes of fat *tortelletti*, of another sort

Take half a capon of flesh, half a pound of hard grated cheese,
and a pound of fat cheese, and a pound of pork belly (*pecetta di
porco*), boiled, fat and good; pound everything well together, and
put it in a vessel, adding then ten eggs, and a little of minced
oily herbs with knives, half an ounce of cinnamon, half an ounce
each of cloves and ginger, pepper — of the one as much as of the
other — mix everything well together, and make a *battuto*; then
make a thin sheet as is said above in the other, and make your
little *tortelletti* as big as a hazelnut, with the *gussa* (?)
[obscured], or a little more; then put them to cook in good fat
broth, adding a little saffron to give them the yellow, and let
them boil for the space of a *Miserere*. And then dish them,
putting on top grated hard cheese, and cinnamon, and sugar.

These can also be made without the sugar inside.

[Editor's note — in plain words: Hazelnut-size tortelletti of
capon, two cheeses, and boiled pork belly, with ten eggs, herbs,
cinnamon, cloves, ginger, and pepper — boiled in fat broth just
long enough to say a Miserere, saffroned yellow, and served under
cheese, cinnamon, and sugar.]

### To make *pastatelle* of meat, for ten dishes

Take four pounds of pork, or of lean veal, and cook it boiled till
it is well done; then take a pound of pork belly, well fatty, or
of veal, and cook it boiled, well done; if you cannot have pork
belly, add half a pound of beef fat, and pound it well with
knives. Then take two pounds of hard grated cheese, and ten eggs,
and a pound of sugar, and a pound of raisins, and half an ounce of
fine cinnamon, and half an ounce of pepper; mix everything
together, as is done for the *battuto* of a *torta*. Then have your
royal paste, and make your sheets, and cut them with the wheel
(*buffolo*); then fill them with *battuto*, and make your
*pastatelle* with their little wheel-edge (*rotello*), as is said
in the *Arte* (?) above; then fry them in lard (*dileguito*), or in
butter, gently, gently, so they do not crack; or cook them in the
*tiella*, or in the oven, or under the *testo*, giving butter
below and above; and when they are cooked and for dishing, give
them sugar on top.

And of this *battume* you may also make a good *torta*, following
the order as the other *torte* are made.

[Editor's note — in plain words: Big meat pastries for ten dishes
— boiled pork or veal with pork belly (or beef fat), hard cheese,
ten eggs, sugar, raisins, cinnamon, pepper, made up like a tart
filling, sealed in royal paste cut with the wheel and crimped,
fried gently in lard or butter (or baked), and sugared. The same
filling makes a good tart.]

### To make ten dishes of excellent fat *tortelletti*, to serve them even alone

Take two pounds of beef fat, or of veal, and when it is well

Then take twelve well-beaten eggs, and two and a half pounds of
good hard grated cheese, and one and a half ounces of ground
cinnamon, and a pound and a half of raisins, and a pound of sugar;
mix everything well together into a *pastume* that is good. Then
make the paste with flour, and saffron, and one egg, and make the
sheets very thin; then make your [tortelletti — the recipe breaks
off in the scan; the shaping and cooking are lost].

[Editor's note — in plain words: A rich tortelletto whose filling
is built on strained rendered beef or veal fat — twelve eggs,
hard cheese, cinnamon, raisins, sugar — in saffroned sheets. The
scan drops the end of the method.]

### To make ten dishes of *guanti*, or little nets (*reticelle*)

Take two and a half pounds of white flour, four ounces of sugar,

[Editor's note — in plain words: "Gloves" — hand-crimped fried

### To make a Catalan *sfogliata*, or ten [sfogliatine (?)]

The Catalan *sfogliata*, or little *sfogliatine*, which are made
only for variety, are composed in this way: dissolving the
aforesaid paste, in the kneading put juice of chard, or of
[obscured — a green herb], so it is made green. When the sheet is
spread, before it is rolled, put three ounces of raisins inside...
[the recipe breaks off in the scan].

[Editor's note — in plain words: A green Catalan puff pastry —
the paste greened with chard juice (or another green) in the
kneading, raisins laid in before rolling. The scan drops the

### [*Brazzatelle* — the method-tail; the heading and ingredients stand later in the scan]

...of butter, ounces [obscured]; work (*gramola*) all these things
very well together. Then make your *brazzatelle* according to the
order they are made, and let them rise (*lettare*) with great
diligence. After they are risen, boil your water, and throw the
said *brazzatelle* in to cook; when they come up on top, take them
out, and put them in fresh water; when [obscured] take them out
and put them to cook in the oven; and if you would put aniseeds in
them, it will be good work.

[Editor's note — in plain words: The *brazzatelle* are
double-cooked like bagels: risen with care, poached in boiling
water till they float, refreshed in cold water, then baked —
aniseed in the dough if you like. The head of the recipe follows

### To make *sosamelli*, most perfect, number 36

Take [obscured] of white flour, and [obscured] of pounded sugar,
well passed through the sieve, and [obscured] ounces of fine ground
cinnamon, and two pinches of ground pepper, and three yolks of
egg, and one with the white, and one eighth of long pepper, rose
water mixed with a little salt; with diligence make your paste a
little hard, and work it very well. Then have the stamp; make what
you wish to seem (?); putting a little musk, they will be better.
Then put them to cook in the oven on a small board (?).

[Editor's note — in plain words: Thirty-six little stamped cakes
— hard-worked paste of flour, sieved sugar, cinnamon, pepper,
long pepper, yolks, and salted rose water, the better with a
touch of musk, baked on a board.]

### To make *mostazzolli* of sugar

### *COMPOSITIONE DELLE PIU IMPORTANTI VIVANDE* — Composition of the most important dishes

#### First: to make fifty loaves (*pani*) of milk and sugar, of nine ounces each

After you have your leaven risen (*sconco* (?) *levaturo*), take
of flower of sifted flour pounds [obscured] — and so much less as
less was that of which you made the leaven — and pounds
[obscured] of well-white sugar, yolks of egg [obscured], and
pounds [obscured] of rose water, and pounds [obscured] of fresh
milk, and ounces [obscured] of fresh butter; knead your *pane*, and
note well that the water, or milk, do not scald; and make also
that the yolks of egg be warmed (?), and warm them putting them in
hot water; put in convenient salt; make the paste so it is neither
hard nor tender, but rather that it have of the firm (?); work
(*gramola*) it very well; then make your bread, and let it rise
well, and cook it with great order, so it does not take too much
fire, but that at your judgment it be good. And this bread is more
beautiful made round than in *torto* (?), or in *pintoni* (?), be
it afterward larger or smaller, as you wish. Govern yourself then
according to this way, which is proved.

## Glossary of measures and pantry words

- *agresto/agrefto* — verjuice: the sour juice of unripe grapes.
- *battuto* — the beaten/minced mixture; the forcemeat.
- *bicchiere* — a cup or glass; here, a small cup-shaped pastry
  (as printed).
- *bracciatella* — a little arm-band: a ring-shaped pastry.
- *brazzatella* — a ring-bread, poached then baked; aniseed in the
  dough if you like.
- *buffetti* — white bread rolls (their crumb soaks for the
  *pizze sfogliate*).
- *buffolo* — the round pastry cutter.
- *buttiero* — butter; *dileguito/strutto* — rendered fat, lard.
- *cassetta* — a little pastry box or shell.
- *cialdone* — a large thin rolled wafer; the model for rolling.
- *cibibo damaschino* — Damascus raisins.
- *dramma, scropulo/scrupolo, grano, ottavo* — dram, scruple,
  grain, eighth (of an ounce).
- *fiadone* — a large pie; *fiadoncello* — a small one.
- *fiasco* — flask; a liquid measure.
- *flamegna* — a baking dish.
- *gramolare* — to work/knead dough hard.
- *grossetto/grossetta* — rather thick (of a sheet).
- *inrosellare* — to glaze with yolk (and color).
- *lettare* — to rise (of dough).
- *libbra* — pound (about 12 ounces, Ferrara); *oncia* — ounce;
  *meza* — half.
- *mandole ambrosine* — bitter almonds ("Ambrosian" almonds).
- *mantegata* — a "creamed" cake or dish.
- *mariconda* — the Ferrarese pressed-cheese composition; also a
  filling.
- *marzapane* — marzipan.
- *mele* — honey (in this book; not apples).
- *morsello* — a sweet morsel, a comfit.
- *morolla* — marrow (beef marrow).
- *mostazzolo* — a spiced honey-and-sugar cake.
- *offella* — a cheese pastry folded with butter like puff paste.
- *pampapati* — the Ferrarese spiced bread (the baking model for
  the *mostazzoli*).
- *pastatella* — a little pastry, a pie.
- *pastume* — the filling-mixture.
- *pasta reale* — "royal paste": the enriched sweet dough.
- *pecetta di porco* — pork belly.
- *pegna* (?) — as printed in the *truffolo* heading; obscure.
- *persutto* — ham (as printed; *prosciutto*).
- *pignatta* — a cooking pot; *tiella* — a shallow pan; *padella*
  — a frying pan; *catino* — a basin; *testo* — the domed lid with
  coals on top.
- *pizzicare* — to pinch the edge shut.
- *ponina* (?) — as printed; likely a fresh cheese.
- *povina* (?) — as printed; likely a fresh cheese.
- *reticella* — a little net: a lattice of pastry.
- *ritortello* — a rolled pastry.
- *rosella* — a glaze (for finishing).
- *rosone* — a rose-shaped pastry.
- *rotella* — a little wheel: a coil, or the crimping wheel.
- *sapore* — the savor, the seasoned sauce.
- *scartozzo* — a paper cone (for keeping spices).
- *sfoglia* — a sheet of paste; *sfogliata* — the puff/leaf
  pastry; *sfogliatina* — a little one; *sgrostata* (?) — as
  printed; likely "crusted."
- *speronella* — the spur-wheel pastry cutter.
- *stampa* — a stamp or mold; *ferri* — the shaped frying-irons.
- *stamigna* — the straining cloth.
- *stellina/stelletta* — a little star pastry.
- *suolo* — a layer (of a built pastry).
- *tegola* — a tile; here, a long flat pastry.
- *torciglione/tortiglione* — a twist (of paste).
- *torta* — a tart or pie.
- *tortella brusca* — a tart (sharp) little tart.
- *truffolo* — a little "truffle": a die of fried paste for
  building.
- *tumino* — a cheese, sliced for layering.
- *acqua rosata* — rose water.
- *da grasso / da magro* — for fat days (meat, butter, eggs) / for
  lean days (fish, oil, vegetables).
- *imbandire* — to dish up for the table.
