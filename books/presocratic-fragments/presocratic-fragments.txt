## DEDICATION

To **Hermann Diels (1848–1922)** and **John Burnet (1863–1928)**.

Diels spent his working life gathering these scattered sentences — quotations tucked into Plato, Aristotle, and the doxographers — and set them in order as *Die Fragmente der Vorsokratiker*: lost books rebuilt, in a literal sense, out of other people's footnotes. Without that patient labour there would be no Heraclitus and no Parmenides on any shelf.

Burnet, the Scots classicist of St Andrews, carried the fragments across into English in *Early Greek Philosophy*, and put them within reach of readers who would never learn Greek. This book stands on both men's shoulders, and claims nothing beside them.

## How this book was made (please read)

- **Base text:** John Burnet, *Early Greek Philosophy*, 2nd edition (1908), as printed on Project Gutenberg ebook #67097 (marked not in copyright). Burnet prints the fragments in English, arranged after Diels.
- **What this is:** Burnet's Edwardian English turned into plain modern English — the kind of plain a humble peasant could understand. No "thee," no "thou," no "thy." The meaning follows Burnet throughout; where his wording was already plain, it is kept close.
- **What this is not:** It is not translated fresh from the Greek. It is not the work of a classical scholar. Anyone who reads Greek should go to Burnet himself, to Diels-Kranz, or to a modern critical edition.
- **Numbers:** Every fragment carries its standard **Diels-Kranz (DK)** number, so it can be looked up in any modern edition. Where Burnet's own numbering differs from DK's — as it does throughout his Heraclitus, where he follows Bywater's older numbering — Burnet's number is given alongside in (parentheses). Nothing has been silently renumbered. A handful of fragments could not be matched to a DK number with confidence; those are flagged honestly in the text rather than guessed at.
- **Gaps and marks:** Burnet's line-count markers and footnote tags have been dropped as clutter. Where Burnet marks a gap with "...", the gap is kept. Burnet's own square-bracket insertions are kept in [brackets]; the drafter's own brief glosses also appear in [brackets] — the two uses are not distinguished, so a bracketed phrase may be Burnet's or the modernizer's.
- **No crowns:** No claim is made here — none — that this is the first, the oldest, the most complete, or the definitive rendering of these fragments. Heraclitus and Parmenides are among the best-served authors in classical scholarship, with many fine modern translations. This is a draft, nothing more, and it does not compete with any of them.

# PART I. HERACLITUS OF EPHESUS

Heraclitus of Ephesus (fl. c. 500 BC) wrote a single book — its title is unknown, the book itself is lost — in a dense, oracular prose that earned him the nickname "the Dark." What survives are some 130 quoted scraps, gathered from Plato, Aristotle, Clement, Hippolytus, Plutarch, and others.

Burnet prints the fragments in the arrangement and numbering of Ingram Bywater's edition (1877): three groups — (1)–(90), (91)–(97), (98)–(130). Bywater numbers (9), (63), and (128) do not exist (no fragment was assigned to them); his (33) is a cross-reference, not a fragment; his (56) simply repeats (45). Each entry below gives the **DK number first**, then Burnet's (Bywater) number.

# PART II. PARMENIDES OF ELEA

Parmenides of Elea (fl. c. 500 BC) was the first philosopher to set out his system in verse — a stern hexameter poem of which the opening survives nearly whole, along with long stretches of its argument. Burnet says he follows Diels's arrangement; but note that **Burnet's numbering of Parmenides (2), (3), (4, 5) does not match the now-standard DK order** — his (2) is DK 28B4, his (3) is DK 28B5, and his (4, 5) are DK 28B2 and 28B3. Both numbers are given below. Burnet omits DK 28B18 (a Latin retranslation, not Greek).

## The Way of Truth

- **DK 28B1** (Burnet 1). *The proem — the journey to the goddess.*

  The car that carries me bore me as far as my heart ever desired, for it brought me and set me on the famed road of the goddess, which alone leads the knowing man through all things. Along that road I was borne — the wise horses drew my car, and maidens showed the way. The axle glowed in its socket, driven round by the whirling wheels at either end, and sang like a pipe — when the daughters of the Sun, hurrying to bring me into the light, drew their veils from their faces and left the house of Night.

  There stand the gates of the roads of Night and Day, with a lintel above and a stone threshold below. The gates themselves tower in the sky, barred by mighty doors — and avenging Justice holds the keys that fit them. The maidens coaxed her with soft words and cleverly persuaded her to slide back the bolted bar from the gates without demur. The doors swung back and opened wide, their bronze posts turning one after another on rivets and pins. Straight through them, along the broad road, the maidens guided horses and car — and the goddess greeted me kindly, took my right hand in hers, and spoke these words to me:

  "Welcome, young man, who come to my house on the car that bears you, tended by immortal charioteers! No ill fate sent you travelling this road — it was right and justice. Far indeed it lies from the beaten track of men. It is right that you should learn all things: both the unshaken heart of well-rounded truth, and the opinions of mortals, in which there is no true belief at all. Yet you shall learn these too — how the things that seem to them should have seemed to them as they do — as you travel through all things on your journey."

  * * * * *

  But hold your thought back from this road of inquiry. Do not let habit, with all its experience, force you to cast a wandering eye, a listening ear, or a tongue along this road. Judge by argument the much-argued proof I have spoken. There is only one road left that can be spoken of. ...

  *(The closing lines of Burnet's (1) — "hold your thought back..." — belong in DK's ordering to the "restrain your thought" passage of DK 28B7, printed again as Burnet's (7) below. The words are given here exactly as Burnet arranges them.)*

- **DK 28B4** (Burnet 2). Look steadily with your mind at things, even when far off, as if they were close at hand. You cannot cut off what-is from clinging to what-is — neither scattering it abroad everywhere in order, nor gathering it together.

- **DK 28B5** (Burnet 3). It is all one to me where I begin, for I shall come back there again.

- **DK 28B2 + 28B3** (Burnet 4, 5). Come now, I will tell you — listen to my saying and carry it away — the only two roads of inquiry there are to think of. The first — that *It is*, and that it cannot *not* be — is the road of belief, for truth goes with it. The other — that *It is not*, and that it must *not* be — this, I tell you, is a path no one can ever learn. For you cannot know what-is-not — that is impossible — nor can you speak of it. [B3:] For thinking and being are the same thing.

- **DK 28B6** (Burnet 6). What can be thought and spoken of must *be* — for it can be, and what-is-nothing cannot be. Ponder this, as I bid you. I hold you back from this first road of inquiry — and from that other one too, on which mortals who know nothing wander two-faced. Helplessness steers their straying thought in their breasts, and they are carried along, dazed, like the deaf and blind. Undiscerning crowds! In their eyes, being and not-being are the same and not the same — and all things travel in opposite directions!

- **DK 28B7** (Burnet 7). For this shall never be proved: that things that are not, are. Hold your thought back from this road of inquiry.

- **DK 28B8** (Burnet 8). One road only is left to speak of: that *It is*. On it there are many signposts, showing that what-is is unborn and undying — for it is whole, unmoved, and without end. It never was and never will be, for it *is* now — all at once, one continuous whole. What birth will you look for? How, and from where, could it have grown? I will not let you say or think it came from what-is-not — for it cannot be thought or spoken that anything *is not*. And if it came from nothing, what need could have made it arise later rather than sooner? So it must either be altogether, or not at all. The force of truth will not let anything arise, besides itself, from what-is-not. That is why Justice does not loosen her chains to let anything come to be or pass away — she holds it fast. Our judgment rests on this: "*Is it, or is it not?*" It is judged, as it must be, that we set aside the one road as unthinkable and nameless (for it is no true road), and that the other road is real and true. How, then, can what-is be going to be in the future? How could it have come to be? If it came to be, it is not — nor is it, if it is going to be in the future. So coming-to-be is silenced, and passing-away is not to be heard of.

  Nor is it divisible, since it is all alike. There is no more of it in one place than in another to keep it from holding together, and no less — everything is full of what-is. So it is wholly continuous, for what-is touches what-is.

  Moreover, it is unmoved in the bonds of mighty chains, without beginning and without end, since coming-to-be and passing-away have been driven far off, and true belief has cast them out. It is the same, and rests in the same place, abiding in itself. So it stays constant in its place, for hard necessity keeps it in the bonds of the limit that holds it fast on every side. What-is may not be boundless — for it needs nothing, while if it were boundless it would need everything.

  The thing that can be thought, and the thought for whose sake it exists, are the same. For you cannot find thought without something that is, which the thought is about. There is not, and never shall be, anything besides what-is, since fate has chained it to be whole and unmoved. So all these things are only names that mortals have given, believing them true: coming-to-be and passing-away, being and not-being, change of place and shift of bright colour.

  Since it has a farthest limit, it is complete on every side, like the bulk of a well-rounded ball, balanced equally from the centre in every direction. For it cannot be greater in one place and smaller in another. There is no nothing that could stop it from reaching out equally, and what-is cannot be more here and less there, since it is all untouchable. From its centre it reaches equally in every direction to the limits.

## The Way of Opinion

Here I close my trustworthy speech and thought about the truth. From here on, learn the opinions of mortals, listening to the deceitful ordering of my words.

Mortals have made up their minds to name two forms — one of which they should not have named — and that is where they stray from the truth. They set them apart as opposites in form, and gave them marks different from each other. To the one they gave the bright fire of heaven — gentle, very light, everywhere the same as itself but not the same as the other. The other is just the opposite: dark night, a dense and heavy body. I tell you the whole arrangement of these as it seems likely, so that no thought of mortals shall ever outrun you.

- **DK 28B9** (Burnet 9). Now that all things have been named light and night, and the names that belong to the power of each have been given to these things and those, everything is full of light and dark night at once — both equal, since neither has anything to do with the other.

- **DK 28B10 + 28B11** (Burnet 10, 11). And you shall know the substance of the sky, and all the signs in the sky, and the shining works of the glowing sun's pure torch, and where they came from. - **DK 28B12** (Burnet 12). The narrower rings are filled with unmixed fire, and those next to them with night, and through the middle of these rushes their share of fire. ## 1. The Milesians

### Thales of Miletus

Burnet is blunt: strictly speaking, we know nothing of the teaching of Thales at first hand — no line of his survives. What follows is Burnet's own **conjectural** boiling-down of Aristotle's reports, numbered (1)–(3) for convenience. These are not fragments; they are educated guesses, and are labelled as such.

- **(1) [conjectural]** The earth floats on water.
- **(2) [conjectural]** Water is the material stuff of all things.
- **(3) [conjectural]** All things are full of gods. The magnet is alive, because it has the power to move iron.

### Anaximander of Miletus

One genuine sentence survives, quoted by Theophrastus (DK 12B1). Burnet gives it in quotation marks inside Theophrastus's report:

- **B1.** "Things pay penalty and make amends to one another for their wrongdoing, according to the fixed order of time" — for they rise out of, and pass back into, the source they came from, as is ordained.

*(Burnet's fuller wording: "And into that from which things take their rise they pass away once more, 'as is ordained; for they make reparation and satisfaction to one another for their injustice according to the appointed time.'")*

### Anaximenes of Miletus

No numbered fragments in Burnet — only reports. The most famous saying, quoted by Aetius (DK 13B2), runs:

- **[B2 — reported saying]** "Just as our soul, being air, holds us together, so breath and air surround the whole world."

The doxographical reports Burnet prints most fully (from Theophrastus via Simplicius, and from Hippolytus) say:

- Air is the one boundless underlying stuff; all things come from it by thinning out and packing together. Thinned, it becomes fire; packed, it becomes wind, then cloud, then water, then earth, then stones.
- Air is always moving — if it stood still, nothing would change.
- "From it, he said, the things that are, and have been, and shall be, the gods and things divine, took their rise."

Burnet prints passages under 141 entries (133 singly-numbered headings including B27a, plus 8 of Burnet's combined printings), covering Diels numbers 1 through 148, except Diels 149 and 150, which he does not quote. **B1.** Listen, Pausanias, son of wise Anchitos!

**B2.** The powers spread through men's bodily parts are cramped, and crowds of troubles break in and blunt their careful thinking. They see only a brief stretch of a life that is no life, and, doomed to die quickly, they are lifted and fly off like smoke. Each man is sure only of what he happens to stumble on, as he is hurried back and forth, and idly boasts he has found the whole truth. These things are so hard for men's eyes to see, for their ears to hear, for their minds to grasp! But you — since you have found your way here — will learn no more than a mortal mind can hold.

**B3.** ...to keep it locked in your silent heart.

**B4.** O gods, turn away from my tongue the madness of those men! Make my lips holy and let a pure stream flow from them! And you, much-wanted, white-armed virgin Muse — I beg you, let me hear what is lawful for the children of a day to hear! Set me on my road from the house of Holiness, and drive my willing chariot! Let no garlands of glory and honour from mortal hands tempt you to lift men off the ground on condition that you speak proudly beyond what is lawful and right, just to win a seat on the heights of wisdom.

Come now, use every power you have to see how each thing stands clear. Do not trust your sight more than your hearing, and do not set your echoing ear above the plain teaching of your tongue. Do not hold back trust from any other part of your body that gives understanding an opening. Weigh everything by the way it stands clear.

**B5.** It is always the way of small minds to disbelieve their betters. Learn what my Muse's sure testimony bids you learn, turning the argument over in your heart.

**B6.** Hear first the four roots of all things: shining Zeus, life-giving Hera, Aidoneus, and Nestis, whose tears are a spring of water for mortals. *(Burnet's note: the four elements are introduced here under the names of gods; the ancient commentators themselves disagreed about which god stood for which element.)*

**B7.** ...uncreated....

**B8.** I will tell you something else. Nothing that dies ever comes into being, and death does not end anything. There is only mixing, and changing of what was mixed. "Coming into being" is just a name men give it.

**B9.** When the elements are mixed into the shape of a man and come into the daylight, or into the shape of wild beasts or plants or birds, men say these things "come into being." When they are separated, men call it woeful death. They name it wrongly — but I follow custom too, and call it the same myself.

**B10.** Avenging death.

**B11–12** *(Burnet prints 11 and 12 as one passage.)* Fools! — they have no far-reaching thoughts — who believe that what never was before comes into being, or that anything can die and be utterly destroyed. Nothing can arise from what in no way is, and it is impossible, unheard of, that what *is* should perish. It will always *be*, wherever you keep putting it.

**B13.** In the All there is nothing empty and nothing over-full.

**B14.** In the All there is nothing empty. So where could anything come from to add to it?

**B15.** A man who understands these things would never suppose in his heart that, for as long as mortals live the life they call life, they *are* — with their good and bad fortune — while before they were put together and after they dissolve, they are simply nothing at all.

**B16.** Of a truth, Strife and Love existed before and will exist hereafter. Boundless time, I think, will never be emptied of that pair.

**B17.** I will tell you a twofold tale. At one time it grew to be one alone out of many; at another, it split apart to be many out of one. There is a double coming-into-being of dying things, and a double passing away. The coming together of all things both brings one generation into being and destroys it; the other generation grows up and is scattered as things divide. These things never stop changing places: at one time all uniting into one through Love, at another each carried a different way by Strife's repulsion. Insofar as it is their nature to grow into one out of many, and to become many again when the one is torn apart, they come into being and their life does not last. But insofar as they never stop shifting places, they are forever unmoving as they circle round existence.

* * *

But come, listen to my words — learning is what increases wisdom. As I said before, when I laid out the headings of my discourse, I will tell you a twofold tale. At one time it grew together to be one alone out of many; at another it parted to be many out of one: Fire and Water and Earth and the mighty height of Air; dread Strife too, apart from these, matching each in weight; and Love among them, matching them in length and breadth. Look at her with your mind — do not sit with dazed eyes. She is the one known as planted in mortal frames. She makes men think loving thoughts and do the works of peace. They call her Joy and Aphrodite. No mortal man has yet seen her circling among them — but attend to the straight ordering of my tale.

For all these are equal and the same age, yet each holds a different office and has its own nature. Nothing comes into being besides these, and they do not pass away. If they were always passing away, they would not exist now — and what could add to this All, and where from? How could it perish, when no place is empty of these things? They are what they are; but, running through one another, they become now this, now that, and like things forevermore.

**B18.** Love.

**B19.** Clinging Love.

**B20.** This contest of Love and Strife shows plainly in the mass of mortal limbs. At one time all the limbs that make up the body are brought together by Love in blooming life's high season; at another, torn apart by cruel Strife, they wander each alone along the breakers of life's sea. It is the same with plants, with fish that make their homes in the water, with beasts that den on the hills, and with seabirds that sail on wings.

**B21.** Come now, look at the witnesses to my earlier tale, in case my earlier list fell short in describing their form. See the sun, everywhere bright and warm, and all the undying things bathed in heat and bright radiance. See the rain, everywhere dark and cold. From the earth come things packed tight and solid. When in strife, all these are different in form and separate; but they come together in love and long for one another.

For out of these have sprung all things that were and are and will be — trees and men and women, beasts and birds and fish that live in the water, yes, and the long-lived gods, exalted in honour.

For these things are what they are; but, running through one another, they take different shapes — so much does mixing change them.

**B22.** All of these — sun, earth, sky, sea — are at one with all their parts scattered far and wide in mortal things. And so, things that mix well are like one another and are united in love by Aphrodite. Things that differ most in origin, mixture, and the stamp on each are the most hostile — wholly unused to uniting, and grieving at Strife's command, since Strife made their birth what it is.

**B23.** Just as painters making temple-offerings — men well taught in their art by wisdom — take pigments of many colours in their hands, mix them in due measure, more of some and less of others, and from them make shapes like all things: trees and men and women, beasts and birds and fish that live in the water, yes, and long-lived gods exalted in honour — so let error not master your mind into thinking there is any other source of all the countless dying creatures we see. Know this for certain, for you have heard the tale from a goddess.

**B24.** Climbing from peak to peak — not to walk one path only, to the end....

**B25.** What is right may well be said even twice.

**B26.** They rule in turn as the circle comes round, pass into one another, and grow great each in its appointed turn.

So: insofar as they grow into one out of many, and then divide and become more than one, they come into being and their life does not last. But insofar as they never stop shifting, they are forevermore, unmoving in the circle.

Fire, longing to reach its like, sent these up — showing as yet neither the lovely shape of women's limbs, nor the voice and parts that belong to men.

**B63.** ...But the stuff of the child's limbs is divided between the parents — part in the man's body, part in the woman's.

**B64.** Desire came upon him, reminding him through sight.

**B65.** ...It was poured out in the pure parts, and when it met with cold, women arose from it.

**B66.** The divided meadows of Aphrodite.

**B67.** In its warmer part the womb brings forth males — that is why men are dark, and manlier, and shaggy.

**B68.** On the tenth day of the eighth month, the white putrefaction arises. *(Burnet's note: the Greek word means "pus"; Aristotle reports that Empedocles regarded milk as putrefied blood, and there may be a punning allusion to "beestings," the first milk.)*

**B69.** Double bearing. *(Burnet's note: said of women, with reference to births in the seventh and ninth months.)*

**B70.** Sheepskin. *(Burnet's note: the membrane round the unborn child.)*

**B71.** But if your grasp of these things fell short anywhere — of how, out of Water and Earth and Air and Fire mingled together, arose the shapes and colours of all those mortal things fitted together by Aphrodite, and so now come into being....

**B72.** How tall trees and the fishes in the sea....

**B73.** Even as at that time Kypris, making warmth ready, after wetting Earth with water, gave it to swift fire to harden....

**B74.** Leading the songless tribe of fertile fish.

**B75.** All those that are dense within and rare without, having got moisture of this kind at Kypris's hands....

**B76.** You may see this in the heavy-backed shellfish that live in the sea, in sea-snails and stony-skinned turtles. In them you see the earthy part lying on the outermost surface.

**B77–78** *(printed as one passage by Burnet).* It is air that makes evergreen trees flourish with plenty of fruit all year round.
**B79.** So first of all, tall olive trees bear eggs....

**B80.** That is why pomegranates are late-born, and apples full of juice.

**B81.** Wine is water from the bark, rotted in the wood.

**B82.** Hair and leaves, the thick feathers of birds, and the scales that grow on mighty limbs are the same thing.

**B83.** The hedgehog's hair is sharp-pointed and bristles on its back.

**B84.** As when a man setting out through a stormy night gets ready a lantern — a flame of blazing fire — and fits horn plates round it to keep out every kind of wind: the plates scatter the blast of the winds, but the light leaps out through them and shines across the threshold with unfailing beams, as much of it as is finer. In the same way Love then trapped the primal fire — the round pupil — shut within membranes and delicate tissues, pierced through and through with wondrous passages. They keep out the deep water surrounding the pupil, but let through the fire, as much of it as is finer.

**B85.** The gentle flame of the eye has only a scanty share of earth.

**B86.** Out of these, divine Aphrodite fashioned untiring eyes.

**B87.** Aphrodite fitting these together with rivets of love.

**B88.** One vision comes from both eyes together.

**B89.** Know that streamings-off flow from all things that have come into being.

**B90.** Sweet lays hold of sweet, bitter rushes to bitter; acid comes to acid, and warm couples with warm.

**B91.** Water fits better into wine, but will not mix with oil.

**B92.** Brass mixed with tin.

**B93.** The blue elder's berry is mingled with scarlet.

**B94.** The black colour at a river's bottom comes from the shadow. The same is seen in hollow caves.

**B95.** Since the eyes first grew together in Kypris's hands.

**B96.** Kindly earth took into its broad funnels two parts of gleaming Nestis (water) out of the eight, and four of Hephaistos (fire). So arose white bones, divinely fitted together by the cement of proportion.

**B97.** The spine was broken.

**B98.** Earth, anchoring in Aphrodite's perfect harbours, meets with these in nearly equal measure — with Hephaistos and Water and gleaming Air: either a little more of earth, or less of the others and more of it. From these arose blood and the manifold shapes of flesh.

**B99.** The bell... the fleshy sprout of the ear.

**B100.** This is how all things draw breath in and breathe it out. All have bloodless tubes of flesh stretched over the surface of their bodies, and at the mouths of these the outermost skin is pierced all over with closely packed pores — enough to keep the blood in, while a free passage is cut for air to pass through. When the thin blood ebbs back from these, the bubbling air rushes in with a surging flood; when the blood runs back, the air is breathed out again. It is like a girl playing with a water-clock of shining brass: she puts the pipe's mouth on her pretty hand and dips the clock in the yielding mass of silvery water — the stream does not flow into the vessel then, because the mass of air inside, pressing on the close-packed holes, keeps it out until she uncovers the pinched stream; then air escapes and an equal bulk of water runs in. In the same way, when water fills the depths of the bronze vessel and the opening and passage are stopped by a human hand, the air outside, striving to get in, holds the water back at the gates of the ill-sounding neck, pressing on its surface, until she lets go with her hand. Then — just the opposite of before — the wind rushes in and an equal bulk of water runs out to make room. Even so, when the thin blood surging through the limbs rushes back inward, straightway the stream of air comes in with a rushing swell; but when the blood returns, the air breathes out again in equal measure.

**B101.** The dog with its nostrils tracking the scraps of the beast's limbs, and the scent their feet leave in the soft grass.

**B102.** All things have their share of breath and smell.

**B103–104** *(printed as one passage by Burnet).* So all things have thought, by fortune's will.... And insofar as the rarest things came together as they fell....

**B105.** The heart, dwelling in the sea of blood that runs in opposite directions — that is where men's thought chiefly is, for the blood round the heart is the thought of men.

**B106.** Men's wisdom grows according to what is set before them.

**B107.** Out of these all things are formed and fitted together, and by these men think and feel pleasure and pain.

**B108.** Just as far as they grow different, different thoughts keep coming into their minds — in dreams.

**B109.** With earth we see Earth, with water Water; by air we see bright Air, by fire destroying Fire. By love we see Love, and Hate by grievous hate.

**B110.** If, standing on your steadfast mind, you will look on these things with good intent and faultless care, you will have all these things in plenty throughout your life, and gain many others besides. For these things grow of themselves into your heart, where each man's true nature lies. But if you strain after other things, as men do, ten thousand woes will wait to blunt your careful thoughts. Soon these things will desert you when the time comes round, for they long to return to their own kind. Know that all things have wisdom and a share of thought.

**B111.** You will learn all the drugs that defend against ills and old age — for you alone I will accomplish all this. You will check the rush of the tireless winds that rise and sweep the earth; and when you wish, you will bring back their blasts with a rush. You will make for men a timely drought after the dark rains, and turn the summer drought into tree-feeding streams pouring from the sky. You will bring back from Hades the life of a dead man.

**B112.** Friends, who live in the great town looking down on Akragas's yellow rock, up by the citadel, busy in goodly works — havens of honour for the stranger, men unskilled in meanness — all hail! I go about among you an undying god, no mortal now, honoured by all as is fitting, crowned with fillets and flowery garlands. Straightway, whenever I enter the flourishing towns with these in my train, men and women, reverence is done me. They follow me in countless throngs, asking the way to gain: some wanting oracles, while others, pierced for many a weary day by the grievous pangs of every kind of sickness, beg to hear from me the word of healing.

**B113.** But why harp on these things, as if it were any great matter to surpass mortal, dying men?

**B114.** Friends, I know the truth is in the words I will speak — but it is hard for men, and they resent belief's assault on their souls.
**B115.** There is an oracle of Necessity, an ancient ordinance of the gods, eternal, sealed fast by broad oaths: whenever one of the spirits, whose portion is length of days, has sinfully stained his hands with blood, or followed strife and forsworn himself, he must wander thirty thousand years far from the blessed ones, being born through all that time in every kind of mortal shape, trading one toilsome path of life for another. For the mighty Air drives him into the Sea, and the Sea spews him out on the dry Earth; Earth tosses him into the beams of the blazing Sun, and the Sun flings him back to the eddies of Air. One takes him from another, and all reject him. One of these I am now — an exile and wanderer from the gods, because I put my trust in senseless strife.

**B116.** Grace loathes unbearable Necessity.

**B117.** I have been before now a boy and a girl, a bush and a bird, and a dumb fish in the sea.

**B118.** I wept and wailed when I saw the unfamiliar land.

**B119.** From what honour, from what a height of bliss, have I fallen to wander among mortals here on earth!

**B120.** We have come under this roofed-in cave. *(Burnet's note, from Porphyry: these words were spoken by the powers that conduct the soul down into the world; the "cave" image is Orphic, not originally Plato's.)*

**B121.** ...the joyless land, where Death and Wrath live, and troops of Dooms besides; where parching Plagues and Rottings and Floods roam in darkness over the meadow of Ruin.

**B122–123** *(printed as one passage by Burnet).* There were Earth-goddess and far-seeing Sun-maid, bloody Discord and gentle-faced Harmony, Fair-and-Foul, Speed and Delay, lovely Truth and dark-haired Uncertainty, Birth and Decay, Sleep and Waking, Movement and Standstill, crowned Majesty and Meanness, Silence and Voice.

**B124.** Alas, O wretched race of mortals, twice unblessed! Such are the strifes and groanings you were born from!

**B125.** From living creatures he made them dead, changing their shapes.

**B126.** The goddess clothing them with a strange garment of flesh.

**B127.** Among beasts they become lions that den on the hills and couch on the ground; among trees, laurels with goodly leaves.

**B128.** They had no Ares for a god, nor Battle-Din — no King Zeus, nor Kronos, nor Poseidon — but Kypris the Queen.... They worshipped her with holy gifts: with painted figures and perfumes of cunning fragrance, with offerings of pure myrrh and sweet-smelling frankincense, casting libations of brown honey on the ground. The altar did not reek with the blood of bulls; men held it the greatest abomination to tear out the life and eat the goodly limbs.

**B129.** Among them was a man of rare knowledge, most skilled in every kind of wise work — a man who had won the utmost wealth of wisdom. Whenever he strained with all his mind, he easily saw everything there is, in ten, yes twenty lifetimes of men. *(Timaios already referred these lines to Pythagoras; others referred them to Parmenides — the poem itself gives no name.)*

**B130.** All things were tame and gentle toward man — beasts and birds alike — and friendly feelings were kindled everywhere.

**B131.** If ever, for the things of a day, undying Muse, you cared for my striving, stand by me once more as I pray to you, Kalliopeia, while I speak a pure discourse about the blessed gods.

**B132.** Blessed is the man who has won the riches of divine wisdom; wretched is he who holds a dim opinion of the gods in his heart.

**B133.** We cannot set God before our eyes, nor lay hold of him with our hands — the broadest road of persuasion into a man's heart.

**B134.** He has no human head on his body; no two branches sprout from his shoulders; he has no feet, no swift knees, no hairy parts. He is only a sacred, unspeakable mind, flashing through the whole world with swift thoughts.

**B135.** This is not lawful for some and unlawful for others. The law for all runs everywhere — through the wide-ruling air and heaven's boundless light.

**B136.** Will you never stop this ill-sounding slaughter? Do you not see you are devouring one another in the thoughtlessness of your hearts?

**B137.** The father lifts up his own son, in changed shape, and slays him with a prayer. Blind fool! They crowd to the sacrifice begging mercy, while he, deaf to their cries, slaughters them in his halls and readies the evil feast. Likewise the son seizes his father, children seize their mother, tear out their life and eat their kindred flesh.

**B138.** Draining their life with bronze.

**B139.** Ah, woe is me that the pitiless day did not kill me before I ever worked evil deeds of devouring with my lips!

**B140.** Keep wholly away from laurel leaves.

**B141.** Wretches, utter wretches — keep your hands off beans!

**B142.** The roofed palace of aegis-bearing Zeus will never welcome him, nor the house of....

**B143.** Wash your hands, drawing water from the five springs in unyielding bronze.

**B144.** Fast from wickedness!

**B145.** So you are distraught with grievous wickednesses, and will not unburden your souls of wretched sorrows.

**B146–147** *(printed as one passage by Burnet).* But at the last they appear among mortal men as prophets, song-writers, physicians, and princes. From there they rise as gods exalted in honour, sharing the hearth and table of the other gods — free from human woes, safe from fate, beyond harm.

**B148.** ...Earth, that wraps the man round.

## 4. Anaxagoras of Clazomenae

Burnet prints the fragments in Diels's arrangement. Numbers are Diels = DK 59B.

**B1.** All things were together, infinite in number and in smallness — for the small too was infinite. When all things were together, none could be told apart for smallness. Air and aether overpowered all things, both of them infinite — for among all things these are the greatest in amount and size.

**B2.** Air and aether are separated off from the mass surrounding the world, and the surrounding mass is infinite in amount.

**B3.** There is no smallest among the small — there is always a smaller. What *is* cannot cease to be by being cut. But there is always something greater than the great, and it matches the small in amount. Compared with itself, each thing is both great and small.

**B4.** Since things are so, we must suppose that in the things coming together there are many things of all sorts contained: seeds of all things, with all sorts of shapes and colours and savours. Men have been formed in them, and the other living animals; these men have built cities and tilled fields, as we do; they have a sun and moon and the rest, as we do; their earth brings forth for them many things of all kinds, and they gather the best into their houses and use them. I have said this much about the separating-off, to show that things are separated off not only where we are, but elsewhere too.

But before they were separated off, when all things were together, not even colour could be told apart — the mixing of all things prevented it: moist and dry, warm and cold, light and dark, much earth in it, and a multitude of countless seeds, none like another. None of these things is like any other. Things being so, we must hold that all things are in the whole.

**B5.** These things being settled, we must know that all of them are neither more nor less — for they cannot be more than all, and all are always equal.

**B6.** Since the portions of the great and the small are equal in amount, all things will be in everything for this reason too. They cannot stand apart — all things have a portion of everything. Since there can be no smallest thing, they cannot be separated, nor come to be by themselves. They must be now, as in the beginning, all together. In all things many things are contained, and an equal number in the greater and the smaller of the things separated off.

**B7.** ...So we cannot know the number of the things separated off, either in speech or in deed.

**B8.** The things in one world are not split or cut off from one another with a hatchet — not the warm from the cold, nor the cold from the warm.

**B9.** ...as these things whirl round and are separated out by force and swiftness. The swiftness makes the force. Their swiftness is not like the swiftness of anything now among men — it is in every way many times swifter.

**B10.** How can hair come from what is not hair, or flesh from what is not flesh?

**B11.** In everything there is a portion of everything — except Mind. And there are some things in which there is Mind too.

**B12.** All other things have a share of everything, but Mind is boundless and self-ruled, mixed with nothing — alone, itself by itself. If it were not by itself but mixed with anything else, it would share in all things if mixed with any — for in everything there is a portion of everything, as I said before — and what was mixed with it would hold it back, so it would rule nothing the way it does now, alone by itself. It is the thinnest of all things and the purest. It knows all about everything and has the greatest strength. Mind rules all things, greater and smaller, that have life. Mind ruled the whole whirling, so it began to whirl in the beginning. It began whirling first from a small start; now the whirling covers a wider space, and will cover a wider one still. All the things mingled together, separated off, and marked out are all known by Mind. Mind set in order all things that were to be, all that were and are not now, and all that are — and this whirling in which the stars, the sun and moon, the air and the aether that are separated off now whirl round. This whirling caused the separating-off: the rare was separated from the dense, the warm from the cold, the bright from the dark, the dry from the moist. Many portions are in many things. But nothing is wholly separated off or marked out from anything else — except Mind. All Mind is alike, greater and smaller alike. Nothing else is like anything else; each single thing is, and was, most plainly those things of which it has most in it.

**B13.** When Mind began to move things, separating-off took place from everything that was moved, and as far as Mind set in motion, all was separated. As things were moved and separated, the whirling made them separate much more.

**B14.** Mind, which ever is, is surely present wherever everything else is — in the surrounding mass, in what has joined with it, and in what has been separated off from it.

**B15.** The dense, the moist, the cold, and the dark gathered where the earth now is; the rare, the warm, the dry, and the bright went out toward the farthest part of the aether.

**B16.** From these, as they are separated off, earth is hardened: from mists, water is separated off, and from water, earth. From earth, stones are hardened by the cold, and these rush outward more than water.

**B17.** The Greeks misuse words when they speak of coming into being and passing away. Nothing comes into being or passes away — there is only mingling and separation of things that are. They would be right to call coming into being "mixture," and passing away "separation."

**B18.** It is the sun that puts brightness into the moon.

**B19.** We call the rainbow the sun's reflection in the clouds. It is a sign of storm — for the water flowing round the cloud makes wind, or pours down as rain.

**B20.** When the Dogstar rises, men begin the harvest; when it sets, they begin ploughing. It is hidden for forty days and nights.

**B21.** Our senses are too weak to judge the truth.

**B21a.** What appears is a glimpse of the unseen.

**B21b.** We can make use of the lower animals, because we use our own experience, memory, wisdom, and craft.

**B22.** What is called "birds' milk" is the white of the egg.

### Anaxagoras's cosmology, as summed up by Hippolytus

Burnet quotes this passage from Hippolytus (*Refutation* i. 8) to show the cosmology, numbering its sentences (3)–(12) himself — these are his sentence numbers, not Diels fragments:

- **(3)** The earth is flat, and stays suspended because of its size and because there is no empty space. So the air underneath is very strong and holds up the earth riding on it.
- **(4)** The sea arose from the moisture on the earth's surface — from the waters inside the earth (when these evaporated, what was left turned salt) — and from the rivers that flow into it.
- **(5)** Rivers come both from the rains and from the waters inside the earth, for the earth is hollow and holds waters in its hollows. The Nile rises in summer from the water coming down from the snows in Ethiopia.
- **(6)** The sun, the moon, and all the stars are fiery stones carried round by the wheeling of the aether. Below the stars are the sun and moon, and also certain bodies that wheel with them but are invisible to us.
- **(7)** We do not feel the stars' heat because of their great distance from the earth; also they are not as hot as the sun, since they occupy a colder region. The moon is below the sun and nearer to us.
- **(8)** The sun is bigger than the Peloponnese. The moon has no light of her own but gets it from the sun. The stars' course passes under the earth.
- **(9)** The moon is eclipsed when the earth screens the sun's light from it, and sometimes when the bodies below the moon come in front of it. The sun is eclipsed at new moon, when the moon screens it from us. Sun and moon turn in their courses because the air pushes them back. The moon turns often, because it cannot master the cold.
- **(10)** Anaxagoras was the first to work out about eclipses and the lighting of the sun and moon. He said the moon is earthy, with plains and ravines in it. The Milky Way is the reflection of the light of stars not lit by the sun. Shooting stars are sparks leaping out from the motion of the sky's vault.
- **(11)** Winds arise when the sun thins the air, and when burnt things make their way up to the vault of heaven and are carried off. Thunder and lightning come from heat striking clouds.
- **(12)** Earthquakes happen when the air above strikes the air beneath the earth — the movement of the lower air makes the earth, floating on it, rock.

## 6. Zeno of Elea and Melissus of Samos

Burnet: "I give them according to the arrangement of Diels." Zeno's numbers are Diels = DK 29B; Melissus's are Diels = DK 30B.

### Zeno of Elea

**B1.** You cannot get to the end of a race-course. You cannot cross an endless number of points in a finite time. You must cross half of any distance before you cross the whole, and half of that half before you can cross it. This goes on forever — so there are endlessly many points in any stretch of space, and you cannot touch endless points one by one in a finite time.

**B2.** Achilles will never catch the tortoise. He must first reach the spot the tortoise started from. By then the tortoise will have gone a little way ahead. Achilles must then make up that bit, and again the tortoise will be ahead. He keeps getting nearer but never catches up.

**B3.** The arrow in flight stands still. If everything is at rest when it fills a space equal to itself, and what is flying always, at any given moment, fills a space equal to itself — then it cannot move.

**B4.** Half the time can equal double the time. Suppose three rows of bodies: one row (A) stands still, while the other two (B, C) move at equal speed in opposite directions.

Row A: four bodies standing still.
Row B: four bodies moving right.
Row C: four bodies moving left.

By the time all are lined up together, B will have passed twice as many bodies in C as in A. So the time it takes to pass C is twice as long as the time to pass A. But B and C take the same time to reach A's position. Therefore double the time equals the half.

### Melissus of Samos

**B1a** *(Burnet's (1a): the sentence Melissus opened his treatise with, per Burnet §166 — Simplicius's paraphrase, note 900.)* If nothing exists, what can be said of it as if it were something real?

**B1.** What was, ever was, and ever shall be. If it had come into being, it must have been nothing before it came into being. But if it were nothing, nothing could ever have arisen out of nothing.

**B2.** Since it has not come into being — since it is, ever was, and ever shall be — it has no beginning or end; it is without limit. If it had come into being, it would have a beginning (it would have started coming into being at some time) and an end (it would have stopped coming into being at some time). But if it never began nor ended, and ever was and ever shall be, it has no beginning or end. Nothing can be forever without being wholly so.

**B3.** As it ever is, so it must ever be boundless in size.

**B4.** Nothing that has a beginning or end is either eternal or boundless.

**B5.** If it were not one, it would be bounded by something else.

**B6.** If it is boundless, it must be one. If it were two, it could not be boundless — the two would bound each other.

**B6a** *(Burnet brackets this as Simplicius's own filling-in, not Melissus's words.)* And since it is one, it is alike all through. If it were unlike, it would be many, not one.

**B7.** So it is eternal, boundless, one, and all alike. It cannot perish or grow greater, nor does it feel pain or grief. If any of these happened to it, it would no longer be one. If it changed, the real would have to stop being all alike: what was before would pass away, and what was not would come into being. If it changed by a single hair in ten thousand years, it would all perish in the whole of time.

Its order cannot change either. The order it had before does not perish, and no order that was not comes into being. Since nothing is added to it, nothing passes away, and nothing changes — how could any real thing have its order changed? If anything became different, that would change its order.

It feels no pain. A thing in pain could not be wholly itself — a thing in pain could not be forever, nor have the same power as what is whole. It would not be alike, if it were in pain: only by adding or taking away something could it feel pain, and then it would no longer be alike. What is whole cannot feel pain — for then what was whole and real would pass away, and what was not would come into being. The same argument holds for grief as for pain.

Nothing is empty. What is empty is nothing, and what is nothing cannot be.

It does not move: it has nowhere to go, but is full. If there were anything empty, it would move into the empty. Since there is nothing empty, it has nowhere to go.

It cannot be dense and rare. What is rare cannot be as full as what is dense — what is rare is at once emptier than what is dense.

Here is how to tell full from not-full: if a thing has room for something else and takes it in, it is not full; if it has no room and takes nothing in, it is full.

So it must be full, since there is nothing empty; and if it is full, it does not move.

**B8.** This argument is the greatest proof that it is one alone — but here are further proofs. If there were many things, they would have to be of the same kind as I say the one is. Take earth and water, air and iron, gold and fire; take living and dead, black and white, and all the things men say really are. If that is so — if we see and hear aright — each of these must be what we first decided, unable to change or alter; each must be just as it is. But as it is, we say we see and hear and understand aright, and yet we believe the warm becomes cold and the cold warm, the hard soft and the soft hard, the living dies and things are born from the lifeless. We think all these things change, and that what they were and what they are now are nowise alike. We think iron, though hard, is worn away by a finger's touch — and so with gold and stone and everything we fancy strong — and that earth and stone are made from water. It turns out we neither see nor know realities. These beliefs do not agree with one another. We said there were many eternal things, each with its own form and strength — yet we fancy they all suffer change, altering from what we see each time. Clearly we did not see aright after all, and we are wrong to believe all these things are many. They would not change if they were real; each thing would be just what we believed it to be — for nothing is stronger than true reality. But if it has changed, what was has passed away and what was not has come into being. So if there were many things, they would all have to be of the same nature as the one.

**B9.** If it were to exist, it must be one. But if it is one, it cannot have body — if it had body, it would have parts, and would no longer be one.

**B10.** If what is real is divided, it moves; but if it moves, it cannot *be*.

## 8. Hippon, Diogenes of Apollonia, Archelaus

### Hippon of Samos (or Croton)

One fragment, recovered from the Geneva scholia on Homer; Burnet prints it without a Diels number:

- **[Unnumbered]** "All the water we drink comes from the sea. If wells were deeper than the sea, we would not drink from the sea — the water would come from somewhere else, not the sea. But as it is, the sea is deeper than the well-waters, so all the waters above the sea come from it." *(Directed against the old belief in "waters under the earth" as a separate source.)*

### Diogenes of Apollonia

Burnet: "I give them as they are arranged by Diels." Numbers are Burnet's = Diels 64B, with one honest shift noted below.

**B1.** When beginning any discourse, it seems to me one should start from something no one disputes, and speak plainly and with dignity.

**B2.** My view, to sum it all up, is that all things are different forms of the same thing — are the same thing. This is obvious. Take the things now in this world — earth, water, air, fire, and the other things we see existing in this world: if any one of them were different from any other — different by having a stuff of its own — and if it were not the same thing, changed and differentiated over and over, then things could not mix with one another at all, nor help or harm one another. No plant could grow out of the earth, no animal nor anything else could come into being, unless things were put together so as to be the same. But all these things arise from the same thing; they are differentiated, take different shapes at different times, and return again to the same thing.

**B3.** It could not be divided up as it is — keeping the measures of all things, of winter and summer, day and night, rains and winds and fair weather — without intelligence. Anyone who cares to think will find everything else arranged in the best possible way.

**B4.** Here are further great proofs. Men and all other animals live on air by breathing it. Air is their soul and their intelligence, as will be shown plainly in this work. When it is taken away, they die and their intelligence fails.

**B5.** My view is that what has intelligence is what men call air, and that all things are steered by it — it has power over all things. This very thing I hold to be a god: it reaches everywhere, arranges everything, is in everything, and nothing fails to share in it. Yet no single thing shares in it just like another — there are many forms of air and of intelligence. It goes through many changes: warmer and colder, drier and moister, steadier and swifter in motion, with many other differences in it, and countless colours and savours. The soul of all living things is the same — air, warmer than the air outside us and around us, but much colder than the air near the sun. This warmth is not the same in any two kinds of living creatures — nor, for that matter, in any two men — but it does not differ much, only so far as they can still be alike. And none of the differentiated things can be exactly like another until they all become the same again.

**B6** (Burnet's numbering; = DK 64B7). *(Numbering note: Burnet omits Diels's fr. 6 — the long passage about the veins — as belonging to the history of medicine, not philosophy. What Burnet numbers (6), (7), (8) are therefore Diels/Kranz B7 and B8, the latter printed by Burnet as two items.)* Since differentiation takes many forms, living creatures take many forms and are many; they are like one another neither in looks nor in intelligence, because of the multitude of differentiations. Yet they all live, see, and hear by the same thing, and all get their intelligence from the same source.

**B7.** *(= DK 64B8, first half, as Burnet divides it.)* This itself is an everlasting and undying body; but of those other things, some come into being and some pass away.

**B8.** *(= DK 64B8, second half, as Burnet divides it.)* This too seems obvious to me: that it is great and mighty, everlasting and undying, and knows a great deal.

### Archelaus of Athens

No fragments in Burnet — only Hippolytus's second-hand summary of his cosmology (Anaxagorean mixture plus warm/cold as the two moving causes; Nous planted in all animals). With no words of his own quoted, there is nothing to render; this note stands so no one goes looking for a section that is not there.
