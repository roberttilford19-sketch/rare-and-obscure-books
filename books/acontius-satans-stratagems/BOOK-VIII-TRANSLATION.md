*The Stratagems of Satan. Book Eight.*

What the way of coming to that end might be, which Satan had set before himself as the end of all his counsels — seeing that the argument has been carried thus far — this only now remains, that we discourse of Satan himself, the inventor, the deviser, and the worker of all these things, of whom there has indeed been no history delivered in writing so full as our inquisitive wits could wish. This much we may gather: that he was created of a certain most excellent nature, and adorned with most excellent gifts, but that when he abused them unto the height of pride, he was cast down from heaven, and those most excellent gifts, by the most just and most dreadful judgment of God, were turned into the utmost wickedness, whereby he took this upon himself, that he should set himself against God in all things, so far as he could do and strive to do it. Nor indeed is he the only spirit of that kind, but there is a very great multitude of them, of whom Satan is the head and the prince. Since indeed it stands delivered in writing that one legion of demons was cast out of a certain man in the country of the Gadarenes [Marginal note: Luc. 8.], and that for this reason it is said that everlasting fire was prepared for the devil and his angels.

But that Satan is the greatest old deceiver, and the most savage enemy of the human race, is clearer in the sacred writings than to seem to need any demonstration. [Marginal note: Satan most crafty, an unwearied layer of snares, tends his wiles with cunning.] From whence that is easily brought about which we said at the beginning: that whatsoever is of such a kind that, if men do it, say it, or think it, it may contribute something to his endeavours, he will always endeavour that it be done, and said, and thought. And although for that purpose he must needs have dealing with the minds of men — that he may practise deceit upon them, that he may delude them, that he may persuade, that he may inflame, that he may work upon them with hope and with fear — after what manner he does these things, it is not our present purpose to philosophise more subtly. This is certain: that he drives men to do the things which he himself desires; that he spares no one; that there is no one of whom he is afraid on his own account, or whom he stands in awe of, however great he may be, whether in wisdom, or in holiness, or in authority — seeing that he hesitated not to assail even the Son of God himself. Nor yet does he so assail all those whom he tempts — nay, not even the least part of them, scarcely — that he can be seen; neither does he address them, as if he would have some other thing from them. For although it is written concerning Judas that *Satan had cast into him the thought of betraying his Lord*, yet it is not written that he so accosted Judas that he could be seen, and exhorted him unto it.

Nay rather, since Satan is a spirit, he seems to deal with the minds of men in no other way than the Spirit of God himself: for that Spirit so moves and works upon minds, that nevertheless they perceive not that there is some other thing which, as it were, addresses them; nay, the things seem to them no otherwise than their own thoughts. Save only insofar as a man, taught what a man is able to do and what he falls short in, if he perceives thoughts to be exceedingly godly and holy, understands that they ought to be ascribed to the Holy Spirit, and not to himself — yet so as to be helped by the Spirit himself even unto this manner of interpreting them. Wherefore whatsoever thought Satan also casts in, it seems to the man altogether the same — namely, that he himself is he who so thinks — only, if he perceives the thought to be exceedingly wicked, not being altogether ignorant of that one's works, he will suspect that it comes from him. Perhaps indeed not every evil thought, and evil counsel, is to be referred unto Satan as its author, seeing that man of his own accord, by nature, never at any time ceases to think amiss and to do amiss with no spur added, and pursues the very same things which Satan most wishes him to pursue. Only this is the difference, that man is holden by some desire, I know not what, of salvation.

Satan wishes nothing for him less than salvation; and to man there oftentimes befals that which Medea says of herself: — *I see the better things, and I approve them too: the worse I follow.* Satan sets himself against God in will and in purpose. Man hunts after present pleasure, and is moved by things present. The things unto which Satan drives a man, he oftentimes, by a set counsel, directs unto an end very far remote. So it comes to pass that Satan sometimes brings a man to do things which are in no way pleasant for him to do: as that he should beat himself with stripes, that he should accomplish some long journey with bare feet, that he should often impose upon himself fasting, and other troubles of that kind, in which he has persuaded him that there is much safeguard toward salvation; and man also perpetrates many evils, Satan himself (as is likely) being unwilling. As if a man of great name, and the same man a great patron of errors and of impiety, should fall into some such crime, by reason whereof he should make shipwreck of his name and of his authority, and should give men occasion to open their eyes, and to perceive the impostures. Otherwise the instincts of man and of Satan are so like each unto the other, that milk is not more like to milk. But man is also, in his errors, liable of his own accord, even if no working of Satan toward the deceiving of him be added thereto.

Wherefore, since a man's own thoughts, and those which are from Satan, are most like unto each other, by so much the harder is it for a man to recognise Satan speaking, if I may so say, in his own heart. Nor indeed is it worth the labour to trace out any subtler rule for discerning the things that are man's from those which are Satan's, so long as it stands agreed what is good, that we may follow it, and what is evil, that we may decline it. But if perchance we shall have ascribed unto Satan that which was man's, or unto man that which was Satan's, the error will be in no wise great. Nay rather, it is to be seen that the same thing has been ascribed now to man, now to Satan: which was done by the apostle Peter, when he rebuked Ananias, who had lied unto the Holy Spirit touching the true price of the field which he had sold. [Marginal note: Act. 5.] For first he speaks thus: *Why has Satan led you on, that you should lie unto the Holy Spirit?* Then again thus: *Why have you contrived a thing of this manner?* And his wife also he rebukes in this manner: *Why have you thus conspired to tempt the Spirit of the Lord?* That makes most for the matter in hand, that Satan casts thoughts into men. As it was said just now of Ananias, so it is written of Judas, that *Satan had cast into his mind that he should betray Jesus, and that Satan shakes the word of God out of the hearts of men.* Since therefore this is Satan's manner of dealing with man, that he can deal with him while he perceives it not, he must needs have from hence wonderful opportunities of assailing him in a thousand ways, and of taking him by storm. For if some man should accost you, and give you evil counsel, or practise a deceit upon you once or twice, and he should return, still to catch you by some fraud, even under some specious pretext — since now the man's temper and will are suspect unto you — you will not suffer yourself to be imposed upon. But since Satan can approach in the manner that has been spoken of, even if today he shall have cast in some most foul and plainly impious counsel, so that whence it is cannot lie hid, yet that will be no hindrance but that the same may return tomorrow, and assail you by another, craftier than it. For for this cause you cannot beware of his counsel, because its author is suspect unto you, seeing that you acknowledge no other author of it than yourself. From whence it may be gathered, that there is no one whom Satan does not assail in some manner whatsoever. For since no deed of his can be a hindrance unto another, whatsoever anyone may be, whether in wisdom, or in holiness, he will assuredly make trial nonetheless whether he can either draw away even the most foolish into error, or drive him unto some most wicked and most sacrilegious deed, no regard being had of the person: for if he shall have accomplished it, he will have gotten an ample triumph; but if the attempt shall have fallen out in vain, he will thereby have made the way of deceiving him by a craftier counsel in nothing harder for himself, so that, a beginning being taken from the least things, he may by little and little come unto greater.

Nor does it matter by what way he first assails any man: for as touching him against whom he has laid snares in secret, he has oftentimes made trial of his mind also by some great crime — the feigned person being, as it were, laid aside, and his own openly assumed — as he did with the very Son of God, with whom, when he had profited nothing by his crafty petty temptations, he hesitated not to discover himself, and to say: If, fallen down, you will worship me, all these things shall be yours. But if he begins with open war, not even that, as we have said, hinders him but that afterwards he may also essay snares. And as he both fights with open war, and lays most hidden snares, so it is not to be doubted but that he uses also counsels neither wholly open nor altogether hidden. [Marginal note: He is never not devising some evil, openly or by stealth.] Yet it is likely that those, with whom he perceives he has need of the utmost craftiness for the laying of the foundations of some great pile, are for the most part exercised with open war, to the end that, whilst they think the whole manner of the war to stand in those conflicts, they may be made the more careless of shunning hidden snares; which if he who lately described the life of a certain most excellent man had weighed, he would not perchance have so boldly pronounced that Satan could never have made an assault upon him, but would have been compelled, some grievous wound received, to draw back his foot.

And after the same manner it comes to pass that nothing hinders him from endeavouring to persuade, as touching the same things, now one thing and now another: for, that he may drive a man unto some crime, he will make light of the matter; he will call unto remembrance (but in a place very unseasonable indeed) how great is God's readiness in sparing, and His mercy, or he will persuade him that He cares not for such things. The crime once committed, his speech will be altogether the contrary: for he will magnify the thing itself in wonderful manner, and will set before the eyes all the examples of divine severity and of vengeance, and at the length will drive the man unto the height of despair; and for this cause the cheat is not perceived, because it does not appear whose that speech is, but the man thinks them to be his own thoughts — whereas perchance, since these are contrary to those former, he has ascribed those unto Satan, but these unto himself. But since Satan is a spirit, and is not burdened with the weight of any matter, he cannot be wearied by any labours; neither do the injuries of heat touch him, nor of cold; for sleep takes him not, nor yet oblivion: from whence we may understand that he devises somewhat against us unceasingly.

And whatsoever he devises, he does it either by himself or by others: we say that he does by himself whatsoever either the king of darkness himself does, or any other impure spirit; for it would be less difficult for us to discern differences of this kind; but by others we understand him to do those things for which he uses the service of men, as of certain instruments. And whatsoever he does by himself, he either keeps his own person — as when he said unto the Son of God, If, fallen down, you will worship me, all these things shall be yours — or he feigns the person of a heavenly angel, as when he exhorts unto those things whereby he may open unto himself an entrance for the bringing of some impiety into the church of God, and he covers them with a very painted and holy pretext [Marginal note: He often puts on the appearance of godliness, and a mask.], and from hence doubtless there came, in great part, that so great liberality of our forefathers toward churches and monasteries: for, riches and furniture for every luxury being supplied unto those who had claimed unto themselves alone the whole care of religion, there followed thereafter a corruption both of doctrine and of all good manners. The things which he attempts by others must nevertheless have their beginning from himself; and this beginning he makes likewise, his own person either retained or another's assumed; and although he essays all things with all men, yet for the most part he keeps his own person with men of desperate impiety, whom he has found most obedient unto him unto every kind of villainy.

With those in whom there is some zeal of godliness he dissembles, and attempts all things under the appearance of godliness: those he makes zealous of himself, and he blinds them utterly, holding forth the greatest hopes, setting before them the largest rewards, according to each man's temper: for some, being ambitious of honours, he loads with titles; others he overwhelms with abundance of riches; others he binds fast unto himself by other things. These moreover he feeds partly with the same things, and partly also with the opinion of things splendidly done by themselves. But that he understands the thoughts of men otherwise than by conjectures drawn from those things which a man does and says, and which befall him, I for my part would not contend, since it is written, that God alone searches the hearts of men. But in truth it must needs be that, at the least for the taking of such conjectures, and for the knowing of the tempers of men, his insight is greater than all belief: for if a man, oftentimes furnished with some wit, and taught also by some experience of things, if acquaintance of a very few days has passed between him and another, learns what is the man's feeling, what his mind, whither he tends, with what things he is delighted, with what he is offended.

What may Satan do, so long exercised in this wrestling-place, and so great an old deceiver? But that that power is beyond measure great wherewith Satan, unless God restrain him, imposes upon men, we may know from hence: that of two thoughts most nearly joined together he can cast the one into a man, and hide the other from him. For what two thoughts could be more nearly joined than these: that the things foretold are the oracles of God, and that what is foretold by them shall altogether come to pass, and that it cannot be wrought by any force or counsel of man that it should not come to pass? Yet by the working of Satan the one could light into the thought of Herod without the other. For he gives credit to the oracles of the prophets concerning the birth of the promised King of Israel, and therefore he enquires of the chief priests and of the scribes in what place he should be born; and together with that, only that he might make those oracles vain, he contrives death for him; and, lest he might escape, he uses that crafty and most cruel counsel, to send forth men who should slay all the infants of two years old and under, sought out in Bethlehem and in the coasts thereof. Unless indeed this was the work of Satan, it was assuredly no man's. But what, in the end, is this bewitchment? That, because it stood in the predictions of the prophets that a king should be born unto the Jews, he should be persuaded that it would be so; and that from the same predictions he could know both where and when he should be born; but that he could also elude those predictions, and hinder God, and make His purpose of none effect?

Is this not exceeding wonderful? From whence we may conjecture that Satan does in like manner in the minds of men, as if, when you would hinder a man from seeing somewhat, you should interpose either somewhat or yourself between him and that thing, or by any means should take that thing away; and, that he may see that which you would have him see, you should set that before his eyes: namely, that when Satan desires to draw a man away from some thought, he busies him with other and other things, and leaves him no empty time at all for casting the eyes of his mind upon it; but the thoughts which make for his purpose, those he casts in everywhere before him. And that this most cruel enemy of the human race hesitates to assail no man, hesitates to lay snares for no man [Marginal note: With what paint he sometimes imposes even upon the holiest men.], has been shown by the witness of the Son of God, from whom he feared not to ask that He should worship him; but with whom he may effect somewhat, a man might not absurdly doubt. For one might say that he loses his labour with those who fear God, and have placed their hope in Him. Which indeed must needs be true thus far: that Satan cannot make void any purpose of God, nor indeed bring it to pass but that God most abundantly fulfils His promises toward those who trust in Him.

But that he drives men unto many things, both to be done and to be spoken, is not so altogether certain: for whence came those first foundations of the Antichristian kingdom? By whose working has the Gospel lost its ancient brightness? Who procured so great authority unto men, that they should now be as a sort of gods? Was there here no working of Satan? Nay rather, whose, if not Satan's? And yet what shall we say of all those men who bestowed their labour upon that matter, of what kind soever, with no evil counsel, but only with a certain good hope — that they thought they did rightly and holily, and judged that their counsels would be for some great good of the church? That none of them had any godliness? Any fear of God? That none was of the number of God's elect? And yet of which of the ancient fathers was there not some labour toward that matter? For whilst, to speak after the Apostle's manner, they built upon the foundation, which is Christ, although they cast hay and stubble into the work, that hindered nothing at all but that they might be both holy men, and of the number of God's chosen. But so much of hay and stubble has nevertheless been brought in, that in the whole building there has begun to be nothing besides hay and stubble, and when it must needs be that the thing should have so ended, it must needs also that it should have taken its beginning from such beginnings: neither can it be denied but that, to whom the end is ascribed, to the same the beginnings are also to be ascribed. And that turning aside also of Peter from the right path of the Gospel, for the which he was reproved by Paul — does it not savour somewhat of the spirit of Satan? Nay rather, did not the Lord himself say unto his apostles, that Satan has his eyes fastened upon them, that he might vehemently harass them, and shake out of them all faith? Which also he was little short of having accomplished. And when he denied that he had anything to do with Jesus, shall we not think that Satan was his counsellor unto that? Then surely there is no doubt that he was, when he dissuaded the Lord from undergoing death. From all which things a sure conjecture may be drawn: that Satan effects many things even with the holiest of men, not indeed that he may destroy them, but assuredly that he may open a way unto the destruction of very many. Which very thing nonetheless does not come to pass without the highest providence of God. To the end that thence vengeance may be prepared against the unthankful, and the despisers of his greatest gifts — namely, the greatest darkness of errors — and, for the godly, that they perish not with the world, exercises of afflictions most necessary. So it comes to pass that oftentimes even the holiest men, beyond all expectation, let out their labour unto Satan, and are as it were certain ministers and instruments of his.

And because, the more eminent place anyone has obtained in the church of God, if Satan can practise a deceit upon him, if he can in any measure bend him, by so much the larger fruit he takes from thence: it is credible that with these men also he does all his utmost, that he may everywhere allure such men into some snares. Nor yet is he so wholly busied with great men, but that he remembers also every most obscure man, after the manner, to wit, of a most diligent husbandman, who so takes care for great matters, that he does not neglect even the least.

But Satan is not only busied in bending the minds of men unto his wishes in the manners that have been spoken of; nay rather his other works are far diverse: he draws down fire from heaven to the destroying of Job's sheep and of his servants [Marginal note: Iob. 1.]; he stirs up that great violence of the winds, whereby the house where the same man's sons and daughters were feasting together is overturned, and they all perish; and the man himself at the last he torments with a most grievous disease. But he works also great wonders, seeing that by his working the magicians of Pharaoh do counterfeit wellnigh all the miracles of Moses, and concerning Antichrist it has been foretold, that by his working he shall be renowned for wonders. [Marginal note: 2 Thes. 2.] From all which things we may conjecture how much he is able to do in very many other matters also: but he is called also *the Prince of this world*, which cannot be done in vain; nay rather, we may gather from thence — since riches, honours, and dignities are called worldly things — that they are in great part dispensed at his pleasure.

Since therefore Satan has so great opportunities, even as great as we have spoken of, whatsoever it concerns him either to be believed or to be done, he gives his labour that it be done, or be believed; if there be need of false reasons, he supplies them, now by himself, now by others. If there be need of wonders, he either works them, or by many wiles persuades men that they have been wrought. If, toward the believing of a thing very absurd, there be need of a certain train of matters — that, a beginning being made from light errors, the minds of men be led thither as it were by certain steps — he does this most ingeniously. And whereas a thing may be believed both upon false grounds, and because it is so indeed, if it be so expedient, he will give his labour that it hold itself after that manner. Wherefore, since it is most expedient for him that he who teaches true things concerning religion be believed to be defiled with some foul crime, and so make shipwreck of all his authority, he will lay for him every kind of snare, that he may fall into somewhat, and that he himself may have matter whereof to triumph; but if he cannot do that, he will suborn men who shall assail the man's good name with slanders; and if by anyone he can shake it, who is said to profess the same doctrines, nothing will be more effectual.

Wherefore he will endeavour, with all zeal, to set those who profess the evangelical truth at variance among themselves; he will sow certain controversies, strifes, discords, brawls, and at the last hatreds, whereby it may come to pass that they tear each other with curses. And when he shall have found that he now resists the truth in vain, he will stir up unto the divine letters those wits in which he shall have scented that there is the least of godliness, the hope, to wit, of great honour, of name, of renown, and of very many advantages being set before them. These he will favour as much as he can, that they may come into great estimation, and at the last obtain ecclesiastical offices; so that when afterwards he shall have driven them into some villainies, and that shall become open, the truth may be branded with infamy from thence, and may find the less credit afterward, when many commonly boast those things: Alas, do they so live, who would seem to be sent as it were from the council of the immortal gods for the restoring of religion and manners? Shall we then learn from those masters to commit these things? Is this their new and hidden wisdom, which they set forth with so high a brow? If riches, honours, advantages, or, on the contrary, want, disgrace, disadvantages, afflictions, can dazzle the eyes of men, they are ready at hand. If hope, fear, anger, friendships, enmities are to be a furtherance unto him, the seeds of them are never wanting unto him.

If there be need of any acquaintance, he devises occasions, whereby he may impose upon him as it were some necessity thereof; and he suborns authors, and adds very specious colours. But Satan seizes from all sides occasions unto his purposes. [Marginal note: He seizes occasions for his business from every side.] For if a man be unskilful and foolish, he will persuade him of whatsoever tales he will; and when he has filled him with most foolish errors, he persuades him that he is endowed with some excellent wisdom, and upon that name lifts up his courage, and makes him insolent, arrogant, and rash; if he be learned, he increases unto him the opinion of his own learning in wonderful manner, so that he persuades himself that he alone knows somewhat, that he despises all men in comparison of himself, and cannot abide them. If anyone be of a crafty temper, and but little religious, him he uses most fitly as an emissary, and as an impostor unto all things, for the filling of all things with deceits, impostures, brawls, and burnings. If anyone be endowed with the knowledge of the truth, and with any godliness, he subjects him, as much as he can, unto reproaches and unto the contempt of men, that no man may make so much as a straw of his words and instructions. But if he cannot hinder but that credit be given unto him, he will give his labour that men's opinion of him be excessive, and that they hold him wholly in the place of God. If a people shall have forsaken the reading of the sacred books, he shakes out of it all the fear of God; he allures it by little and little unto pleasures, unto excess, unto licentiousness of life, unto every kind of lusts, and at the last unto a certain beastly custom of living.

If it shall cherish those studies, he sows from thence strifes, discords, and all the other evils which we have spoken of, when he cannot shake the doctrine: he brings his engines against manners; or if here he should labour in vain, he assails the doctrine. But for the most part he attempts both at once, and, the sloth of men being well espied, endeavours to make great progress; if he light upon men somewhat more wary, he will take upon him the person of some heavenly and notable angel; he will inflame the minds of men with thoughts too holy (if I may so speak), whereby, to wit, they may transgress the bounds of divine discipline set before them. For example's sake: whereas nothing is more glorious, than to be ready, as often as the cause of the glory of God shall require, and God shall drive us unto it, to meet death in witness of the evangelical truth — he will cast into some ambitious man a desire of such a death, that by that means he may commend his name unto the everlasting remembrance of men. For with desire of such a death Origen is said to have burned a long time, and of his own accord to have caught at the occasions of it. Which indeed could scarce have proceeded from any good spirit, since we see that not even the Son of God himself brought greater courage unto his own punishment, than was needful.

Forasmuch as he prayed also the Father, that (if it might be) he might lawfully decline it, yet so, that his will, not his own, might be satisfied. From whence it appears, that that kind of death was a horror also unto him; so far is he from being borne with desire toward it, save inasmuch as he was most obedient unto the Father, and thirsted for our salvation. Which being so, how can it be, that either Origen, or any other man, was of a more present mind, than the very Son of God himself? Therefore such a desire could scarce be without some rashness, and a too great, and that an empty, confidence in himself; which could befall a man, even when he in no wise perceives it himself, as we have taught elsewhere by many examples after what manner it could be done. The great praises of the martyrs (for let us now use this word, by this time grown usual) and that glory which followed them that were afflicted with punishment for the name of Christ, did somewhat tickle the minds of men. Unto the same author belongs that so great severity of the ancients in ecclesiastical censures, which we have spoken of; which procured so much veneration and authority for the bishops, that it opened a way for them unto dominion over the people, and even over kings themselves; it may also come to pass that Satan favour holy thoughts, and as it were add spurs unto a horse that is already running, that he may seasonably bring them soon unto snares.

Which he did, when he brought it so to pass, that mighty men, and even kings themselves, did sometimes subject themselves unto wicked pastors; that he might procure the highest veneration for those men, godly indeed and holy, whose heirs their successors should afterward be — who, when they had by little and little degenerated from the holiness of their ancestors, at the length that veneration, by its contrivers, should be turned unto the oppression of the good, and unto tyranny; and when, that counterfeits might sometimes ambitiously seek bishoprics, he pricked forward wealthy men to exercise the highest liberality toward bishops who should distribute gifts unto the poor, and soon after (that it might come unto the successors) toward the churches themselves also: which, how great a cunning was it? or who could at the beginning have held such liberality for suspected? But now we see whither the matter is come. So, that he might persuade men that godliness stands in the difference of meats, he persuaded them first, that the body must be chastised by abstinence from those meats which did greatly nourish it, and made it exceeding strong; and by the use of those which did waste and enfeeble it; so should it be more obedient unto the spirit and unto right reason; so should it the more easily be able to obey the commandments of God; when afterward the cause wherefore it was done had come into oblivion, whereas the custom of abstaining on certain days from certain meats remained, it was easy to fasten an opinion of godliness upon such a kind of abstinence.

But the chief cunning and craftiness of this old deceiver is discerned in the sowing of strifes and dissensions: [Marginal note: But the chief study of this old deceiver [is], to sow strifes and dissensions.] for when it is come to that pass, that he cannot now shake the sacred books out of the hands of men, he kindles them also with a certain insatiable thirst of knowing all things (even those of which there is no use), and daily casts in scruples; and whatsoever scruple he has cast in, he persuades also, as much as he can, that the truth of that matter is altogether necessary to be known, and whithersoever the judgment shall have inclined, to this part or to that, it follows that he persuade also, that he who errs in that matter cannot be partaker of salvation. And so he persuades a man, that it belongs to his office, what God has opened unto him, to open that also unto others; and when Satan persuades some men one thing, and others another, of the same matter, hence are controversies, from which it comes to pass that no knowledge of the truth follows, but rather that darkness is poured upon the eyes of men, and that greater daily; and that discords, dissensions, and brawls arise from thence; and that they tear one another with mutual injuries; and leave no room for any hope of concord; and he leads each man into as great admiration of himself as he can, insomuch that every man seems unto himself to be a kind of god, from whom it is lawful to receive laws both of living and of believing.

Which is nothing else, unto a man's wit, loving itself more than enough, than to add spurs unto a horse that is already running. But from the too great opinion which every man has of himself ariseth rashness, whereby it comes to pass, that whatsoever comes into his mouth, he blurts it out; whatsoever occurs, he sets it down for certain, no more diligent examination being applied; and he pronounces it as a kind of oracle; and the more confident the words which the most crafty enemy can draw out of him, the more gloriously he thinks it fares with him. For so (as it has been said elsewhere) it would seem that no place at all is left for retraction, without great disgrace; and if any man set himself against him, so great confidence must needs follow him, with the highest insolence also, in despising the other, whosoever he be. And that he may the better obtain what he wants — as it is meet to hate all false doctrines whatsoever, and to abolish them with all endeavour — so he endeavours to persuade men, that whosoever will maintain any false doctrine are most worthy of hatred, and of execration; and that of whomsoever worships God from his heart it is required, that he take up such hatreds; and that men of this sort are to be entertained, both in deeds and in words, as harshly as may be. If any man in the church hold the place of a pastor, or of a teacher, and there be any estimation of the man, he sets before his eyes the dignity of the office, with the which it becomes every man to honour him with veneration, and that it is the duty of every man to give credit unto him, and not to think that a controversy is to be moved against him; that whoso has done otherwise, has done it impudently and arrogantly; that by that means the dignity of the sacred ministry is overturned, and trodden under foot.

Whoso shall attempt that, does even as if he endeavoured to cast God himself down from his seat. If any word of his be held for false, he persuades men that all faith of religion must needs fall together, and that the Christian commonwealth must therewith be wholly overturned, no otherwise than if he were the only foundation whereupon it rested and stood; and as if God had cast off all care of it. Unto him who discharges no such office, he will put it into his thought, that knowledge is not joined with any offices, neither is it placed in the judgment of the common sort, which often highly esteems and admires certain most foolish men; that he also, although he be not mighty in so great authority, yet goes before them in knowledge; wherefore, unless the ecclesiastical pastor, or the teacher, use toward him an equal right in all things, he will paint that unto him as a kind of great insolence and pride — nay, even a tyranny in no wise to be endured — but the matter itself as exceeding holy, and worthy of great praise, if any man shall set himself against it, and endeavour to overturn such tyrannies.

Therefore, bringing with one hand (as the proverb says) fire, and with the other straw, wheresoever he can, he makes the greatest burnings; and that they may never be quenched, he never ceases to add matter. Water — that is, every human, mild, and prudent wit — he keeps away from the burnings as much as he can, lest it bring any help toward the quenching of them; but rather that comes to pass which Ennius says: *The good speaker is despised, the rough soldier is loved.* So minds inflamed with fury do all things, and speak, rashly, inconsiderately, insolently, proudly; and they put in so many things beside the cause, that scarce every hundredth word makes to the matter. Whatsoever has once been pronounced, this execrable enemy never suffers (if only he can hinder it) to be retracted; but toward the exacting of retractions he makes men, as much as he can, most rigid; nothing is pronounced whole; there is no patience of hearing. From whence it comes to pass, that they do not understand one another; and that, being asked often of garlic, they answer of onions. Wherefore one attributes to another those things which he neither said, nor perhaps ever thought; for which causes what one man devises against another, no speech can express. Never is the northern ocean so troubled, but that the state of the church seems sometimes much more turbulent. Here we see some of our brethren subjected unto most cruel torments, others to yield unto the most wicked enemy, and for fear, unto the dishonour and reproach of God, to do whatsoever he pleases; there we hear both drums, and the clangour of trumpets, and thunders uttered by warlike engines, and the most savage voices of men exhorting one another against our blood and our own bowels. There is made before our eyes a horrible slaughter of our people; neither age nor sex is spared; even the very births not yet brought forth into the light are sought out unto death, and are torn into many parts. What do we in the meantime? These things, forsooth, move us nothing; but we plead gallantly, we contend, we brawl. Reproaches, curses, and shames are cast to and fro most petulantly; so the common cause is daily more and more dishonoured; we stir up daily more atrocious hatreds against ourselves; and we whet for the common enemy the sword, that he may sometime draw it against ourselves; and we supply him the wood wherewith he may burn our own bodies. And so great a workmaster is Satan, that he suffers no man to understand that there is any fault in himself; every man casts his own crime upon his adversary, and sets himself beyond all blame and cause.

I should have great store to rehearse of those things which are daily to be seen and heard, worthy indeed of many tears. But lest I incur any man's offence, and our speech be therefore the less fruitful with him, I forbear to rehearse them by name. But that affected me in wonderful manner which happened not so long ago, France miserably burning with civil war sprung from controversies of religion. There was published a pamphlet, in which almost all, as many as have gotten themselves any name in the restoring of the Gospel, were defiled with so reproachful and unworthy names, that they are wont to be much more gently entertained by the Papists; and yet the author of the pamphlet was one who had long been held to be of the same party. See, say I, the madness of men! Are all things there burning so miserably with intestine war, and we here so shamefully, and so unworthily abusing our leisure? But anger had so blinded the man, that he could not perceive what he did, and how pleasant a spectacle he prepared for the eyes of Satan; and they who can refute nothing, but at the first word fight with the stings of reproaches, cannot learn, even when they have most often made the trial; from such seed nothing but such a harvest is to be expected.

Seeing therefore we have such an enemy, whose arts of deceiving are so hidden, various, manifold, and crafty; whose practice is of so long continuance; whose most delightful and sweetest rest is (while he incommodes us in some matter) never to rest: what shall we do? [Marginal note: Therefore security is to be shaken off, and armour is to be put on against an enemy so crafty, so various.] Shall we give ourselves unto sleep and unto sloth? Shall we not look about us? Shall we not take heed unto ourselves? Shall every man flatter himself, so that he thinks other foolish men indeed to be the men that must beware, that they are to be warned to watch, but that himself is now become so prudent, that he can no more be allured into any snares? And yet this is the chief of all those things which he endeavours. Nay, neither is it often in vain, that he brings men (those especially who are strong in authority, and in whose prudence and vigilance the whole safety of the church seems to be placed) into such an opinion of themselves. But I hold it most certainly persuaded, that as every man persuades himself that he is furthest off from the snares of Satan, so is he nearest unto them. Why do I say nearest? Nay rather, that he is already held fast bound by them as straitly as may be. Forsooth, a young beginner, who has to do with a most crafty veteran, would not always be versed in the greatest danger: for be thou of so great prudence, sagacity, wisdom, and experience of things, that thou easily goest before all men.

What wilt thou yet be, in comparison of him, but a new soldier? Which indeed is not said by me to this end, as though I would have it seem that no hope were left for the declining of his acts and his wiles. There is altogether hope, and that indeed most certain; but for whom? Surely not for them that lean upon their own prudence, or sagacity; nor for the secure, the yawning, and such as take no heed unto themselves; nor for those who consider not with what kind of enemy they have to do, and therefore despise him; but for those who, when they have well found out the dangers, provide themselves, according to the greatness of the dangers, with fit armour to resist, and to repel the enemy; which armour Paul finely describes. For he will have *the loins of the Christian soldier to be girt with the girdle of truth*, whereby he seems to require a mind loving sincerity, from which all hypocrisy, or dissimulation, and paint may be away, and which prepares itself in earnest for the fight; he will have *the breast armed with the breastplate of righteousness*, that a good conscience may be present, in that whatsoever he does, he will have it referred unto the glory of God, and be himself a witness unto himself, that he measures it neither by his own advantages, nor disadvantages, nor by any other accounts; he will have *the feet shod with the shoes of the Gospel of peace*, that he may learn to despise the thorns of ignominies, and to forget them, and not to apply his mind unto the avenging of any injuries, but rather so to prepare himself, that he be ready to render unto every man good for evil. He covers his side *with the shield of faith*, whereby he may undauntedly pledge his soul against the fiery power of the most wicked enemy, or against his extreme craftiness in deceiving; but may certainly resolve, that he wars under that commander who will not forsake him in the time of need; nay rather, who will fortify him with fit counsel, and with strength firm enough; that he is a care unto him; that he has wisdom and strength enough to keep him unto him, and from all the wiles, assaults, and attempts of Satan; for which cause there is nothing wherefore he should tremble. He arms his head *with the helmet of salvation*, that under pressing afflictions the expectation of salvation may comfort him, since in it there are so many and so great goods, that no thought can attain unto them, in even the least part. Lastly, he arms his hand with the sword of the spirit (that is) with the word of God, whereby he may both receive the blows of the enemy without hurt, and wound the enemy himself, and utterly dispatch him.

But lest, in the place of that trust which, being so armed, he ought to have placed in God, any slothful security should creep into his mind, he will have prayers to be added; and prayers indeed of that kind, which proceed from a mind both no unfit estimator of the dangers, and conscious of its own weakness, and mindful of the divine promises. With prayers he joins watchfulness; whereby he admonishes, that help is in such sort to be expected from God, that thou must in the meantime look about thee; to observe the disposition of Satan, that thou mayest have ever before thine eyes who thou art, what thy charge is, what the state of things is, what the times are; lastly, on what part, at every time and moment, Satan may assault thee.

Not that thou shouldest place thy hope of escaping out of the snares of Satan in that thy watchfulness; but we must remember, that divine help is in such sort promised unto us, that he will nevertheless have us expect it from himself, not sleeping, but watching. So that godly husbandman expects his harvest from the liberality and providence of God indeed, but in the meantime gives not himself unto sloth; nay rather, he tills his ground, and joins his sweat with his faith. Lastly, the Apostle adds also perseverance. For since from this enemy there is no time, no moment, void of danger, never any peace, no truce, who, I beseech you, could hope either to put off his armour, or to remit even ever so little of the carefulness of keeping watch, without the highest and most certain danger? These are the things which at present we have been able to say of the stratagems of Satan; unto which, although we deny not that many more things might have been added, yet those things which have been opened by us from God (for which cause immortal thanks are to be given unto him) are so many and so great; and the manner of declining them, added, is of such a sort, that if any man shall not only once run them over, but shall often and diligently read them through, commit them unto memory, and make them familiar unto himself, both with frequent meditation, and then also with observation, exercise, and use, he shall easily understand, that there is come unto him very much of skill, and of prudence, for the sustaining of this fight.

So that whether Satan endeavour to pour darkness upon the Gospel, or to corrupt manners, or to lay the foundations of some new tyranny, or to hinder the course of the same Gospel, thou mayest easily smell out what he attempts, and see by what manner his counsels may be broken. But it is to be weighed again and again, how horrible those destructions are: darkness brought in upon the Gospel, the hindering of its course, the corruption of manners, ecclesiastical tyranny, and the butchery of consciences. For let there come into our minds the people of Israel, but especially the unheard-of blindness of the Pharisees, of the Scribes, and of the Priests, whose eyes neither the most clear predictions of the prophets, nor so many and so great miracles, could open, unto the acknowledging of him whom they had awaited with so great desire. The Papal kingdom is before our eyes. Unhappy the people, which has gotten such, or such kind of, pastors? Unto whom does it commit its salvation? Whom does it believe cannot err?

What devices, what dreams, what trifles has it suffered to be thrust upon itself in the place of the Gospel? How foul, how abominable, how destructive a traffic does it suffer to be exercised upon itself! That it should buy poison for a medicine, damnation for salvation, for life death, and that an everlasting death? It worships, in the place of God, gold, silver, wood, stones, and other most foolish inventions of its own madness. And whereas the Apostles, in almost one moment, spread the Gospel through the whole world, how long, and with how little fruit, has it been laboured for the restoring of the same? Unto prating, unto contentions, unto fights, very many are furnished; but there appear not so many examples of true godliness, to comfort us and to cheer us, as there stand forth of ungodliness, to draw from us most plentiful tears (unless we were wholly of stone). These things it becomes all men who love Christ to meditate upon assiduously, but those especially who handle the rudders of the church; lest by any means it come to pass, that, unwittingly, they lend their labour unto this most cruel enemy, toward the accomplishing of his wicked deeds. *Woe unto the wretched man to whom that shall have befallen; for if unto him who shall have called his brother a fool, the punishment of everlasting fire is set forth, and it were better for any man, a millstone being hung from his neck, to be sunk in the sea, than to offend any, even the least of those who give faith unto Christ; and if, according to the greatness of their sins and crimes, every man shall suffer punishments; whereas it has been said unto certain towns, in which many miracles had been wrought without fruit, that it shall be more tolerable in the day of judgment with Tyre, and Sidon, and Sodom, than with them:* what punishments think we await those men who have conspired with Satan unto the destruction of very many peoples, and unto the desolation of the kingdom of Christ?

See what a difference there is: whether thou dost only call thy brother a fool, or takest away his life, and that not only the temporal, but the eternal; and not from one man only, but from many; and not from many men only, but from many peoples. Neither shall it excuse thee, that thou didst it ignorantly; since thou mightest, by the oracles of God delivered unto thee, by which thou wast warned what thou oughtest to follow, and what to beware of, not to have been ignorant. If, I say, for so light an offence the punishment of eternal fire is to be undergone, what shall be done for this so huge a wicked deed? See what this matter is: of the greatest praises of God, and of those set above all human thought, we can scarce name any greater, than that for our redemption he delivered his own Son unto death.

What manner of deed, moreover, shall his be, who shall have afforded unto any man any cause of destruction, and how greatly to be detested before God? Woe therefore unto you, who, catching at empty air — that ye may seem to be of some value, whether in wit, or in learning — or drawn on by an unseasonable curiosity, never make an end of sowing controversies, strifes, and brawls. Woe unto you, who, that ye may indulge your hatreds, your envy, your emulation, and may serve an estimation of yourselves, I know not what, do not only not restrain and settle strifes, but also provoke minds more and more, and so render them immortal. Woe unto you, who, whether by depravity of mind, or even by carelessness, bring no reverence unto the handling of matters of so great moment, nor weigh what ye do, and so fill all things with offences. Woe unto you, who, with your eyes fixed only upon your own dignity, estimation, and the opinion the common sort has of you, that ye may obtain among men the place of certain gods, hold your brethren in contempt, afflict them, oppress them, and usurp unto yourselves a certain dominion in their consciences; and so build walls round about the kingdom of Satan, and rear up bulwarks for it: woe, say I, unto you, woe, woe! How horrible punishments await you in that day! and unto men of that order — for what else would it be, than to lose my labour and my oil? Unto those I speak them, who, I hope, are yet endowed with ears, wherewith to hear; who, since they love Christ, are assailed by Satan with his wholly crafty snares; let every man beware for himself, lest he use the labour of them, who persuade themselves of no such thing, unto his own attempts. The Papacy was not first born at Rome, but draws its origin even from our first parents; there is none of us, who does not carry his own Papacy in his breast, most certainly to bring it forth upon whatsoever first occasion, unless with great prudence he shall have searched himself out, and shall plainly have done violence unto himself. Let them understand, who have ears.

Woe unto that wretched man, to whom it shall have befallen to behold the greatest multitude of damned men, unto whose destruction, of what kind soever, he himself, even unknowing, has contributed his labour; especially if, being admonished, he has not even taken heed unto himself: who, I beseech you, shall be able to bear the severity of the Lord's countenance, joined with the highest majesty? What courage shall there then be? What mind? What sense? Likewise, brethren, let ambitions, smokes, angers, grudges, strifes, discords, and all other affections, take their leave; let there be of us all one heart, and one will; let Christ be the one mark of us all, and the glory of his name; let us at length shake off that sleep, through which it comes to pass, that we cannot discern what snares have been laid for us.

And let no man applaud himself, that he now sails in harbour, that he is now set beyond all danger, that exhortations of this kind will be profitable unto others — for I repeat that which I had said a little before — whosoever he be at length, who so reasons with himself, that he is either nearest unto the snare, or is already held most straitly bound. That, forsooth, is the speech of Satan, whereby he renders a man secure, and foolishly confident; whose manner is, then chiefly to promise peace, when there is anything rather than peace. Beware for thyself, say I; beware — nay rather, since we know what an enemy is set before us, let us put on the arms which we have now spoken of; and let this be our chief carefulness, that we never cease to be afraid; nay rather, the greater progress we shall seem to have made against him, the more let us certainly resolve that we must beware of him; so that it may come to pass, that we never cease to strive with God in prayer, that he would show us the snares — but, say I, to every man his own; not only those which are set for our neighbour; for the necessity of so praying is utterly greater than all belief.

FINIS.
