*The Stratagems of Satan. Book One.* The most convenient approach to learning Satan's arts is this: if we look into what the end of all his designs is — and that is ready to hand. For since he is said to have been a murderer from the very beginning, what other end are we to believe this to be than the destruction of man, and that an everlasting destruction? Since, then, he is the greatest old deceiver, there is no room for doubt that he is contriving whatever makes for man's ruin; and it is equally certain that he will strive to turn man away from whatever could avail for his salvation. Now man's salvation consists in this, that he obey God; his destruction, if he do the contrary. “If you wish to obtain life” (says the Lord) “keep the commandments.” Here, moreover, by the name of a divine commandment, or of the divine law, we take everything that we are commanded not only to do or to avoid, but also to hold as persuaded. Hence Satan holds this before himself as his aim: that we should not obey the divine law. And a man may indeed break the law, both one to whom the law is known and one to whom it is not known; for although you sin much more grievously if, having come to know God's will, you do not obey it, than if you do it through ignorance, yet ignorance will not make anyone guiltless, for there is hardly a case in which some fault does not lie in a man's ignorance.

But these two things — ignorance of the divine law, and obstinacy against it — are so joined together that each is somehow the cause of the other. For because you are ignorant of God's will, you act contrary to what he wills, very often thinking that you are doing a thing most pleasing to him, from which you incur his anger — a thing which, among other results, brings it about that you are deprived of all the light of right judgment, and are day by day overwhelmed by denser darkness. If you hold the law and do not obey it, you will fall away, by the most just judgment of God, from the knowledge of it and slip into some great errors. And since this is the end of Satan's designs that we have stated — since, that is, Satan's business is with man, to destroy him — then, so that we may the more easily get at those designs, man's nature must be carefully considered. Although man was at the beginning so created that he obtained a nature that was good, upright, and perfect in every part, yet, God's prohibition having been violated, he fell from that nature into another wholly contrary to the former — a most corrupt nature, that is, and one liable to every vice.

Hence, first, he loves himself beyond measure, but with a love that is blind and intemperate; led by it, he shrinks from his own true good, while what is evil for him he pursues — just as our first parents gave credence to the serpent when he said, “As soon as you have tasted this fruit, you will be as gods, endowed with the knowledge of good and evil.” So deeply has that persuasion stuck in the human race that each man seems to himself to be, as it were, a kind of god. And this he shows at once whenever some occasion offers. For as soon as he has been raised to some dignity or to ampler fortune, he thinks that everyone owes him everything, so as to serve his convenience even at their own inconvenience; that they should reverence him and honour him; and that he himself may by the best of rights use any man, and all his goods, as his own pleasure inclines him. If he acquires any learning, he at once thinks that he knows everything, and that he alone knows it, so that the whole world ought to be governed by his wisdom. And to win such an opinion of himself he will stop at nothing, nor will he be able to understand that in this he does less than rightly; or if he does understand, he will not much care. He is wonderfully given to fulfilling the pleasures of the body, and in those matters he practises the utmost intemperance. He loves this life, and devotes all his thoughts to it; of the other life he does not so much as think. He dreams that this life will last for ever; and although many thousands of men fall daily before his eyes, he can never seriously take in that he himself was born under the same condition, so that one day he must die.

And because whatever you may anywhere desire seems capable of being obtained by wealth, and wealth is spent on fulfilling desires, while on the other hand the things you would consume over an infinite time seem to need to be infinite in number, there arises a certain insatiable desire of heaping up wealth — as for Alexander the Great, both for this very reason and because of a certain unheard-of helplessness of mind, the empire of the whole world seemed far too narrow. About knowledge of God and of his will, and likewise of his own true good and evil — which rests wholly in God's favour or God's displeasure — he is altogether blind, yet in his own eyes he seems most sharp-sighted. To assent to truth in this kind, and to cast away errors, is as repugnant to his nature as it is for any heavy thing to seek the heights — not because he hates the true as true, or delights in the false as false, but rather because in these matters he uses a corrupted judgment, so that he takes false things for true and true things for false. And as he is himself disposed, without understanding that he is so, so he supposes God to be; and he wishes to worship him, as one so disposed, with gold, silver, precious stones and jewels, with great and costly buildings, and sometimes also with such things that, if one man wished to honour another with them, he would think himself laughed at and insulted.

He wants to have him visible, and for that reason he fashions him for himself according to his own fancy. He cannot seriously persuade himself that God knows human affairs, or cares for them, or governs them — that he will give rewards or exact punishments; whence he is turned aside from committing those things which are said to belong to God's displeasure neither by piety toward God nor by fear of punishment. Moreover, each man has no eyes to look into himself, to see what he sees in another; for what belongs to another he is lynx-eyed; he easily interprets it in a bad sense, and is exceedingly suspicious. But if once a man begins to defer to another man — especially in those things which touch divine matters — in that he oversteps all the bounds of right reason, and wishes to make a kind of god for himself out of him. Otherwise, he wants things to go well for himself alone; he alone wants to excel in glory. And so, if any advantage or praise comes to another, he envies it; he hinders it, if he can, so that the addition is not made; he belittles the other man's praises, magnifies his falls, and slanders him. If anyone resists him ever so little in anything, he is strongly inclined to anger and hatred, and cannot easily lay aside offences; nay, rather, he whets himself more and more to bloodshed, to destruction, and to every monstrous kind of revenge.

Such by his nature is man: arrogant beyond measure, haughty in spirit, intemperate, covetous, insatiable, greedy for what is another's, a supplanter, a liar, contentious, envious, greedy for revenge, a murderer, blind, headlong, stubborn, impious, and born for every crime. To draw it into a sum: man's nature — the corrupt nature, that is, which we live by — is little unlike the nature of impure demons. It is true, however, that the things we have said, and those we could still have added, are not seen equally in all men; for different traits show more in different men, and because either the capacity or the opportunity is lacking, some vices fail to come forth. The constitution of the body, upbringing, habit, pursuits, and many other such things carry much weight; they both make a man more inclined to vices and draw him away from them; yet there is no one in whom the seeds of all the vices are not present. Nevertheless, certain traces of the former nature still remain in man: an obscure knowledge, as it were, of just and unjust, of honourable and dishonourable; and that rewards are owed to good deeds and punishments to evil deeds; and he seems born for a desire of knowing and learning. There is in him a certain power of arriving, from things perceived by sense, at the knowledge of things that cannot be perceived by sense — so much so that by such industry he raises himself even to some knowledge, I know not what, of the divine nature.

He has some suspicion of another life, a life that will last for ever — blessed for the good, but for the bad full of troubles and torments; witness the fables of the poets, which, since their speech is fitted to the opinions of the common people, are stuffed with examples bearing on this. Joined with this feeble opinion of another life there is also a certain desire for salvation, led by which desire he seeks ways of winning the divine will over to himself, and of worshipping God. But these remains of a good sense are so overwhelmed by the great and manifold force of the corrupted nature that in most men they utterly vanish, while in some they struggle mightily against the corruption of nature, as may be seen in Socrates, Seneca, and some others; yet that force is never so great that, unless helped by divine aid, it can be of any weight toward salvation. Nay, in the end it seems to lead man into denser darkness. But this vexes Satan: that God, in his supreme goodness, follows the human race with great love, and from time to time chooses for himself servants from the human race itself, endows them with the acknowledgment (knowledge) of his law, and sends them to peoples to instruct them.

But since man's nature is such as we have set it out, and since we have said that the end set before all Satan's designs is the one we have named, the way by which that end may be reached must be searched out.

There are, in all, two states, in one or other of which a people must always be: for either it lives in the light of truth, or it is enveloped in the darkness of errors and ignorance. Nor do we understand the light ever to be so great that no mists of error remain — which had to be pointed out, lest mention of error, when we come to deal with the time of light, should seem out of place. Now when a people enjoys the light of truth, Satan tries to pour darkness over it; and if there is darkness, he strives that it remain for ever. And a man may live in the darkness of ignorance either if he practises no religion at all, or if he practises a false and corrupt one. Again, a man may also be without religion at all: both one who has never even heard by report anything that touches religion, nor has thought about it, and one who is persuaded that there is no religion — which is the case with those monsters among men who either deny that God exists at all, or deny that he cares for human affairs. That there should ever be a people so savage that in it there is not even any thought of God seems hardly credible; but that there is never not some number of lost men who are persuaded that the thought of God is empty and foolish is more certain than to need proof; and Satan uses these men as his slaves for the perpetration of every most monstrous crime.

But once the true and godly doctrine of religion has existed in any people, then, for it to slip back from that doctrine into errors, a change of opinions must necessarily take place. Now a people either notices that a change is taking place, or does not notice it. If it does notice it, then — for it to fall away from the doctrine it approved to some other, false and impious one — some cause must arise that brings the change about. And the causes why a change of true opinions about religion into false ones takes place are: false arguments; movements of the mind, such as desire, anger, hatred; a good opinion of those who hold wrong views; and, of those who teach true things, a bad opinion; and lying signs, or prodigies. By false arguments we mean arguments from which something false is somehow concluded, which happens in several ways. For sometimes everything assumed is false — as when they argue in this way: that the Mass (as they call it) must be retained because the Roman Church has had it in use from the times of the Apostles onward, and because everything that has long been in use in the Roman Church must be retained.

Or what is assumed is partly true and partly false — which happens when they argue in this way: because the institutions of the Apostles must be kept (which is true), and the enumeration of sins into the ear of a priest was an institution of the Apostles (which is false), therefore the custom of so enumerating sins must be kept. At other times the things assumed are true, but they have nothing to do with the matter — as if you were to say: the distinction of foods must be retained according to the prescription of the Roman Church, because fish, vegetables, and other things of that kind nourish less than meats and eggs. At other times the arguments are such that, although something seems to be concluded, nothing is in fact concluded — such, plainly, as the arguments of the sophists are. Let this be an example: it is not lawful for a Christian man to take his sister to wife; but every Christian woman is a sister; therefore it is not lawful for a Christian to marry a Christian woman. But the movements of the mind that can cause a change of opinions from true to false are various: some of them relate to those who profess true things, others to those who profess false things; others, finally, regard the doctrine itself, both the true and the false. Hatred can be taken up against the true doctrine, evidently because it accuses man, and forbids the things for which he is held by desire, and commands the things which he would do unwillingly.

For whatever doctrine you come to hate, from that you will easily leap away. Toward a false doctrine, on the other hand, there can be love, because it lets the reins run freer toward pleasures. Of the professors of the truth it breeds a bad opinion, if they themselves seem to believe little of what they teach — which happens if their life is at odds with their words — and if they seem to look more to their own advantages and their own interests than to be held by zeal for the true religion. It also takes much credit away from them if God seems to approve their cause little, pressing them with calamities and afflictions — which, however, happens only among those who measure things by their own judgment, not by the rules handed down from God. The contrary things win a good opinion for the professors of false doctrine. False signs are sometimes things falsely narrated, and sometimes things which, though they have been fabricated and contrived either by men or by Satan, are believed to have been produced by God.

Now the things that change opinions either come into each man's mind of themselves, or there must necessarily be some few seducers who propose them to the rest — which is what generally happens, for otherwise hardly any notable change could come about. And the seducers either teach publicly against the received doctrine, or secretly; and either with someone opposing, or with no one opposing. But that anyone could openly contradict the received doctrine with no one opposing him, there would have to be in a people absolutely no diversity of minds — which cannot be — and if those who oppose are even more powerful than those who favour, they will hinder the seducer, without doubt. Indeed, at a time when the true worship of God exists in a people, if anyone tries to sow false doctrines, there will be more who oppose than who favour — unless, perhaps, the whole people were in the worst possible disposition — and the seducer would have to use the greatest art for the purpose, as, for instance, if he had first acquired some great reputation for wisdom and for holiness, or if he abused the authority of those who were held to be such men. This is what those men once did who brought letters to the churches as if sent by apostles, or thrust upon them other writings inscribed with false titles, and by such imposture seduced the simple.

Such was the Gospel of Nicodemus, and the Itinerary of Peter, and other things. Nor were whole writings only foisted, as offspring, on those who were not their parents; there was also the complaint of many that their true offspring had been corrupted. So among the works of Augustine and of other most famous men there are not a few spurious works, and many passages corrupted. So great was the shamelessness of some men that, even while the authors were still alive, they inserted into their writings the things they wanted to persuade men of — the very thing that Origen complains happened to him.

Otherwise, it seems that errors could hardly be spread at the beginning in any other way than among a few, and secretly. Indeed, it can also be understood here that a seducer can secretly turn some few away from the received doctrine, but that he cannot turn very many without its at last becoming open — since a noticed change can hardly take place without controversies. And those who oppose resist either by reason or by authority: by reason, if someone defends the received doctrine and attacks the new one by speech or by writings, whether he uses argumentations or testimonies; by authority, if those who preside over the churches, or the churches themselves by their common votes, condemn that new doctrine as impious and repugnant to the word of God, and if the magistrate, under a penalty laid down, forbids it to be taught or defended.

Now just as, if the seducers are properly resisted, all Satan's designs are defeated, so it is most likely that Satan bends all his wiles, and the whole force of his cunning, to this end: that whatever is done in order to resist him should not only not hinder his designs, but should even help and greatly further them. It will therefore be worth the effort to understand by what stratagems he achieves this. First, then, as regards reason — the reason customarily set against the seducers, which is done by disputing, whether that is done in person or between absent parties — this is certain: that it has the force to resist a false doctrine only on these terms — if it conveniently makes plain both the weakness of the foundations on which that doctrine rests and the firmness of those on which the received doctrine rests; and, besides, if those with whom the business lies have weighed it carefully with a calm mind and an uncorrupted judgment. Unless, therefore, both the reasoning is of that kind, and the speech has the power of bringing its opponents to this tranquillity of mind and (so to speak) soundness of judgment — but has rather the power to bring about all the opposite — it will not resist Satan; it will, in a wonderful manner, favour him.

But to weigh what reasoning is fitted to an opponent's mind and judgment, and what is not, no one can do unless he himself is of a calm mind, and remembers to weigh it, and to do so carefully. And since one must be of a calm mind for this, the things that commonly disturb the mind must not lie hidden. And you will generally be stirred, if it privately matters to you that no change of doctrine take place — which falls upon all those who discharge the office of teaching. For no change of doctrine can take place without loss to their dignity and reputation, as though they had taught things less than true, and without a great deal also falling away from their advantages; Love of the doctrine itself also brings it about that you cannot easily bear a man who contradicts you with an even mind; and as every likeness of character, of pursuits, and of opinions is of the greatest force for winning affections, so every unlikeness has weight toward hatred; and if insults and accusations from an opponent are added, minds must necessarily be stirred with a still much greater force.

And you will suitably weaken nothing unless you have first understood what it is. But a man who does not listen attentively, who does not listen patiently, who does not suspend his judgment right to the end, cannot sufficiently grasp what is being said; but when at every first word he seems to himself to divine what the other man means, and anticipates his words with his own judgment, when that man has meant one thing and this man has understood another, he refutes not his opponent's arguments, but those he has fashioned for himself by his own rashness and false interpretation. And these things happen at the time when an opponent is held in hatred and when we are angry with him, but most of all if we despise him. Contempt has as its primary, and almost sole, cause this: if you are too pleased with yourself; if you claim too much for yourself, whether in knowledge or in judgment. If there is any fault, moreover, to which man seems to be born, it is arrogance, which gathers strength and growth from various things.

In this kind, first: if a high opinion of some man on the part of many seems to be added to him; if a few show themselves to think otherwise, and those few are themselves held in small esteem. Joined with this: if a man seems to himself to have already given a great and certain proof of his own wisdom and judgment — which happens to those who have generally, or always, come out of disputations as victors, and who, since they often speak before the people, rarely have anyone to contradict them, or never have; for unless they take care with great prudence, they can hardly fail to seem wise to themselves alone, but despise all others in comparison with themselves, and count them as nothing. And that saying is well known: “Compliance breeds friends; truth breeds hatred.” Therefore, those whose lot it is that they seem able to do good and to do harm, to oblige and to disoblige, to confer honour and to confer disgrace — no one will readily contradict them; rather, many will flatter them. And do not suppose you need to be the Roman pontiff to find flatterers. For since nowadays not very many people give their attention to sacred letters, apart from those who have turned their minds to ecclesiastical offices, concerned for the most part more about their belly than about anything better — offices which are obtained either by the votes or by the recommendation of those men, namely the pastors of each church, who are of outstanding authority — although those pastors are otherwise not at all wealthy, you would nevertheless see most people flatter them no differently than if they were gaping after the richest priesthoods at that Roman Idol. What will happen if any great wealth is joined with the authority to promote men to offices? But neither is this a thing of no account: that (as custom now has it in many places) every man's life and reputation is exposed to the censure of the preachers (as they call them), so that if you incur their offence, and are plainly overwhelmed by censure and infamy — so that everyone prefers to have them as friends rather than as enemies, and that you would not easily find anyone who wished to oppose them unless he were led by a most serious cause. If some learning is joined with these things — learning which by itself, as Paul testifies [Marginal note: 1 Cor. 1], not through any fault of its own (since it is a great gift of God), but through the fault of human nature, is wont to puff a man up — there will be a very great addition to arrogance and insolence. Therefore, if anyone of this number has to resist a seducer, unless he has carefully weighed what authority, learning, abundance of wealth (if there is any), and celebrity of name can otherwise do, it will be a great wonder if he does not act insolently and arrogantly, so as to cast at his opponent whatever comes into his mouth, and nevertheless be exceedingly pleased with himself; so as to wish to do all the speaking himself, and often to triumph before the victory has been won — so that a good cause, having got a bad defender, now begins to seem less good.

[Marginal note: Men do not dispute today, but brawl, fight with swords, hotly, abusively.] This also generally happens in practice: whoever holds something with certain persuasion, and holds the reason why he thinks so, can somehow hardly fail to wonder at anyone for not seeing what he himself sees; and so, unless the other man yields when the reason has scarcely been indicated in a single word, he flares up, as if it were certain that this was being done out of perversity and obstinacy of mind; and nothing is so near as that it should come to insults. And since a disturbed mind can neither understand nor judge rightly, if, for the man who is led by error, besides the error's being in any case refuted — and that not very suitably — his mind is in addition disturbed by abusive words (which is generally done by those who think they are fighting from a superior position), that man will be able to acknowledge his error much less. Such is the disposition of man, born, that is, to excessive exaltation of mind and pride, that in all things, if he can, he wishes to seem to excel others. Thus he yields to anyone very unwillingly, or confesses his own slips; and as each man does anything most unwillingly, so he understands with the greatest difficulty that he ought to do it. As proof of this, let there be those who litigate in the courts about anything whatever.

For how great a business is it to persuade those who unjustly claim something, or deny it, that they are acting unjustly? And the more their minds have grown hot with contention, the harder and the more grievous does yielding seem. Therefore, if, besides your having shown the erring man his error, not very suitably and not very clearly, and besides your having disturbed his mind with harsh words, this is added — that you unseasonably exaggerate the magnitude and foulness of the error, as if you seemed to wonder that anyone had been of so little judgment, so stupid and dull, as to be able to blunder so shamefully, or you say anything of the kind — what else will that have been than to snatch away from the man whatever judgment was left to him, and to drive him outright to madness? So that he can now do nothing less than either suspect any error at all, let alone openly acknowledge and confess it.

If, then, these things are admitted into disputations — that the error is not clearly shown to the man who, himself led by error, was seducing others; that the man who has opposed himself to the seducer does not understand what the seducer says, and on that account overturns not those things, but the things he had fashioned for himself by false suspicion, and defends the received doctrine not by the reasons by which he ought, but by certain inept ones, such as rashness and a mind maddened by anger could gather together — which it would be easy to dissolve, and the man who errs thinks himself not defeated, but insolently afflicted with many and grave injuries: disputations of this kind, although they seem to be instituted to oppose Satan, nevertheless break none of his plans; nothing could be more apt for advancing his designs. For they do not attack errors, but render them invincible; they do not wrest them away, but scatter them far and wide; they do not remove them, but propagate them, and in a wonderful way at that. They render them invincible for this reason: that the man who is in error, since from the things brought against him he has an easy and ready escape, has good cause to persuade himself that stronger things cast against him are not so; and when he has seen that his own arguments are not refuted, but certain other things — I know not what — which he himself never even thought of, why should he not believe that this was done for the reason that, since they had nothing with which to answer his arguments, and yet wished to seem to answer them, they had quibbled at his words? By these things he is plainly compelled to determine for certain that it is not he who is engaged in error, but those who oppose him. And if bitter words, full of insolence and injuries, are also added, and, if you please, threats too, and other such things, what is left here except that he should persuade himself most firmly that his opponents — since they cannot defend their cause by reason, have placed all their reliance in force alone, and in insolence? And since hatreds are conceived from injuries, what can confirm errors with greater obstinacy? And since each man leaves his descendants heirs of his errors as much as of his hatreds, they can never be torn out of men's minds. Thus peoples are split into sects, which pursue one another with a hatred more than Vatinian, sparing no kind of injury; in which matter men indulge themselves all the more because they do not seem to themselves, in doing so, to be obeying the emotions of their own minds, but to be doing a thing exceedingly pleasing to God — while in the meantime they both incur God's wrath in greater measure day by day and are overwhelmed by denser darkness. Those disputations scatter errors far and wide, because the noises that are made in them, and the brawls that arise from them, furnish matter for talk to many; and since there is everywhere a great diversity of minds and judgments, matters are both related and received in varying ways. And so, if many disapprove of the error, it nevertheless pleases some, and some also — just as they disapprove of the error itself — will at the same time disapprove of the things that were employed to attack it, such as insolence, abusive words, and other things of that kind, and will therefore begin to be less pleased with those who opposed themselves to the error — from which they afterwards admit some other error more easily. And a man to whom the error is pleasing, when he relates matters, can hardly fail to favour the party of which he approves, so as to add, take away, and change many things at his own discretion; and so the evil creeps on like a kind of contagion, and is spread far and wide. Finally, errors are propagated from this source in many ways. For first, the man who opposes himself to an error can scarcely take care not to fall himself also into some error: either because (as is said in the proverb), in order to avoid Charybdis, he is carried into Scylla, not knowing how to hold the middle course — which he would do if a man, in order to oppose those who seem to attribute everything to the written word of God and to the reading of it, and who do not perceive that there is need of the Spirit of God as interpreter and guide for understanding it rightly, should try to transfer men's minds to divine inspirations alone; for the Spirit was to be sought in such a way that the written word's own dignity should be preserved, not as if it were some human invention, but as a thing handed down by God. Or because he allows the seducer something that ought not to be allowed — which perhaps some of the ancient fathers did, who so resisted the philosophers that they contaminated the purity of Christian doctrines with philosophical reasonings. It can also happen that, when you think that a view which — is to be held as true can be expressed in words more significant and more open than it was expressed in the divine letters, and that the handle for quibbling is thereby better removed (for the human mind never does not wish to be more cautious and wiser than God), you use words, or forms of speaking, such that from them another, less true and less godly, view can at some time be gathered. Moreover, since in whatever controversy arises, many things are usually brought forward on both sides, each man for his own party: if the contention has become somewhat sharpened, and has inflamed minds, you will easily see not a few things both asserted and denied by each party which, with minds composed, they would never either have asserted or denied; for that saying comes to pass: “Rage furnishes arms.”

Whatever weapon chance has cast in the way is seized; rage does not allow it to be noticed of what kind it is; and whatever has once rashly slipped out, especially in the midst of contention, pride wishes to stand ratified and fixed; and from this new controversies arise, and in the same way new errors without end. It is also evident that by contentions both the matters themselves and men's minds are alike thrown into confusion; truth is lost, and many are driven to persuade themselves that nothing certain can be established, and so cast away all zeal for religion. This too can happen — that while men brawl perpetually about some one point of religion, many other things, as if despised, fall into oblivion, and perhaps the chief things; and that a certain custom of that kind arises, by which a godly man is now distinguished from an ungodly man by this one thing alone (whatever his life may otherwise be) — that he seems to abhor very strongly the doctrine which is held heretical in one point or another.

O, if it were before our eyes how many and how great disasters Satan has brought upon Christ's kingdom, and what devastations he has made with this single weapon of contentions, a man would plainly be made of iron who could hold back his tears. Nor, indeed, am I now complaining of those fanatical spirits, Satan's emissaries, who never cease to sow prying, empty, and ungodly quarrels — for what else would you expect from them? — but much more of those who, although they love the true worship of God, conduct themselves in religious controversies without sufficient consideration, and do not perceive that, very often, with a kind of zeal for the honour of God and for preserving pure doctrine — but a very headlong zeal — they do many things by which, although they think they are providing beautifully for Christ's cause, they nevertheless so do Satan's business that they scarcely leave anything for him to wish for. Indeed, I except not even ordinary pastors — I mean certain men unskilled in things — but each of the foremost men, the very pillars of the church, as I call them; nor, indeed, those of this age only, but of all ages; the prophets (as they are called) and the apostles I except. For let me be allowed at some time to grieve for the church's lot, and to give my tears to the common calamities. Let whoever has no share in this fault know that he is in no way touched here; we accuse no one by name. Indeed, to whomever this fault may belong, whatever we say, we say it not to assail or brand them, but to remove a corruption than which no pestilence in the church can be more pernicious. We have not here declared war on men, whom we desire both to be saved and to be most highly adorned, but on Satan. We pursue, as we said in our preface, only the things themselves, which cannot be endured without the most certain ruin of the Christian commonwealth. But if there are any who think that this cannot be done without their being, as it were, pointed out with the finger, I should gladly like to understand from them whether it would have been more just for me to have regard to their dignity and reputation, or to the public safety. Certainly, if they are what they wish to seem — lovers of God and of his honour — they will not only take this office of ours in good part, but will also give great thanks to God that at last, through someone (whoever he may be), he has made it plain that those plans are not from them which they themselves thought to be especially necessary for the church's safety. But if they are those (which I do not in the least suspect) who — [Marginal note: In handling controversies the greatest gentleness and mildness are necessary.] — regard their own dignity as more important than all things, it was no part of my office to postpone to their reputation either the good condition and safety of the commonwealth or the honour of God. Nor, indeed, was their favour worth more to me than God's favour had to be. Alas for the madness of men! The Gospel had occupied almost the whole world; there was so much contention, so many lawsuits, so many brawls about the very points of religion, and generally about things of no great weight, that it gradually almost perished; certainly, in some corner or other there survived only certain small remains. In our age also, by a certain incredible goodness of God, some light had risen for us; but what are we doing? Are we perhaps, terrified by the example of our ancestors, diligently inquiring how it happened that the Christian people was first torn into sects, and afterwards, together with godly and holy doctrine, also lost all discipline of morals, so that the greatest part of it did not retain even the very name of the Gospel? O unheard-of sloth, worthy of infinite tears! Indeed, as if there existed no example by which we might be warned what to avoid, we proceed again in the same footsteps, with closed eyes, to overturn everything. And we omit nothing by which we may make a devastation greater than any that has ever been.

How many sects arose in former times, and grew to an immense size — sects of which, if at the beginning some gentleness and mildness had been employed in the controversies, if patience, if modesty, if zeal for peace had been employed, not even the names would ever have reached us! But we, as soon as anyone seems to put a foot outside the path, think that nothing else need be done than at once to sound the trumpet, to fill everything with insults, reproaches, and clamours, and with whatever comes into our mouth — when often nothing is more inept — what is cast at him seems most apt, and we think that in this way we discharge our office most beautifully, and that great praise is therefore owed to us: most prudent men, forsooth, who bring oil to quench a fire! Indeed, here, where there is need of the most considered counsels, let wraths be gone, let arrogance be gone, let clamours be gone; and let reason alone rule here — not the reason which Satan governs, but the Spirit of God.

In the first place, moreover, we must beware again and again that no unnecessary controversy be stirred up. Does something perhaps offend your ear, as strange? Your own impulse, indeed — a teacher by no means fit for any skilfulness — will urge you to run to meet it at once, so that, with it as your author and Satan instigating, you may headlong, at the least occasion, level the highest things with the lowest.

But, I say, the matter is great, full of great danger; here there is need of counsel, not of rashness. For what if what you have heard does indeed present some appearance of novelty, while in fact there is none in it, but this only happens: that you have interpreted the words contrary to the meaning of the man who spoke them? What madness, then, would it be, where there is agreement about the things themselves, to stir up quarrels about words, and, as it were, to beat the clouds with your fists? But if you approach the man, if you ask him in a friendly and humane way what he means, once the words have been explained, every occasion for controversy will have been removed. And if something is altogether strange, even then it is not at once advisable to listen to that most cunning sower of brawls sounding the trumpet; but since it has been said that no people is ever in so great a light that there are not some clouds of errors, it must first be carefully weighed whether it agrees or not with the doctrine which is contained in the divine letters, lest it happen that, when you think you are resisting some error, you resist the truth. But that this ought to be done — that the truth should never be resisted, however much appearance of falsity it may in the end have — there is no one who does not most willingly confess; but that you yourself should beware of it, when the time comes — that is as hard to do as anything can be.

For whatever teaching anyone has once drunk in, he cannot persuade himself that it is not true; and accordingly he holds that whatever disagrees with it is false, and that it must be resisted, lest it should find belief. But if all men were so constituted that they reject whatever savours of novelty, no error that existed in the Church could be rooted out by any means; rather, from that one error many would in time spring up one after another. What, then, is to be done? This at least is beyond all doubt: that if those who live in errors were so constituted that they were willing to hear, patiently and with calm and composed minds, those who disagree with them, and to weigh diligently the strength of their reasons — even as if it could somehow come about (whatever it may meanwhile seem) that they themselves were erring — most of them would be called back from their errors; but they obstinately persist in them for this reason, that they can come to no suspicion at all that they are in error, so that whoever wants to be taught otherwise than they themselves think, they reckon he should be crushed at once, his case unheard. Who, then, does not understand that the best counsel for them would be that whatever they heard foreign to their own persuasions they should not think it must be rejected forthwith, but should pause a little, and each one should think thus in his own mind: "To me indeed the things seem true which I have up to now believed with the common crowd; but what if the same thing should happen to me as commonly happens to very many — that precisely in the matter in which I seem to myself to be most certain, I am in fact most in error? Why, then, should I not first hear the man? Why should I not weigh what he says?"

It is Paul's precept: "Despise not prophesyings: prove all things: hold fast that which is good." [Marginal note: 1. Thes. 5.] And I would gladly learn from you — whoever you are who think that all error is, in your most certain opinion, far from you; "think," I say; indeed, and from you too who plainly see it and feel it: by what means do you think those who err can be brought to suspect that they are erring, and thereby be made a little more attentive to hear? By that means, I think: that it is certain that this happens to far too many, and not only to those from the unlearned crowd, but also to the most learned men (as the almost endless controversies stirred up among them testify) — that although they think they most certainly hold right views, they are nevertheless involved in the greatest errors. And so those men act prudently who, remembering that they are men, and that they themselves may be deceived just as much as any others (even though they do not perceive that they are erring), nevertheless, as if they could err, offer themselves as attentive hearers to those who profess that they can teach them better things; and they do this in such a way that they nevertheless admit nothing except after diligent examination, with divine help implored for the purpose.

"All that is quite so," you say; "but come: among whom do you want this example and this reasoning to have force? Among those who hold right views? That is not what you want, I think, but among those alone who err. But this is the very thing I would like to know, plainly: how are you going to prove that this reasoning applies more to those who err than to those who do not err? Is it by this very fact, that they err? But they do not even perceive that it is possible for them to err; and that is still the point at issue in this reasoning — that they be brought to some suspicion of error. But if those who err think they are as certainly free from error as those who hold right views — indeed, generally much more certainly, since (as the wise man says) his own reasonings smile most beautifully upon a fool, whereas a wise man seeks counsel — what other way there is of recognising error, if there is any in the Church, I do not see, than that this be laid down as a general rule: that, because it is so proper to man to err, no one at all, provided he is a man, ought to be so confident as to hold himself persuaded that he cannot err, and accordingly, whatever at length comes into controversy, to think that he will hear anyone's words on the matter in vain. But," you say, "one must not waver in the doctrine of religion; rather, one must hold as ascertained what one holds." That is true, and for that reason the knot is most difficult to untie.

To resist the truth, and not to recognise an error and cast it away if there is someone who can point it out, is exceedingly bad. But a man who errs cannot come to a recognition of his error before he thinks it possible that he is being led by some error, so that he prepares himself to listen; yet neither is it permitted to waver in the doctrine of religion. By what thread, then, shall we at last find an exit from such perplexing windings? But hope must not be thrown away: an exit must absolutely be found. And this much at least is now certain: the truth must never be resisted. But neither is this doubtful: that a man who errs, while he errs, cannot understand that he is erring; and it is also plain that it is the greatest hindrance to the recognition of the truth if a man is vehemently persuaded that he is led by no error, and that the right way to the recognition of error, and its first entrance, is this: if any suspicion of error should occur. What, then? Are we to approve a perpetual hesitation in the affairs of faith? Not that either; rather, I think effort must be made that we attain knowledge as certain as it can be. But what, in the end? Since a man may be persuaded about some matter both well — believing what the thing actually is — and badly, believing otherwise than it is; and since the man who is well persuaded may be persuaded both in a perfect manner — which happens to him who has attained certain and clear knowledge — and in an imperfect manner, which happens to those who indeed embrace true things but are led only by opinion, and cannot give a certain account of why they think so: this is clear, that the man in whom there is certain knowledge, so that he has true, constant, and infallible grounds for his opinion which he can give, cannot now be brought into doubt about that matter, or be led astray.

Hence, whatever advice you give him, urging him to try to come to a suspicion of error, that advice can in no way harm him — at least in those matters of which he has knowledge; and much less can it harm a man who is imbued with false opinions. For a man who so errs that he even suspects that he is erring no longer errs in every respect, but clings partly to error and partly to truth; and the more he fears that he is erring, the nearer he is to the recognition of the truth. It remains, therefore, to see what must be said about those who, although they embrace true opinions, have not yet attained knowledge of the matters. In their case, a suspicion of error is nothing other than a kind of approach toward error — a thing which cannot in itself seem not to be bad — but it is also an error if you falsely arrogate knowledge to yourself when you have only opinion. And as error cannot fail to be counted among evils, so it is also an evil to be in danger of some pernicious error.

But a man who is led only by opinions, and does not hold what he holds as ascertained, may be led astray into error. He is therefore in danger that this may happen; but it is best to be removed from all danger. Wherefore, just as, although all pain is counted among evils, yet if it is undergone for the sake of driving away a grave disease it seems to have the character of a good, and a notable one, so too, since opinion is a kind of weak and invalid knowledge, and, if not a kind of disease of the mind, at least a condition exposed to disease, the man who casts a suspicion of error into someone — by recalling how many there are who, though they persuade themselves of nothing less, are nevertheless sunk in the greatest darkness — so as to show him the way of attaining certain and constant knowledge, ought to seem to be giving not a pernicious counsel, but a salutary and exceedingly necessary one. In order, therefore, that you may never make trouble for a man who is prepared to teach true things, you must either attain knowledge — as full and as certain as possible — of all the things that can ever come into controversy in the Church (and who does not see how great a task that is?), or at least, if ever you hear something a little more unusual, before you condemn it you must pause a while and consider what knowledge you have of that matter.

And, lest you be deceived by taking a vehement persuasion for certain knowledge, we must see what is meant by the name of knowledge and what by the name of opinion — a thing which we ought to do, however, not as philosophers, but as disciples of Christ. [Marginal note: One must hold to the divine word alone, to the Spirit, and to prayers.] Nothing is more certain than that we (as far as concerns the doctrine of religion) can have nothing certain except from the testimony of the divine voice. Many passages of the divine Scriptures testify to this, but these few will serve for many. David indeed, king of the Israelite people and likewise a prophet of great name, although he had resolved to build a temple for the Lord, could not divine God's will in that matter; nor did Nathan, himself also a great prophet, attain it any better by exhorting him to carry out his design — Nathan, through whom God forbade David to build any temple for Him: an example which is by no means to be despised. [Marginal note: 2. Reg. 7.] As for Joshua, who succeeded Moses, the Scriptures testify that he was endowed with the spirit of wisdom, and that God promised that He would be with him perpetually; yet he was commanded to see to it that he fulfil the law which he had received from Moses, and not turn aside from it either to the right hand or to the left; and the reason why is added: "That you may understand," says the Lord, "what must be done." [Marginal note: Deut. 31.] From this we may understand that something is certain for us only on this condition: if there is a word of God. But in how express words is this found elsewhere! "My thoughts," says the Lord, "are not like your thoughts, nor are my ways like your ways; but as much as heaven is higher than earth, so much do my thoughts and my ways rise above yours." [Marginal note: Isa.] Nor is it enough to rest in any way on the divine oracles; they must also be known with the divine Spirit as interpreter — since we see so many differing opinions about religion even among the most learned men, and those most thoroughly practised in reading the divine Scriptures. For Paul testifies that the Spirit of God alone attains to divine things, and that the sense of man neither perceives the things that are of the Spirit, nor can perceive them, because they seem foolish to him, but that the man endowed with the Spirit judges all things. [Marginal note: 1. Cor. 2.] In regard to this, James exhorts anyone who lacks wisdom to ask it from God. [Marginal note: Iac. 2.] Hence only he has knowledge of divine things who both rests on the word of God alone and has attained a true understanding of it, with no other interpreter than the Spirit of God Himself. And all this will be granted, even not unwillingly, by those to whom our whole discourse is addressed.

But a great difficulty lies beneath this. For most men hold it as a persuasion that what they hold can be confirmed by the testimony of the divine word, and also that they have had the Spirit of God as guide and teacher of their understanding — men who nevertheless go astray by the whole heaven, as they say. They seem to themselves to have asked the Spirit from God and to have placed all their safeguard in Him, although they do not notice that they have placed it in almost any other thing rather than in Him. But before you can determine for certain that you have attained a Spirit of that kind, and that you have learned anything under His guidance, you must turn away your eyes, your mind, and all your thought from everything that could have persuaded you of anything, or confirmed a persuasion already conceived — of which kind is the opinion about you, or about any other man, concerning his knowledge, judgment, spirit, and holiness; then the multitude of those who agree with you, or of those who praise you, admire you, and depend on you; then also the length of time for which the opinion that is attacked has prevailed; finally, whatever is not God and is not His word — from all of that you must turn away, as if nothing of the kind existed. At last, having cast away every other hope and confidence, you must fix your eyes and mind on God alone, depend on Him alone, and, thus disposed, ask the Spirit from Him.

If you do this — if you do it often, if seriously, if trustingly — then at last you will be able with the best right to determine for certain that you are held by no pernicious error, or, if you are held by one, that it will be disclosed to you. But these things are far easier to express in words than in deed, so liable is the human mind to arrogance and rashness, so liable to error; indeed, it is born for almost any confidence rather than for confidence in God. Finally, so crafty, so diligent, and so furnished with various arts for deceiving is Satan, that even when you have done everything in the highest degree, it is still a great business not to slip somewhere. Yet I would by no means wish this understood as said by me to this end — that I should think there is no place for certainty in divine things; I am as far from that opinion as anyone can be. But this I would by no means wish: that a man who has not yet arrived at any certainty, and who perhaps is still immersed in false opinions, should arrogate knowledge to himself; but that he should labour to attain it, and with the labour that is fitting. A man who has once arrived at clear and certain knowledge — exhort him as much as you will, and let him himself also do as much as he can to suspect that he is erring — will nevertheless never be able to come to a suspicion of error. Exhortations of this kind, therefore, can stir suspicion only in those who either are erring or have not yet attained firm and constant knowledge, but can still attain it; and when the right way of reaching it is shown to them, and they are warned what hindrances and snares they ought to beware of, I do not understand why the counsel given to them should be able to seem not exceedingly useful and salutary.

Wherefore, so that no one may incur any just rebuke by setting himself against the truth, whether publicly or privately, and whether by word or in writing, I would earnestly advise that whatever he thinks about religion, he examine one by one the grounds by which he was led to hold it as a persuasion: how diligently, and with what certain Spirit, he has searched the divine Scriptures; how circumspectly and prudently he has turned his eyes away from absolutely everything that is neither God nor God's word; then also with what confidence he has poured out prayers to God; and finally, how certain he holds any given matter to be. But although you may have done all these things with the greatest zeal you can — since modesty can never be displeasing to God, nor can He disregard the prayers of a man who does not cease to ask that he be granted day by day a greater certainty of faith — still, if something new, and for that reason seemingly false and impious, should strike your ears, I should think it best that, before you set yourself against it, you pause a little and think again and again in your own mind how ascertained you hold it to be that that word is false and impious; and that you contend afresh with God in most ardent prayers that, if you are of a right opinion, He confirm you in it more and more, but if anything should befall you after the manner of men, He open your eyes in such a way and impart to you so much light that you can recognise the error and cast it away.

For if we, though we are evil (as the Lord says), are so disposed that we give our children good things, and do not give stones if they have asked for bread, nor a serpent if they have asked for a fish, what are we to think of the heavenly Father? When we never cease to ask Him for certainty of the truth — kindled, namely, with a desire to satisfy His will and to keep the truth safe, lest somehow through error we become enemies of the truth — are we, I ask, to think that He will send us errors in place of certainty of the truth, darkness in place of light? How foreign would that be to His infinite goodness, and indeed to His most certain promises! There is also this consideration: that since (as has been said) we can have nothing certain unless we have been taught by God, and we cannot prescribe to God in our prayers a time within which He must teach us, it may still happen that, although you have turned your eyes away from all things that are not God, and have placed all your confidence in God, you have not yet been taught by God, but that He is about to teach you through the very man whom you take for a seducer.

For if indeed He has already taught you, He will also have added so much light and certainty that no room is left for hesitation; yet care must be taken only that Satan does not play a trick on you. One must remember how great that old deceiver is, how cleverly he knows how to put on a mask under the appearance of an angel of light (to use the Apostle's words), and it must be held as ascertained that no one can beware of him more than enough. But let that saying pass: "It is an old error, long since and often refuted; what is it, that the cause is still badly defended?" What if up to now Satan alone has played both the parts — of attacker and of defender? But come, let it be so, by all means; at least, in order that by your example you may provoke those who err to that ready openness which is necessary — so that they may be less obstinate toward those who wish to teach them — use this courtesy: that you first hear and understand, before you condemn. At least do this, so that you remove every ground of complaint that you are rejecting the matter without a hearing. What? Would the truth perchance become less conspicuous by being examined more often, and not rather more and more certain and illustrious day by day? Nothing is easier than to sow a quarrel; but once it has been begun, it is not so easy to find an end to it.

Therefore Solomon most aptly compares the man who stirs up a quarrel to one who opens a way for waters (understand it as if for waters gathered into some great and broad pool); for at the beginning the thing seems contemptible, but the water makes a way for itself ever broader and deeper, and gathers greater force, until, its banks being widely broken through, it destroys whatever it meets, hollows out ditches, covers the fields with gravel and flood-debris, tears up trees, levels buildings with the ground, and presents a horrendous sight. So too, from small and most contemptible beginnings of controversies, we at last see the world filled with dissensions, slanders, curses, and clamours. So that a man who does not think he must beware of stirring up a quarrel, unless plainly compelled, as of the greatest evil, can seem either to see nothing at all, or not to value the Church's welfare even a hair. Wherefore, even if in the end it is entirely ascertained that what is being spread is false, still, before you think any quarrel ought to be stirred up, you must consider how much the matter is worth. For concerning empty questions there is a precept of Paul's: not that we refute whatever falsehood is asserted, but that we avoid them. [Marginal note: Tit. 3.] Wherefore I think this one thing should be done: that minds be called away from curious questions of no value — not because it is said to be false, but much more so that they may not waste good hours badly; besides that it often happens that, while the matter in hand is a most trivial one, minds disturbed by the heat of contention rashly put forward, on this side or that, things which, since they are of greater weight, shake the purity of doctrine not a little. There is added the fact that men can come no less easily to insults, curses, and quarrels if the dispute is about an ass's shadow than if it were about some great matter — evils from which a Christian breast ought greatly to shrink in horror. But this is the task, this is the labour: to see which questions are useless, as distinct from those in which there is harmful error. For whatever comes into the mind, Satan at once sets to work to persuade us that the matter is of the utmost gravity, so that you think the hope of salvation cannot stand unless what you are to hold has been settled beyond doubt.

For my part, I think this: for a man who occupies himself with the reading of the divine writings not led by empty curiosity or ambition — namely, so that he may turn out a clever disputant about divine things — but by true piety toward God, and by no chilly desire for his own salvation, it will not be difficult to discern which questions are trifling and empty, and which are not unworthy to be explained. But nevertheless, because many in this kind, to the great harm of the Church, stray far from the goal, it will not be beside the point to seek out some method, and a sure one, by whose guidance anyone may distinguish between these and those without error.

[Marginal note: Abstain from curious questions; pursue the useful ones — what they are like, and what each of the two kinds is.] This, then, is certain: if there is some one thing to which all teaching about the Christian religion is directed as to its end and goal, then any matter which, when known, can bring no use toward that end — every question about such a matter will be an empty one. We must therefore see whether there is any such end, and if there is, what it is, and what things lead us to it. Now John testifies, in these words, not only that there is such an end, but also what it is: "Jesus also did many other signs, which are not included in this book, but these have been put into writing, so that you may believe that Jesus is the Christ, the Son of God, and that, believing, you may obtain life through him." By these words it is plain enough that the end and goal of Christian teaching is eternal life. Therefore whatever can be of any use toward obtaining eternal life must be called useful to know; but whatever can contribute nothing to that must be despised. "What?" you will say; "will no mention of the honour and glory of God be made in this place?" I answer: whatever pertains to God's honour, all those same things, when known, without doubt contribute to our salvation; and God's honour is seen most in our salvation itself. Therefore if, in order that we may obtain eternal life, there is anything that we ought to do, then, since we shall not do it unless we have learned that it ought to be done in that way, it will be necessary to know it.

And since God has given a law, for whoever does not fulfil it in every part He has appointed the punishment of everlasting destruction — a law which, since man cannot fulfil it, and since there is something else which, if he does it, he can nevertheless obtain salvation — he must understand both that he cannot satisfy the law, and what that thing is which, if he does it, will be for his salvation. And since that thing is this: if he is persuaded that the Son of God, made man, paid to the Father the penalties for his own offences, and that he himself has been made clean by His blood from every stain of sin — but that holiness and innocence of life are nevertheless required of us, not so that relying on them we may promise ourselves salvation, but so that our conduct may be such as befits sons of God and servants redeemed at so great a price — since, I say, these things are so, we must both know the promises about Christ, and not be ignorant of the things in which holiness and innocence of life consist. And since there are many things which can also lead us as if by the hand to place in Christ the hope of our salvation, and to fulfil God's law (as far as that can be done), and to be without hindrance or delay, it is exceedingly useful to know what helps, and what hinders. But knowledge of His nature persuades us to place our hope in God, and kindles His love in us, led by which we strive to obey His commands: not as the philosophers wish to know it, so as to argue subtly about it, but so that we understand how wise He is above all thought, good, gentle, merciful, and how exactly He makes good His promises. His works bear witness to such a nature: the creation of the world and of all things in it, but especially the examples of how great a care He had for those who worshipped Him with pious and holy fear, bringing them help in their times of need and guarding them from the snares of the world and of Satan; and then how many, and how guilty, men He has raised up and saved after they repented; and otherwise, how sharp an enemy of sin He has always shown Himself; and other such things. If, then, to the knowledge of His nature there is added the remembrance of His promises, most often repeated, it greatly confirms and strengthens faith, and drives us with great force to undertake seriously the amendment of life. To the same end belong also explanations of oracles, or prophecies, especially those which are referred to the present times; since they are a most illustrious testimony to divine providence, they are wonderfully effective for the strengthening of minds. Whatever things, therefore, touch on such matters are exceedingly useful to know, and worthy above all to be worn on everyone's lips at home and abroad, in private and in public.

And since we can accomplish none of the things said a little before unless aided by God's help and Spirit, and God wills this help and Spirit to be sought from Himself, God's will of this kind must be known, and the promises held fast — that if we ask, those things will be given to us — and in general the manner of calling upon God, and of giving thanks for benefits received, must not be unknown. On the other hand, it is a great hindrance to us in all these things if we judge about God's nature and His will, and about every outcome, otherwise than from that teaching which has been handed down in the divine writings. A hindrance is the corruption of our nature, which with great force leads away and drags our mind, heart, and pursuits from God's commands and ordinances to the opposite. Therefore, since we must struggle in perpetual war against our inborn vice, so that we may subject our nature to the divine will, it is also greatly necessary to know both this vice of our origin, and whatever we are taught in the sacred volumes for softening and taming it. And since it is not enough if each man cares for himself alone, but each one must have regard, according to the measure of the Spirit with which he has been endowed and the kind of life to which he has been called, for the whole Church — and not only for those who are still alive, but also for posterity — whatever can make the way of obtaining any doctrine useful for life less easy and less unimpeded, or can give occasion either to abandon it or to value it less than is fitting, it is of the greatest importance that what that thing is should not be unknown.

Since the Scriptures also warn us that we have a most great and most crafty adversary, who has also received his name from this, who without any ceasing contrives snares for us, by which to drive us headlong and turn us away from God, who can doubt that it will be of the greatest use to us to have his arts thoroughly known, and to understand by what prudence they are to be avoided? Finally, a doctrine cannot be useful unless it has some use; if it is placed in speculation alone, never emerging into any action, it must be held empty and trifling. Indeed, not even every doctrine which can in any way pertain to obtaining salvation is to be counted among the useful ones, worth treating very carefully, or, if dissension arises, labouring greatly to settle what is to be held; but this must be added: that there are not, besides, other things that can lead us suitably enough to the same end. This can be known from the fact that, although we had to be persuaded that Jesus is the Christ, the one who had been foretold through the Prophets, and although the signs performed by him were directed to this — that it might be known from them who he was — nevertheless not all of them were committed to writing, but only so many, and such that from them it would be easy to know who he was.

But if someone were to maintain that the Lord performed some sign of which no mention is made in those books which we have about his life and deeds, it would not be worth the trouble to search out the certain truth of the matter, or to raise a dispute about it, because, if it were established that the thing was done in that way, there would be no other fruit of it than that it would testify that Jesus was from God, and was the one whom he himself professed to be — which very many other miracles, placed beyond all controversy, testify so fully that a greater certainty cannot even be desired, whereas, even if there were no credence in that miracle, the authority of the other signs which the most holy writers have handed down in writing would be no less. From which it would follow that, whether that miracle were believed or not, nothing would either be added to us or taken away. Since, moreover, even if the teaching of religion seems to contain something absurd, or to set down contradictory things, that is a hindrance to its being embraced by those who are still strangers to it; whatever pertains to removing those absurdities and reconciling passages that seem to conflict both pertains to this, and cannot be without fruit — unless perhaps such reconciliations have to be bought at the price of great disturbances of controversies and dissensions. By such considerations, therefore, useful questions can be distinguished from empty ones with no trouble — at that time, when judgments are not yet disturbed by partialities and by the heat of contentions, and when men have not yet begun to treat them as grave matters; but when men are held by a kind of spell, as it were, inborn pride, hatreds, indignations, habituation to error, and Satan do not allow judgment to fulfil its office adequately. For which of us does not wonder greatly today that the ancients litigated with such contention of minds, when was to be celebrated the day of Passover (as they call it)? And about some other trivial matters; and yet at that time it seemed that almost the highest matters were at stake. We, who use free judgment in those matters, see clearly how unworthy a thing it was for God's Church to be split as if into sects, for such slight causes.

My advice, therefore, is this: as soon as you perceive that something false is being taught, before you prepare yourself to contradict it, weigh carefully whether it is perhaps of no importance, or of little; and if it is of that kind, either raise no dispute about it, or, as the opportunity allowed by place, time, and person — both yours, and his with whom you have to do — permits, only turn the man away from speculations of that kind toward weightier matters, and do this with as much tact as possible, so that no disturbance of minds follows from it. But the true way of leading men away from empty questions is this: if those whose office it is either to instruct the people, or to treat divine things in the schools, and who betake themselves to writing, leaving aside curious questions and casting away empty display of talent, themselves insist only on those things, and press those things, which pertain to strengthening faith, to rooting out vices, to instilling the fear of God, to nourishing piety, to consoling the afflicted, to raising them into a better hope, and to keeping those who wish to be counted by the name of brothers in brotherly love, with sweet agreement of minds among themselves, and other such things. Nor are these things of such a sort that they should quickly become cheap, as if they ought to be less everyday. For first, although the chief heads themselves are indeed very few and extend very widely, then by variety of treatment all weariness can easily be removed, especially if Gospel preachers carefully observe what things the people most need, and at what time: as, if the people are elated by prosperity and loosen the reins to self-indulgence, they themselves should terrify minds with prophetic threats and bring them back into order; if affliction presses, they should come to the rescue with consolations; if the people are sluggish with idleness, they should exhort them to honourable occupations; if the people seem too intent on acquiring property, they should be warned of the brevity of this life and led away from that excessive care; and so let them bring forward the things that will seem most suitable for each season. And yet why do we call those things too everyday, which can scarcely ever be imprinted on our minds even in the smallest part? So that time must always be wanting for a subject, whereas a subject for the time (and indeed within the limits stated before) can never be wanting. But this seems able often to give occasion for stirring up empty questions, if, in expounding the sacred writings to the people, someone lays down for himself a law of dwelling long, without distinction, on every word. This is what most people now seem to do. For since the many useful things they might say are not always at hand, curiosity must necessarily be given full sail. There are perhaps some who, since they both abound in a wealth of matter and, according to their talent's dexterity, can seize from anywhere an occasion to say anything, will have treated every passage most fully without blame; but (as the proverb says) it is not given to everyone to reach Corinth. Those whose store is not so ample will do much more prudently if they attempt what they can accomplish. Where many things are available that are worth hearing, let them say many; if the passage suggests few, let them not be ashamed to put a small shoe on a small foot, and to make greater progress. What does it matter if, when you have had twenty sermons on one chapter of the Gospel history, you expound the next whole chapter in no more than one? The Holy Spirit did not will to teach all things in all places, but one thing in one place and another in another; therefore not all your learning is to be poured out in one place, even if you can do it not ineptly. But how absurd it is that you should prefer to descend to trifling inventions, or to drum in things with which everyone's ears are already full, rather than not seem to bring enough to the explanation of each word! Some sin in this by a sort of inept desire to show off their abundance; others by example, attending more to what others do than to what needs to be done.

The occasion for curious inquiry is also much greater for those who expound the sacred books in the schools. For since many commentaries on them are already extant, which even students themselves can read through, and teachers are unwilling for their work to seem empty or of little necessity — as if they were only reciting other men's expositions, reflections, arguments, and the very words — but want always to have something that students have not found in the commentaries; besides the fact that necessity compels them to change their seats, carrying things disputed in one place over into another, to commend their own acumen they either devise objections to solve, or invent new words and new forms of speaking, or set forth new questions. It would certainly be better to have no teachers at all than any such: for what else will they have done than turn theological study back into sophistical vanity? "What, then, are they to do?" you will say. What? Indeed I would prefer anything whatever, rather than that they should so abuse their leisure. But what else would I wish, than that they should not corrupt, not pervert, things said once, well, suitably, and in a fitting place, nor darken them with empty questions? That they should both enjoy those things themselves, and allow others to enjoy them? That they should prefer to report things well said by others, rather than to show off their own acumen? If something had been passed over, if anywhere they themselves saw what earlier men had not seen, that in that they should commend their own diligence? But what is worse, for many it is not enough to teach in this way with the living voice; they also want their inventions to be extant in published great volumes, so that the crop of clever arguing may be more abundant day by day.

For how many commentaries on Paul's Epistle to the Romans are already extant? A new one comes out into the light daily; for whoever expounds it in a school with some commendation wants to publish a commentary. Although if you collect the things which he himself explains more suitably than others, or which he adds of his own, they are few. The rest will be the same things which earlier men either wrote on the same passage or treated elsewhere. And since later men deliberately wish to abstain from the words of earlier men, and those earlier men have generally pre-occupied the most suitable forms of speaking, which breathe more of the spirit and affect one more, these later men will necessarily have to use less suitable ones, and such as only smell of empty display of talent, and leave no sting in the mind. Those who are deceived in judgment, and think they are performing no useless service, when you have warned them there is ground even to forgive them; but those whom display and ambition drive to writing, those I would not hesitate to number among the chief plagues of the Church, since they hire out their work to none other than Satan, and that most wickedly. But let us now proceed to the rest.
