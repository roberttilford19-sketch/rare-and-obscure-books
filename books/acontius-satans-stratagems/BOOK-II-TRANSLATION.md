*The Stratagems of Satan. Book Two.* Of useless questions — by what method they could be distinguished from the useful ones, and that they ought to be avoided — has been spoken in the preceding Book; now, if the matter which is called into controversy, and which is contradicted, shall have proved to be of no light weight, what it befits a godly man to do: it follows that this must be spoken of. And here indeed, [Marginal note: Satan's working in controversies of great moment.] if you have opened Satan's ears, there is no doubt that he will take pains to reap from it a great fruit for his own designs. So too he will take pains, as far as he can make and strive to do it, that that error be not indeed wrested away, but be spread far and wide, and that it be besides, as it were, a seed for very many: that from it there may arise the greatest lawsuits, discords, contentions, sects, devastations, and burnings: and that thus there be room for no moderate, circumspect, and grave counsel, but that all things be done rashly, inconsiderately, and in no order.

What, then, will this Christian and prudent mind do here? Will he think, forsooth, that he must obey him? Will he choose him for himself, to follow, as the leader and author of his own counsels? And will he not rather see, with the utmost striving of his mind, that all things be done as prudently as possible? So much so that not even one word (if it can be done) should go forth unless it has been polished by the utmost reason? Will he not weigh again and again what needs to be done? Here, here there is need of prudence; resist the beginnings, while the flame is small: for if you allow it any time, and even bring it wood besides, who at last will be able to extinguish the burning? These things, I say, must be foreseen in time. If indeed you hear something ungodly, what do you think Satan's speech in your heart will be? He will perhaps try whether he can also set the error in favour with you: but if he finds these doors shut, and takes another way, "Ah, the crime!" (he will say) "Ah, the man worthy to be swallowed alive by the earth, from whom so unspeakable a word could fall: worthy for fire descending from heaven to seize him and consume him: of all men who have ever been born the most worthless and most impure!"

Could anyone mark out, or even think, such things? If room is given to meditations of that kind, what will you have done or said that will not be the origin and cause of six hundred evils? Come, persuade yourself of this: that the author of such thoughts is not the Spirit of God, but Satan. Thoughts of this kind are not fit to remove the evil: but to increase it more and more, and to render it past remedy; to cure the evil, something else must be done. And first indeed, how do you know whether perhaps this man (whatever he may seem) is one of the number of God's elect, whom God himself has allowed indeed to fall, by a certain hidden, but most wise and most loving, counsel of his own, so that he may raise him up again with much greater glory? If you understood that he was that man, could you, then, think anything more inhuman against him who was destined to possess the glory of the elect and the inheritance of the kingdom of heaven, against your brother, against a member of Christ, against not only a servant of God, but also a son? "But indeed" (you will say) "on this reasoning the Magistrate too will have to spare thieves, robbers, cut-throats, and other criminal men, since they too may all be of the number of the elect, destined one day to return to soundness."

I answer. A godly and wise Magistrate, when he afflicts those men with punishment, indulges no hatreds against them, but performs the office to which he has been called by God: to whose will, once known, he understands that obedience must nevertheless be rendered, whatever his own reason may dictate to him: just as Abraham too, at God's command, was ready to sacrifice Isaac, his own son, most dear to him and also most innocent. Meanwhile, toward those who are to be punished with death, he omits no office of humanity. "But you will add: Will the ears, then, of a man who loves God be able to hear execrable utterances against God himself, and not be moved, as if they did not touch him at all?" Indeed I wish the godly man to be moved, and to be vehemently moved, but I would wish that emotion to be a zeal not headlong and full of rashness, but moderated by the best reason, whose author would be not Satan, but the Holy Spirit; if you had a brother, whom you followed with a love by no means common, or, if you prefer, a son: you could not even see his dangerous fall without being greatly moved (especially if he had received a grave wound from it), but with what emotion, pray, at last? One by which you goaded him with harsh words: by which you overwhelmed him with curses and reproaches? On the contrary, you would run to him; you would raise him up with the greatest dexterity; you would bid him be of good heart. And you would omit nothing that could lift his pain, or at least soothe it; you would spare no labour: you would sit up watchful for whole nights. And if pain from the wound had made him somewhat fretful, nothing on that account would fall away from your supreme love toward him. I would wish, therefore, the emotion of your mind to be very like that: that you indeed take pain from your brother's fall; that you grieve that God has been offended; that you grieve for the lot of those who have been taught depraved things by him, because their salvation is in danger on account of those things. But let the fruit of that grief be that you strive, as far as you can, either to remove the evil or to lessen it, and, as in a matter in which one cannot err without the greatest danger, that you look again and again lest you do or say anything by whose cause the evil could receive an increase — indeed, anything by whose cause you have not first seen a certain hope of fruit; I would wish also that the same grief render you so anxious that you observe diligently what deed of yours, or what word, has been the cause of what good, or of what evil; and that you so prepare yourself that, if ever you noticed that what you had done for a good end had turned out badly, you would be ready to correct the error even at the cost of your own reputation.

Moreover, three things seem to be looked to: what regard is to be had to him who sows errors; what, to those who have assented to the errors; and lastly, what, to those who are in danger lest the contagion of the errors harm them. As far as concerns the author of the error, in the first place you must look again and again at what end and goal of all your counsels you set before yourself. [Marginal note: The author of an error is to be treated gently and in a friendly way.] Most men, weighing how pernicious a thing error in religion is, and how greatly, therefore, he who scatters errors harms God's Church, think that nothing sufficiently atrocious can be devised against him. And so at the very first word they burst out into abuses and reproaches, and fill everything with clamours, in which you would wonder how greatly they please themselves, and how excellently they think they are defending Christ's cause. Nor indeed does it much matter with them whether someone teaches a false thing through ignorance, or in order to serve his own desire. Indeed, just as if they held it for certain that nothing of this kind ever happens except maliciously, they treat no one except as a malicious seducer. Hence those set forms of words: "The most impure swine was not ashamed to blurt out this and that"; "the most cunning shape-changer leaves himself nothing undone by which he may destroy the simple with his poison"; and many others of that kind, with a stock of which certain men wonderfully abound.

But if someone thinks that he is teaching true and godly things, and is acting well and holily, and therefore tries by whatever arguments he can to persuade men of his errors, and thinks besides that very much praise is owed to him, then, if you rush upon him and contend that he is acting maliciously, shamelessly, and wickedly, he will think that the greatest injury is being done to him. And so he will be moved and kindled with anger, and it will be altogether settled for him to defend the error, and not only to repel the injuries but also to repay them with increase. But then contrary sympathies of men will come in, and everything will be thrown into confusion with contentions, quarrels, and clamours, so that nothing good will follow, but very much evil; wherefore, although it ought to be your goal that he who sows errors should not escape a just and deserved censure, nevertheless the man who, himself deceived, deceives others would have to be treated far otherwise than the man who did it maliciously; nor indeed should punishment for malice be exacted unless the malice had first been established; and in a doubtful case, moreover, it would belong to a well-constituted mind, in its judgment, to incline to the gentler side, and to prefer to interpret that there is error underneath rather than malice. Indeed, since those who try to bring in new doctrines generally incur great hatreds from men and bring great calamities upon themselves, the man who could take any pleasure in stirring up men's hatreds and persecutions against himself must be of a strange character: so that no one ought to believe that anyone does that, unless compelled by very great conjectures. And if it is error, not malice, I for my part think this: if there is anything in which there ought to be any place for clemency, it ought to be in this kind.

There is someone endowed with the fear of God, who strives to win approval, if it can be done, from the whole race of men, for the things he thinks true and saving: in his case, if you look at men's mind, would anything harsher that you devised against him not seem to have been done inhumanly and cruelly? He has not been endowed by God with a better talent, or with better judgment, nor indeed with a greater spirit; if therefore you are angry with him, you would seem to be angry not so much with him as with God himself, who has not furnished him with better judgment. But if you look at the fruit, nothing is less profitable (as has been said) than angers, indignations, and attacks full of accusations and abuses. But if there is bad deceit in him, I for my part will gladly confess that no censure, and no severity of punishment, can be devised so great that it would not still be, by many parts, less than the enormity of the crime, that a man whom no reverence for God, and no concern for the salvation of peoples, moves, but who, provided he satisfies his own desire, holds everything upside down.

But although a man may be worthy of great censure, it is nevertheless not expedient to censure him at once according to his deserts. "All things are lawful for me" (says the Apostle) "but not all things are expedient." Wherefore it must be looked to again and again what is most expedient, what regard ought to be had to persons, what the place requires, what the time; which things a mind disturbed by anger, or by any other emotion, cannot see. For anger pours darkness over the eyes, dulls the judgment, disturbs and corrupts it; in short, it is the worst counsellor in all things and at every time. Let a man be indeed worthy to be overwhelmed with stones, to be torn wholly limb from limb: that may be done by the judgment of the Magistrate without anger, without insult, without abuse. For what need is there of these affections, the true enemies of all right judgment? And it will be done with much greater both gravity and fruit, and it will seem to have proceeded not from a man's desire, but rather from reason itself. Wherefore also the example of that severity will be impressed on men's minds with much greater force. But care must be taken for these things: that he who was seducing the people should seduce them no more, and should allow there to be room for the truth; that those who had been seduced should return to soundness; that those who are still sound should be fortified beforehand against the contagion of error.

Whether it is also to be added that, by a just censure as by a kind of bridle, the licence of ill-disposed minds be restrained, so that they do not blurt out whatever comes into their mouths, we shall see afterwards. It can also happen that God wishes some men to be rebuked most sharply, so that on the last day they may both have less excuse and undergo a greater severity of judgment; but just as it can be certain to no one among all who may be of the number of such men, so there is no one who ought to have regard of that kind; if God wishes any to be so rebuked, he will also stir up those who will do it; only care must be taken that we do not allow ourselves to be seduced by Satan, and his promptings to be thrust upon us in place of the breathings of the Holy Spirit. But, so that you may understand by what method you can draw a malicious seducer away from seducing the people, this must be considered: that the man who has brought himself to admit so great a crime must be singularly ungodly, and devoid of all fear of God; if therefore, whether he is present or absent, you say the things of which he is worthy, if you paint him in his own colours, and he then desists at once, what will that be? Will he not seem openly to confess that he is guilty of all the crimes which you have ascribed to him?

Could you, then, promise yourself so great a moderation of mind, so great a forbearance, from a man in whom there is no fear of God? Is it not much more likely that he will try to repel every crime from himself, and will heap many more upon you? Will he not rather weary his talent day and night in devising lies and slanders by which to make your name infamous? And what is there so alien to the truth that a cunning speech does not make it seem probable? And when angers and hatreds gradually grow on this side and on that, when vehement accusations are heard daily on both sides, and the people is split into contrary sympathies, what could you gain from it which a man of sound judgment would not greatly regret? If therefore there is any hope (and it is a slender one) of calling such a man away from his undertaking, it does not lie in this, that you expose and accuse his malice, but rather in this: if, with all mention of deceit and malice omitted, you first so maintain the truth, and render it so thoroughly seen, that it can almost be touched; then, that you do it with the utmost dexterity and gentleness; for since it is very hard to obtain from a wicked and abominable man that he be willing to seem defeated, and to have slipped by error, you will then hope ineptly that he will be willing to seem also, through the utmost crime, knowingly and deliberately, to have practised a deception on the people.

But as for leading back from error those who have been seduced, I for my part do not see what good reproaches cast at the author of the error do. For it generally happens that whoever assents to someone's doctrines at the same time embraces him with a certain love, reveres him, and judges him worthy of praise and honour, so that, if you set about dishonouring him with abuses, he will say that you are doing him a signal injury, and will therefore be of an estranged mind toward you. And who does not know that goodwill or hatred, and the other emotions of the mind, can do very much, by inclining the judgment to this side or to that? Certainly that will be nothing else than to stop up both his ears against you, so that he cannot even hear you. Nor indeed do I understand to whom it will be of notably greater use for fortifying beforehand those who are still uncorrupted. For a speech stuffed with abuses and accusations can hardly fail to proceed from anger, or from some vehement emotion of the mind; and where there have been emotions of that kind, it is a wonder if all things are said circumspectly enough, and if many things are not rather done amiss, either by reporting the seducer's words wrongly, or by answering his arguments not aptly enough; from which things (as we have shown) great disadvantages follow. Nor indeed are there lacking many whom all slander, whether it has a just pretext or an unjust one, offends, and who think ill of a man in whom they notice any zeal for slandering, and estrange their mind from him.

And what else is that than for men to be rendered more inclined to assent to those whom you have striven with all zeal to deform with reproaches? Especially if they have learned to feign, cunningly and craftily, whatever modesty and patience they show. I for my part would not deny that with some men accusations also carry so much weight that in the end they cannot hear even the name of those whom the accusations touch without horror, far less will they now suffer themselves to be seduced by them; but that generally happens only at last when the accusers stand in great repute for integrity, holiness, and innocence, so that they seem to have been able to be brought by no cause and no reason to do injury to anyone — an opinion of the people about you, settled and constant, which you will much more easily have falsely claimed for yourself than have obtained. For often we ourselves do not sufficiently understand by what spirit we are led; and however innocent you may be, who could bring it about that many things, devised most holily, should not commonly be taken by the crowd in a bad part? And those same men who withdrew all belief from the accused because of the accusations would, as is fitting, have done the same when, in their hearing, the accused men's doctrines had been reported without any slander, and overthrown, humanely and candidly, by the firmest and most certain arguments and testimonies.

It has thus far, then, been made plain (as it seems) that from curses very many, and often very great, disadvantages are created, but that their usefulness is either none, or very small — which usefulness, even so, can be obtained from elsewhere much more surely. It remains only that we speak of that reasoning, that by this kind of punishment, as it were, the authors of new doctrines are restrained; and here this must first be understood, that in this place we are not treating of the office of the magistrate: here we follow the duty of a private man, and also of him to whom the instruction of the people in godliness has been entrusted. Of the magistrate we shall speak afterwards.

Since, therefore, the power of punishing anyone belongs to no one except the civil magistrate, others must take care not to put their hand into another man's harvest. A distinguished passage of Cicero, from the fourth book *On the Republic*, is quoted in these words: “Our Twelve Tables, on the contrary, although they had sanctioned very few things with capital punishment, thought that among them this also ought to be sanctioned: if anyone had sung against another, or had composed a song, which should bring infamy or disgrace upon another. Rightly; for we ought to have our life placed before the judgments and the lawful decisions of the magistrates, not before the talents of poets; nor ought we to hear disgrace except on this condition, that it be allowed to answer, and to defend oneself in a trial.”

What could even be thought more just than that sanction? That it forbids anyone to hear disgrace except on this condition, that it be allowed to answer, and to defend oneself in a trial? Or is reputation a thing to be so despised that it ought to be exposed to anyone's whim? [Marginal note: In rebukes let all bitterness be absent.] If he who holds power so orders himself that he marks with infamy whomsoever he pleases (even if he does it otherwise for a just cause), his case unheard, who will say that he acts as a king, and not rather as a tyrant? Therefore, will that which not even the highest magistrate can do except by open tyranny be permitted either to any private man, or to him whose task it is to instruct the people in all honesty and moderation not so much by words as also by his own example? It is indeed the part of an ecclesiastical pastor to reprove, to rebuke, to chide; but he ought to do it not as an enemy, but as a father, insofar, namely, as it is either necessary or can be useful for procuring amendment. But let him remember that the power of punishing anyone is not part of his own office, but the magistrate's; and let us see with what outcome, at last, he will use that power, about which it has been said above, if he contends that it pertains to himself. For my part, I would not deny that many of them, struck by the examples of those whom they see daily brought by this method into the utmost hatred and disgrace alike, become less bold in bringing forward the things which inquisitive pursuits often suggest; but unless the life of those who use this kind of punishment is free not only from every fault, but also from suspicion, and unless their holiness and innocence are conspicuous in every matter, so that they reflect something divine rather than human, I would think they will by no means profit very much by this method, at least.

For it will happen just as if someone should hold back waters with dikes: for those waters indeed seem for a little while to obey, and to halt their course, but nevertheless they seek an exit on every side, or at least, raised higher, they will overflow, and at last, the dikes having been eaten away and broken, they will burst out with great force, and will make a devastation many times greater than if their liberty had not been hindered at the beginning; for he whose liberty is restrained by a bridle of this kind obeys indeed for a time, but he bears it ill, and gradually gathers poison, which, when he is full of it, he must pour out, and the more destructively the longer he has held it in, since his hatred toward those whom he thinks to hang over him like tyrants makes him easily turn away from, and abominate, whatever they think; and so he gives his attention to every snare he can: first, to turn aside now these men, now those, and to make them of his own party; and so the evil gradually grows, and gathers strength, so that at last he does not fear to show himself openly as a sect, and to disturb the commonwealth — of which thing, among many others, the very recent example of the Münster sedition can be a most ample proof.

It is wonderful, moreover, with what arts Satan equips this kind of men, and with what successes he advances their undertakings; and if those who think that much protection for preserving the purity of doctrine is placed in reproaches weighed this more carefully, they would perhaps delight in them much less. Finally, not to make it long: as I would not deny that bitterness in rebukes ought sometimes to have a place, so too I maintain that all the things which are required if any fruit is to be obtained from it meet together very rarely. And, to set aside the question of fruit, that example which Jude records in his canonical epistle is very great and wonderful: that Michael the Archangel, when there was a contention about the body of Moses, did not dare to hurl a curse even against Satan himself, but only wished that God would rebuke him. O we rash men, how much more greatly do we very often dare, not against Satan (which would nevertheless be a very great arrogance, that we should wish to take more upon ourselves than the Archangel thought should be taken by him), but against men perhaps no less dear to God than we ourselves are!

If we weigh this one example, and then consider with what licence we tear one another with mutual curses, surely he must be remarkably blind who does not see that this whole habit of cursing has proceeded from no other spirit than Satan's. But since it has been said that whatever is done on account of the report of any error that is being spread must all be referred to some end, and that it must be seen, among other things, that effort be given that he who was led by error be called away from it, and that neither curses nor any assaults can at all help toward that, but rather hinder it most of all, it follows that we should inquire what, therefore, is the best thing to be done.

Now there are two things that are required for that: on the one hand, demonstrations of such a kind as have force to lay open the error in a man of that mind, imbued with those persuasions with which he who errs is imbued [Marginal note: How a seducer is to be healed.]; on the other hand, that he weigh those demonstrations with a whole and sound judgment. But if the demonstrations are too little fitted to the man's understanding, or he does not weigh them, or does so with a judgment tainted and corrupted, we shall not attain the end of our purpose.

Moreover, judgment is then sound and uncorrupted when God has healed it by his own Spirit, and has made it capable of the truth, and, besides, when it is troubled by no suspicions and by no movements of the mind. Therefore nothing is so much to be avoided here as that anything slip out, or that you do anything, which could wound his mind and alienate him from you — of which kind is every word that has any signification of a will estranged from him on your part, that stings him or accuses him, whether of fraudulent intent, or of ignorance, or of impudence; in short, that brands him with any mark whatever. Rather, he must be called by all humanity and kindness toward gentleness, moderation, and goodwill; and no one can be a better teacher of that humanity than love alone — but love that is sincere, and by no means arranged for show — and unless you understand that it is in you, you ought to undertake nothing before you have obtained it. But if you see nothing in the man that would win him to you, consider how many and how great things there are in God, and in his only-begotten Son, whose work this man is. Unless God had willed him to be among the living, he either would never have been born, or would have died at once, or certainly would not have lived so long; but now you see how God nourishes him, and wills the sun to rise for him just as for you, and in a thousand ways makes him a partaker of his infinite beneficence and goodness.

Should you, therefore, his fellow servant, think that you must bear feelings contrary to him? For who knows (as has already been said) whether he is perhaps one of the Lord's little sheep, which, although at present it wanders far from the flock, unsound, is nevertheless at last to be both found and healed by the best Shepherd, and led back to the flock? And if it is so, do you not understand that it is the Lord himself who meets you under his person, weak and in the worst condition, to put your love to the test, which he will afterwards recall on the last day? And since it is not yours to judge whether he is, or is not, it ought to be enough for you that it can be that he is, so that whatever kindness you have shown him, the Lord will credit to you as bestowed upon himself. Do not look, therefore, at his person, but at Christ himself, whom he represents; and remember with how wonderful a love he was toward you — he who did not refuse, himself just and innocent, to undergo death for you, unjust and guilty of the greatest crimes, and death, moreover, so savage and so bloody. Oh, if there were any suspicion about some man that he would one day become a king, and the best of kings, so that his present fortune seemed humble and obscure, how would we nevertheless not despise him? Or with how great veneration, rather, would we attend him?

But what, indeed, is royal honour and loftiness, if it is compared with that dignity and splendour which await God's elect? Shall we, then, judge a man, whom we do not know whether he was perhaps born for that glory, unworthy of our humanity and goodwill? It will also be of no small help toward winning such a man to you, if, descending into yourself, you call back all your actions, your life, and your thoughts to a diligent and rigorous examination, and measure them by the rule of the divine precepts. And if you find that you too have offended before God with the greatest and very many foul faults, and nevertheless cherish a good hope that he will not deal severely with you, nor punish you according to your deserts, but will rather bear himself toward you as a most merciful father and God, what is there why you should not hope that the same mercy can befall him? And just as, on account of that hope, you by no means seem to yourself to be one who ought to be despised, what cause is there why you should not judge the same also of another in his case? Moreover, with meditations of this kind frequent and ardent prayers to God must be joined, that he may soften your heart, and sow in it by his own hand true and living love; and when you have obtained it, with it as teacher you will learn ways of dealing with your brother so fit and suitable that it will be a wonder if in a short time you have not called him back to soundness.

And since no one can attain divine things unless helped by the divine Spirit, before you undertake any disputation you will strive with prayers from God — some other godly men also having been joined with you for that purpose — that he may be willing to be present to the erring man with his Spirit, and to open both his heart and his eyes together for recognising the truth; indeed, you will also exhort him to ask the same thing.

Demonstrations, indeed, have that force of laying open the truth only then, when God is their author, just as it is written, “Such a tongue and wisdom shall I give you, that no one shall be able to resist you.” And likewise: “As the rain and the snow come down from heaven, and do not return there, but rather water the earth, and make it soft, and make it sprout, and bear fruit, and supply bread for food, so shall my word be that is uttered from my mouth; it shall not return to me empty, but shall accomplish what I have willed, and shall prosper for those to whom I shall have sent it.” [Marginal note: With arguments fit for the matter and for the man. Luke 21. Isaiah 55.] Since this is so, prayers to the Lord are necessary here likewise, that he may not only unlock the erring man's heart, but may also dictate to you those words which are most fit for the demonstration of the truth.

Unless it is absurd, then, to leave some place also to those arts which, although they are called human, nevertheless — if you have used them in no other way than as one who both sows and waters, but places all his hope of increase in God alone — no one can deny are gifts of God, I shall add also this: that those demonstrations have the force of persuading which, having taken their beginning from the best-known things — as from the clearest passages of the divine letters, which cannot be understood in several ways — in a fixed order and with a continuous thread, no middle step having been omitted or transposed, arrive at the matter proposed; or at least begin from that opinion which is to be overthrown, and from some other thing placed outside all controversy, and gather consequences step by step, until they have led on to some great absurdity which, since it can in no way be defended, the man with whom the business lies cannot fail to confess that something false had been laid down — especially if this is done in words and forms of speaking that are not at all obscure, or ambiguous, or perplexed, but clear, plain, and in common use. Yet (to say what the matter is) very few can accomplish this, because there are wonderfully few who do not wish to say and to write much more than to meditate.

Moreover, it is not enough if an argument you have brought forward seems to you to be fitted to the matter; it must be fitted both to the talent and to the judgment of the very man who is to be persuaded — for example, in this way. When you value the authority of Origen so highly that you think faith must altogether be given to him, if indeed you bring forward some passage of his which openly supports your opinion in that place, that testimony will seem to you to suit the matter most beautifully; but if the man with whom the business lies thinks that little faith, or none, is to be given to Origen, you will have accomplished nothing by that testimony, nor indeed will you have advanced any more if, with a Turk, or with any other enemy of our religion, you deal by the testimonies of the divine letters. It will be the same if, when the contest must be with arguments, you take up arguments which, although they are most certain to you, the opponent does not yet hold as persuaded. For things must be taken up which are certain not to you only, but also to him. It will also be the same if you pass over in silence some part of the argumentation known indeed to you, but not likewise to the opponent. As if you should say that sins are not to be recounted to a priest, because God has by no means commanded it, and add nothing further; for a man who does not yet know — and besides, as long as it is not ready to hand that we ought to keep only those things which God himself has commanded — will be moved in no way by that reasoning.

There is a similar, but much more obscure, passage of Cicero in the *Laelius*, whose words are these: “Scipio used to say that no saying could be found more hostile to friendship than his who had said that one ought to love in such a way as one day to hate. Nor indeed could he be brought to believe that this had been said (as was thought) by Bias, who was held to be one of the seven wise men, but that it was the opinion of some impure man, or an ambitious man, or a man who referred everything to his own power. For in what way will anyone be able to be the friend of a man of whom he will think that he can be his enemy? Indeed, it will even be necessary for him to desire and to wish that his friend sin as often as possible, so that he may give him, as it were, more handles for reproving him. Again, at his friends' right deeds and advantages it will be necessary for him to be troubled, to grieve, and to envy.” Thus far Laelius. For someone might wonder how these absurdities follow also from Bias's saying; perhaps he would not wonder if the argumentation were whole. And so that you may understand by what reasoning a man can be moved, and by what less, you must have it most fully known what he says, what he thinks, and then also (besides the matter that is in controversy) with what other opinions he is imbued, which he holds most firmly persuaded, and what his judgment is. Moreover, there is nothing so customary in controversies as that one man should quibble at another's words, and draw out something absurd.

From this it generally comes about that not what is said is attacked, but what by a false interpretation you have fashioned for yourself — which, what else is it than (as we have said) to stir the opponent's stomach, and to confirm his confidence and obstinacy, and to open a window for yourself for babbling out six hundred follies which suit the matter not at all? In this, who would imitate unskilled physicians, who, the disease having been examined too little diligently, distinguish remedies that are not at all suitable, and kill the sick whom they ought to cure? And yet many are exceedingly pleased with themselves if, the words having been wrongly taken, they can accuse the opponent of some great absurdity. But this habit, indeed, is to be left to empty sophists; here, namely, where such grave matters are to be handled, let it not be your purpose to seem to have overthrown the error by whatever method, but strive for this: that you overthrow it in fact, and leave the opponent nothing that he can even open his mouth to utter — which will certainly never happen unless you diligently see to it that you rightly grasp the other man's words. But if something seems to be said very absurdly, care must be taken all the more not to have grasped it sufficiently, and rather to seek an explanation than to attack an absurdity when it is not yet sufficiently certain whether that was the opponent's opinion.

And if indeed the disputation is held face to face, the fault in it is generally greatest in this: that each man holds himself well persuaded that he divines, from the very first word, what the other wants for himself, so that he wants to answer absolutely before the other has spoken; from which it comes about that shouts are raised, angers are kindled, and nothing at all is accomplished. This rashness of divining must therefore be let go, and the time for answering must be waited for. Others, when some argument has been brought forward, want to triumph before the victory has been won, and cannot wait while the other answers. For when they themselves see no escape, they think for certain that no one can see one. Away with that levity of yours — for we do not all see all things; for though you may see more than I in all other matters, what wonder if I by chance see more than you in this one alone? But what will you have done by that importunity of yours, except clumsily betray your own marked arrogance, insolence, and peevishness? If in profane matters experience as teacher has given that saying the place of a proverb: "And sometimes even good Homer nods." And that one: "Often even a fool has spoken very timely things."

What will happen in divine matters, about which the truth testifies that it so pleased the heavenly Father that he hid his secrets from the wise, but made them plain to infants and sucklings? But it may be seen that, if an adversary notices that you have grasped his mind — namely what he thinks, and by what reasons persuaded he thinks it — and sees you nevertheless ready to contradict, it can hardly happen that much does not fall away from his confidence. Before, he was persuaded that, if only his notions were sufficiently understood, no one would fail to assent; but now, when he sees someone who understands them and yet does not assent, he must necessarily wonder. What else is that than that he is already brought to some doubting whether what he holds is not sufficiently firm? And at the same time he is kindled from this with a desire to understand what you have to object against it; which desire has made him fit to understand, and when that point has been reached, it is little short of the matter being already accomplished. On my advice, therefore, let this be the chief labour in driving away error: that you understand in the best possible way what is said, from the mind of him who uttered the words; not how you can interpret the words with a sharp quibble; indeed, if any force is to be done to the words, I would much rather it be done so that they serve the author's mind than that they be dragged elsewhere.

Among other things, another fault is very often committed in misinterpreting another's words, in that we reason thus in our minds: if what that man says were so, this and that would follow from it; and so we attribute to our adversaries the consequences that have been gathered by our own judgment, as if they stated them in that way, and we wear away all our time refuting things which perhaps they never even thought of, by which we both irritate them and also expose ourselves to laughter; to make this plainer, we shall use such an example: because the Pontificians say that sins committed after sacred ablution must be expiated either by our good works or, after death, by a kind of purgatorial fire, if we infer in this way — "If any sins must be expiated by our works or by our torments, then Christ did not blot them out, then he did not fulfil that for which he had been sent, to destroy the works of the devil" — and accordingly report that the Pontificians deny that Christ fulfilled that for which he had been sent, our speech will indeed be true to that extent, insofar as they in a certain way deny it, since namely this follows from what they lay down, but it will not be plainly true insofar as they neither expressly lay it down nor perhaps think it; and so, if we go on to rebuke them with many words on account of consequences of that kind, as if they were their positions, they will say that we are mad.

What, then, needs to be done? That we report what they say, adding nothing, taking away nothing, changing nothing. But if absurd things follow from their words, they are to be gathered to this end alone: that it may become plain that what they lay down is false. This candour is worthy to be loved, whatever matter is at last being treated; but it befits Christ's disciples discoursing on divine matters as much as anything possibly can. [Marginal note: But always do this, that the truth may conquer — not your opinion, but the truth.] From every Christian encounter the desire to conquer ought to be altogether absent; for let there be one aim alone, that the truth may conquer, and think it nothing to you whether it becomes plain through you or through anyone else; judge that your name will stand in a sufficiently good place if God's glory stands secure for him, to which it belongs in the highest degree that the truth be vindicated from impostures. Wherefore, if it does indeed happen that through the disputation itself what was hidden from you before becomes known to you, take care not to be ashamed to acknowledge it; care nothing about who it is to whom you ought to yield. Understand that it is most honourable to be taught by God, through whomsoever it at last comes about; let those indeed be ashamed who never learn. If you are ashamed of him whom God has used as an instrument to teach you, I know that you are despising not a man, but God, whose severe vengeance you will not escape unless you embrace with a reverent mind the grace offered to you.

Moreover, so that you may meet with as little difficulty as possible in this, when the matter arises, it will be best provided for if you carefully avoid those things which can increase this difficulty; of which kind is it, if you have asserted anything with great confidence — as if you have often testified that you will count it a great sacrilege, or a crime, or even madness, if anyone dares to contradict; if you have said that a man who does not see this is exceedingly stupid; and things like that. For it will afterwards be exceedingly grievous to be seen to have strayed so far from the mark; wherefore it is much better always to speak circumspectly — not so that you seem always to hesitate (for that would not be praiseworthy), but so that you simply assert the things that seem true, and deny in the same manner the things that do not seem so, and then adduce arguments and testimonies for this side or that, only abstaining from those formulas: "What impiety is it?" "Rashness?" "What impudence? Or what stupidity, to say this or that?" and from similar things. Mathematicians indeed use the firmest and most certain demonstrations, yet among them you will find no such word, for they add nothing to demonstrations either of clearness or of certainty.

If it can be free for us, whenever a truer opinion is disclosed, to embrace it, why should we have imposed on ourselves, as it were, a law, by such rashness of words, of clinging perpetually, with great pertinacity, to a false one? To this seems to belong that counsel of Solomon: "Beware of embroiling yourself in a lawsuit, lest, if by chance you succumb, you be covered with shame, and it not be sufficiently certain what you should do." [Marginal note: Prov. 25.] And since we cannot know in what matters we are erring, so that our speech about them may be cautious, we shall hardly ever apply, when need arises, the caution that will have been necessary in them, unless we acquire for ourselves the habit, whatever the matter under discussion, of avoiding formulas of words of that kind as certain most dangerous rocks. It also increases the aforesaid difficulty in a wonderful manner, if you hold your adversary in great contempt; wherefore you will act as much more honourably as also more prudently, if, whatever kind of adversary at last happens to appear, you keep yourself within the bounds of humanity. And if you abstain altogether from every indication of contempt. And since, whatever you at last do, you will hardly bring it about that you are not unwilling — your natural disposition strongly resisting — to be seen to be vanquished, your mind ought to be very well fortified against causes of that kind, namely by some effective consideration impressed upon the mind by frequent meditation; and indeed no more effective one presents itself than this: that we remember that this whole life lasts for a single moment, very like a play, in which leading men, kings, slaves, matrons, and maidservants are brought on.

For when the play is finished, the actors, having laid aside those parts, are either equals, or sometimes he who had been king in the play will be of humbler fortune than he who had been a slave. But if one of the actors were to take seriously those parts in which a less honourable place falls to him, and were to prefer to incur the indignation of him who outside the play can both, as a friend, relieve him in his need, and, as an enemy, afflict him his whole life, would not everyone cry out that he is utterly mad, because he values the emptiest smoke of one hour more than so many advantages of a lifetime? Or is there not here the same madness, or even one many times greater? Whether you are victor or vanquished, for how long a time will either the petty glory or the disgrace last? But if knowingly you resist the truth, and thereby make God hostile to you, what, unhappy man, will become of you? Perhaps it will have come about by your stubbornness that the truth remained oppressed, and that to the destruction even of a very numerous people, so that there will be as many, as it were, most bitter accusers of you as have perished for that reason. Who, thinking on these things, would not tremble all over? Farewell, therefore, farewell to all this stubbornness; and let each man be resolved to have no regard for his own dignity in this kind of matter, but to be wholly in this, that in every controversy Christ may conquer; and let him not care even to become the refuse of the world, provided that in everything his glory stands secure for God's name.

It stings you, evidently, if the victory must be allowed to a man whom by chance a little before you had reckoned to be nothing in comparison with yourself. Is it really so? But is there not another contest for you with Satan, by far the greatest? He, namely, has chosen you for himself, as a kind of citadel, to be assaulted with all his engines and with every effort; but by you the defence of yourself has been undertaken. Now therefore, if through an uncontrolled exaltation of mind you struggle against a truth you have come to know, will you not betray the citadel to Satan? Will you not surrender yourself most shamefully to the enemy — and to such an enemy? And that in the eyes even of your own commander, from whom nothing can be hidden? Or is there really nothing here that should sting? But it must be weighed again and again what choice is offered here. The contest is twofold, and it is not granted to gain the victory in both. If you are victor there, the truth having been oppressed, it is certain that in this contest which is with Satan you must succumb. But if it is too grievous to succumb here, you must let down your crest somewhat there; and, so that you may do this with a still less reluctant mind, this also must be considered: that, if you succumb, it will by no means seem so disgraceful to others as to you; for who does not know that nothing is so proper to man as to be mistaken?

And indeed, in general, whenever there is any controversy, this is to be expected: that one party succumbs. It is not, therefore, greatly to be wondered at if you are vanquished rather than your adversary; and though it be a disgrace to be vanquished, yet it is worthy of the greatest praise, and very apt to win the greatest goodwill of all good men, if you are of such candour as to be ready to yield, not unwillingly, to a truth you have come to know. Wherefore, if something must fall away from the opinion and fame of your learning (if there is any), yet that will be abundantly compensated by the praise of goodness, which can easily be recognised to be worth much more, from this: that a learned man, but an unjust one (if indeed he is known to be so), is abominated by everyone, whereas a good man, though with no learning at all, everyone thinks worthy of love and also of praise. Nor indeed ought you to persuade yourself that, unless you yourself yield willingly, it will not be known that you were vanquished; rather, the more you struggle against it, the greater cause you will give your adversary to proclaim your stubbornness and injustice, and the more material you will also give to many conversations about you that are by no means favourable: so that at last you can neither preserve the name of your learning, and are about to incur the mark and odium of the basest stubbornness.

If you meditate on these things assiduously, it will be a wonder if much does not fall away from the innate uncontrolled passion of your mind, so that, when the matter requires it, you will bear with a calmer mind that the truth conquer. [Marginal note: One who is about to enter a disputation, drive away pride, put on modesty.] And since it is also exceedingly difficult in controversies neither to be irritated nor to irritate, effort must be given that by very similar meditations, as by certain medicines, the mind be so prepared beforehand that nothing of that kind be committed. Wherefore, if you are about to enter a disputation, you must do just as a very brave and very cautious man would do when about to descend into single combat; for he will think over all the arts of striking, and will premeditate a method of defence against each one. Besides this, he will consider which parts of his body he has less armed, or in which there will be danger of a greater wound, so that he may guard those more circumspectly. Then, moreover, he will so sharpen his mind for the contest that he holds it resolved and determined either to conquer or bravely to meet death. In this way you must think within your own mind of whatever can happen to you while you are discoursing, to kindle your mind with anger, to pour darkness over your eyes, and in short to disturb everything — as that your adversary will heap reproaches upon you; that he will accuse you of acting impiously, or impudently, or wickedly; that he will call you ignorant of all things, stupid, dull; that he will deride you as a man of nothing; that he will quibble at your words; that, in short, he will use the utmost insolence; and that nevertheless you ought to be resolved to be moved by nothing, but rather to follow the thread of the disputation you have begun, with the utmost gentleness and patience, right to the end.

If nobility, if wealth, if learning, if authority or fame of name, or anything else there is that can lift up your spirits: think that in that part you are naked and exposed to injury, and that, unless you are on your guard, you will receive a wound there. But you will be on your guard, if you understand that these things are nothing else than Satan's weapons, with which he attacks you, and that they will at last lose all their power to harm only when you have at last ascertained how empty they are. But this ought to be fixed in your mind: that the business is not with the man with whom you dispute, but with Satan himself; and that the sum of the matter is not that point which has come into controversy, but many more and greater things. That Satan aims at this: to make from one quarrel endless quarrels, to kindle angers, to tear the Church apart into sects, to stir up seditions, to erect tyrannies; in short, that he is contriving nothing except the greatest conflagrations and devastations.

Wherefore he will send in not one litigant, or a second, but very many, one always more peevish, more stubborn, and more seditious than another. And that this is the chief point of his purpose: not to cast darkness over the question that is being treated (since that, compared with the other things he will contrive, is the least), but much more to conquer your patience, which, once stormed, he will seem likely to attain the rest with less trouble. Come now, what then will be the duty of a good soldier here? To be broken in spirit? To be wearied? To yield to the enemy? By no means; but the more shamelessly he presses the attack, the more resolutely you must resist him. And this must be done the more eagerly because the Apostle James gives us a most certain hope of victory, having testified that, if we resist the Devil, he will depart from us. [Marginal note: Iac. 4.] O, if only this were sufficiently fixed in our minds: that, whatever controversy is stirred, the business is not with the man who stirs it, nor is the dispute about one point of religion, but that through that occasion Satan aims at dissensions, sects, brawls, seditions, conflagrations, and the overthrow of the whole Christian commonwealth! How much would that be worth, for softening our minds and composing them more and more toward gentleness, and toward a quiet and circumspect manner of discoursing?

But among other things that make a disputant exceedingly peevish, arrogance holds the first place: if you seem to yourself to know all things, and in comparison with yourself despise everyone. But wealth, magistracies, ample priesthoods, celebrity of name, and other things of that kind often add to these spirits. If a mind has been driven mad by this persuasion, you can suffer no one to pronounce a complete opinion; his mouth being scarcely yet opened, you will think that you have sufficiently grasped both what the other was going to say and even what he could say. And with one word — with one word, though? No indeed, with one laugh, I know not what kind, or with a stern look, or with any gesture whatever, you will seem to yourself to have abundantly refuted whatever has been objected; but whatever you have affirmed or denied, if anyone dares to mutter anything against it, let him be held guilty of treason and a sacrilegious man. Meanwhile another man is so far from being called away from error that there also come to the error the utmost indignation and a firm purpose — since he could not maintain his own views with you — of defending them among all sorts of people, and of spreading them widely, if not openly, at least secretly; a disease from which it is very difficult to guard. For in proportion as each man excels in authority and speaks from a higher place, so he thinks he ought to seem wiser, and generally wants his authority to prevail in place of reason.

But yet this evil must be met, from which almost countless, and most pernicious, evils can arise. But above all, it must often be considered how absurd it is, if you abound in wealth, to arrogate to yourself any knowledge or learning on the ground of your wealth; or, if you are in a magistracy or a priesthood, on the ground of this office or that; or, if you are noble, on the ground of your nobility. For what have those things in common with wisdom? If you wish to judge after the manner of men: if there has been no talent, no teachers, no long and assiduous labour of learning, those things are indeed nothing; and as for the fame of a name, what else is it than the testimony of the crowd? And of what an author, pray? Of one who, as it were, is scarcely ever not raving in the highest degree; whereas, if you listen to him who alone can neither deceive nor be deceived, not even those things — talent, teachers, studies — are anything at all, but you understand that all things are placed in the one Spirit of God. So that, if this one Spirit is present in abundance, even if nothing else exists besides, you cannot fail to be most wise; if this one is wanting, even amid the greatest abundance of all other things, you cannot fail to be most foolish. Why, then, do these most empty names puff you up? — seeing that nature by itself, unaided by any external things, flatters each man more than enough already, and brings it about that he seems to himself to know the most; care must be taken that a foolish confidence in such things does not become an addition to the disease of our nature.

And whereas, to one who considers the other faults of human nature one by one, each of them seems to have wonderfully deep roots, and to be able only with the greatest difficulty — I will not say to be torn up, but even to be cut back in some part only — yet about arrogance, so at least I judge, if it is compared with the others, it seems to cling almost not at all. For if you are kindled to anger, if you take up hatred, if you carry off another's property by theft, if you kill a man, you cannot fail to understand that you are doing these things; so that, if you do so rather often, it does not escape you that your mind is inclined to anger, to hatreds, to thefts, and to the shedding of blood, and you can do some violence to yourself, and gradually struggle up toward sounder habits; but if you have persuaded yourself that you know more than is right, you cannot in the same way understand that you are doing so. For in whatever matter you go astray, it cannot in any way come about that, while you are erring, you should understand that you are erring — no more than that you should at once live and not live; since to err, and to acknowledge the error, would be nothing else than to err and not to err. It can indeed come about that, after you have been for some time engaged in error, you come to your senses again and acknowledge the error; but how few there are by whom an error is acknowledged, God knows. And even among these very men, the very few whom we know, each man's excessive love of himself never fails to have something by which to excuse him, so that he casts the blame on any other thing rather than on his own judgment; and so it comes about that he cannot notice that he is arrogating to himself more than is right; and since the disease lies hidden, no medicine is even sought; and I see that we can make no progress here in any other way than by that reasoning which we touched on just now above; and since that reasoning ought to be fixed and firm in our minds, we ought not to grudge repeating it.

Now it was of this kind. Since we see that all things in life are full of dissensions, both in divine things and in profane, in public and in private, at home and on campaign, in short in all things, and yet truth is one in all things, very many men must necessarily err. And since those also who are believed to excel the rest in wisdom generally disagree among themselves, many wise men too, as they are held to be, must necessarily err. And since one man errs in this matter, another in that, the matters in which each one errs are therefore very many; and since no one so excels in wisdom that something human does not befall him every day, though he persuades himself of nothing less, reason with yourself in this way: other men all err, and err often; and it is not the mark of a man of great wisdom, nor of a polished judgment, to err often; therefore no one of them all has attained so much wisdom that very much is not wanting to its full measure; and yet there is no one who does not attribute very much to himself; and when he does so, he is greatly deceived in his own opinion.

What judgment, then, will you have formed about yourself? Will you not think in this way? I too am a man; therefore that which is so proper to man must necessarily be present in me also, even if I cannot notice it, so that in this way you may strive to break down your arrogance? But there is in man a certain spirit or other, I know not what, the greatest of flatterers, and wonderfully skilful it is, which amid meditations of this kind murmurs I know not what in the heart, but in such a way that it stirs and works upon him instead — and that to madness — rather than that its words are noticed at all. But if you could catch its meaning, it would be of this kind: it is quite true, no one who attributes too much to himself is not deceived; but this arrogance is far from you; your case is altogether different, since you are led by a certain singular instinct, which does not allow you to go astray in judging. And so great is the power of this enchantment that, even if you have waged a long and most fierce contest with yourself, you can nevertheless scarcely lay aside even a very little of that empty persuasion. But this, however, you ought to hold as certain: that the less you are able to suspect in the matter of your arrogance, the greater it is; for while you please yourself, the wisest king, Solomon, numbers you among the foolish: “To a fool,” he says, “his own reasonings smile beautifully; but a wise man seeks counsel.”

So that it seems to be the summit of human wisdom to hold one's own wisdom suspect, and to understand that one needs another's counsel. This saying, indeed, must constantly be thrown in the face of that most wicked spirit, so that it may not turn your mind away from modesty. And so that you may understand the more that modesty is altogether necessary in disputations, I shall add something which at first sight may seem not a little absurd, but which is nevertheless as true as anything that is most true: whoever you may be, you cannot have a controversy with any man about any matter, but that, in proportion as you seem to yourself to know more than he, he in turn will think that he knows that much more than you. The reason is ready to hand; for if you look to the matter that is in controversy, if he thought that you judge better than he does, he would assent to you, and there would be no controversy. Since, then, he disagrees, he must think that he judges better; in other matters he may perhaps indeed seem to prefer you to himself, but in reality he will not do so; for if each of you were to go over all his own opinions, in those on which he has agreed with you he will approve his own judgment as much as yours; but in those on which he disagrees with you, he will certainly set his own before yours, so that he seems to himself the wiser.

This is most clearly seen in the judgment we form about authors. For you can attribute to each man only as much praise as satisfies your own judgment; and you must necessarily detract as much as there is in him that does not please you; so that each man's own judgment is his rule of right and wrong. Since this is so, whatever the controversy may be, even if he is the most ignorant of all men, and you are persuaded that you are a man who can speak oracles, as ill as you could bear it that you should be an object of contempt to him, so ill will he bear it if he is an object of contempt to you, and perhaps by so much the more ill, the less liberally he has been educated. Do you wish, then, that I should tell you in one word how he ought to be treated, so that all bitterness may be absent? Imagine that everything stands in the opposite way: that he is the man whom you seem to yourself to be, and you are the man who seems to you to be he; and take pains, as far as you can, to show forth the conduct which would befit your person, and his, if things stood so. Unless you do this, you can scarcely — indeed, you can hardly at all — escape the charge of insolence. If anyone has constantly meditated on these things, and has exercised his mind in them with diligent attention, he will, unless I am mistaken, turn out a disputant most fit to resist, by reason, errors as they spring up. Since enough has now been said about this, we must next speak about authority.
