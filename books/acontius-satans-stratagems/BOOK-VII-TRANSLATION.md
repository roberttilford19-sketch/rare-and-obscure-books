*The Stratagems of Satan. Book Seven.*

But when Satan has at last got possession of things, and has fortified his kingdom with all the defences he could, it follows that we should discuss by what arts he takes care lest any spark of truth shine in from any side so as to be able to penetrate there, by what arts he repels his most embittered enemies, the assertors of the truth, and what nevertheless is his art of defeating them utterly. And it is indeed no great matter to know toward what end Satan has his mind, his eyes and all his thoughts fixed. He would indeed wish all the received errors to cling firm in men's minds. But if it must needs be that some be detected, that those who should recognise them might be very few: then too he would wish that, where errors became known, not truth should succeed into their place, but other errors, or an unbelief in all divine things. [Marginal note: That errors should not be cast out, and truth should not enter, in what manner Satan strives.] Of those things which the godly ought to believe, he would wish nothing, or at least as little as possible, to be made known, and if anything be made known, that it should come to the knowledge of as few as possible: and that they should get a most infirm foundation, and be unable to stand.

And since neither errors nor the things which are to be believed can be detected, unless there are both those who may detect them and those to whom they may be detected, he himself would indeed wish there to be no one either who could detect anything, or to whom anything could be detected. But for a man to do anything, it is necessary, namely, that he be both able and willing to do it. But if he either cannot or will not, he will certainly never do it: and if you are to be able to teach another anything, you must first live, and be able to be there where those are who may hear you: or be able to send your writings, which may supply the office of the voice; and besides this you must know beforehand what you are to teach others, since no one can teach others the things he himself does not know. That there should be at no time anyone at all who has a knowledge of the errors and of the truth, the adversary can in no way attain: for the divine power to teach whomsoever it wills is much greater than that the enemy should be able to stand in its way. But he strives nevertheless in that way in which we have said the doctrine of religion is depraved and corrupted; and although he cannot bring it about wholly — that those who have been sent by God to make known the truth should neither live nor teach those for whose sake they have been sent — yet, that he may attain it, he leaves nothing undone for his part.

Accordingly, as soon as he perceives that some light has shone upon anyone's eyes, he contrives his destruction by whatever means he can: but if another means be wanting, he strives to accomplish it through the magistrate. But if this is to be done, he must be known; and to know him who has been sent for the sake of making known the truth will not have been difficult, inasmuch as he cannot be silent, or hide his treasures. But if he open his mouth, there will not be wanting one who shall denounce him as soon as possible: but if he has done it more cautiously, yet certainly when they have detected those institutions which have been provided (as we have said) for a defence of Satan's kingdom — as among the Pontificians, unless he be present at masses, unless he venerate images, and by other indications of that kind: among which this will have obtained not the last place: if, at a time when there is some number of those who think godly things, in the presence of one of them talk is held about them as about the greatest heretics, and many things are said against them with the utmost calumny, and he has not been heard to launch even his own stings of reproaches, or at least unless he has mingled his own laughter with others at every most witty reproach: then indeed much more, if he has dared with a single little word to free the innocent in some measure from the calumnies: as if indeed, when mention is made of such men, everyone ought to rush upon them with every kind of reproach and injury.

And since no one of the people may lay hands on anyone, it must needs be done through those whose part it is to punish wicked men, or in particular heretics. Nor indeed can barbarity and savagery ever grow so strong that it should be lawful for magistrates — who otherwise would assuredly not reign for long — to shed blood without any colour of a cause; nay rather, the accused must be seen to stand convicted of some offence worthy of death. But if to him who is said to have seduced the people leave were given to defend himself, since the power of truth is as great as it is, when set against impostures it could not fail to be recognised, and a most certain danger would be created for Satan's kingdom. Therefore every encounter of that kind must be evaded by Satan. But yet, so that nothing may seem to be done unjustly, it is enough to compose a name of a heresy or of a sect, or to usurp some old one, and to condemn it; so at the beginning they said Luther was renewing the errors of John Hus and of Wycliffe. And those who have embraced the evangelical truth were afterwards called Lutherans from Luther: and so whoever is reported to favour Lutheran doctrines, it does not seem that there is need of a greater examination of the cause why he should be burned; and that the people should not curiously inquire what just cause there is for punishing this kind of men with death — nay rather, that they should applaud their punishments — very much makes for the matter: that this sect, as they call it, labours under the foulest disgraces of every kind, and is so ill spoken of that they cannot even utter its name without indignation.

This indeed they attain by the arts of calumniating. So in the time of the nascent church Christians were said to meet by night, and, the lamps having been put out, to indulge most foully in promiscuous lust: which calumny has been most wickedly renewed also against the Christians of the renascent church: and others have been added besides — that they altogether loosed the reins to every kind of lust, that they took away the difference of actions and of works. That however anyone lived — whether someone ordered his life according to the norm of God's precepts, or allowed himself anything whatever — it was all the same, they teach; and by whatever things they can, the enemies of the truth strive to kindle the greatest odium against us, by which they may turn men away from all thought of inquiring what our doctrines really are, and of what kind they are. And where the state of things is such that those who claim the care of religion for themselves are themselves armed with the sword — as they are at Rome and in many other places — they themselves will punish God's servants. And they would punish in such a way that, unless the greatest stupor occupied men's minds, it could easily appear by what spirit they are led: but where either some king rules, or any other magistrate whatever, that the same be done must be obtained from the magistrates: but in such a way that there be no place for pleading the cause: and to obtain this, there is need of the same arts of calumniating.

[Marginal note: That there may be none who teach, he assails such men with calumnies.] But this is the calumniator's art: that when he falsely accuses anyone, he so occupies both ears of him before whom he accuses him that the accused can obtain no part: but that, as if the accusation were most certain and most just, so he is condemned, no opportunity of speaking ever having been granted him before an equitable judge. And that this may happen, many things must concur: in the calumniator himself, great authority: in him before whom they accuse, great ignorance of those things about which the matter is: and, concerning those things which he values most highly, the greatest suspicion: in those who are accused, the greatest poverty and the contempt of all: so that no one either deigns or dares to favour them even with a single word. The calumniator's authority has two parts. One is, that he is seen to be of such innocence and such holiness that he would never burden anyone with an unjust accusation. The other is, that he is seen to be of such wisdom that he cannot be mistaken in discerning the crime of him whom he accuses.

How in general authority would come also to the wicked pastors of churches has been said above. But they confirm it especially before princes and other magistrates (to say nothing of the arts of assenting, of flattering, and other arts of that kind) by a feigned remarkable holiness, and by the name of the office which they usurp for themselves. It is agreed that, as each thing is most distant, so it is least beheld: but each man's life is far distant from the prince's eyes, inasmuch as he, occupied with governing the vast bulk of his own greatness, cannot observe how each man lives, or of what disposition he is. Nor indeed can he say anything except from the reports of his own men: by which very often a deception is practised, not otherwise than upon the prince himself, and many are also corrupted by gifts and by favour. In the ecclesiastical pastors, therefore, and in their helpers, the prince can see the countenance, the bearing, the dress, the speech, most diligently composed for authority, gravity and the simulation of holiness: nothing besides. So that it is in no way likely that any wicked counsel lies hid under such specious coverings: especially if the prince himself is of a candid mind, as those commonly are who are sprung from illustrious births. That men of that kind have sufficiently ascertained what is true in religion, what false, what godly and what ungodly, cannot be doubtful to those who do not examine the matter more subtly, on account of their profession.

Nor does anyone in the least suspect that they do not also know fully and diligently each man's deed before they report it, on account of the opinion which is held of their both holiness and wisdom. That princes are ignorant of those things about which God's servants are accused, that opinion chiefly brings about: that the care of religion and the administration of the church do not pertain to them, but to the ecclesiastical pastors alone; persuaded of this, they neither bestow any labour on the divine letters, nor will they be at leisure to hear any controversies of religion. But whatever the pastors persuade them ought to be done, when they diligently execute it, they think they have so fulfilled their office in all its parts that there is nothing further you could wish. And so that the calumniators may the better order all things at their own pleasure, there is nothing they have ever less done than to exhort a king, or any other magistrate, to the reading and meditation of the divine writings. But princes are most suspicious concerning the extent of their fortunes, their dignity and their rule. Wherefore if anything of the received errors be touched, he who has dared it is forthwith denounced, as one who is contriving something against the present state of the commonwealth, and is planning seditions and commotions.

And that such despised men may not have anyone who would be willing to hear their cause, they lay snares by the same arts for the princes' familiars as for the princes themselves. Wherefore, since the enemies of the truth alone are heard, and the greatest faith is given to them, they load God's servants with so many reproaches, revilings, contumelies and suspicions that they inflame both princes and the whole world into the greatest hatred of them and their destruction, and drive them altogether into fury: and so great is the power of calumny that it overcomes all faith and all thought by infinite parts. What then? Since the Christian religion contains such honourable precepts of life, and the first Christians were of most holy and most blameless manners, yet how great a labour was it to thrust it into the ears of the Roman emperors, and to make them believe that the Christians were not seditious men, nor given to any kind of shameful life — nay rather (what they were) both most obedient to princes, and of a life most free from blame? But let us pass over those times: for what had to be persuaded to them really abhorred too much from the judgment of reason: that a man afflicted with punishment was the Son of God, and indeed God himself.

What has happened in our age? It is now about forty-seven years since Luther began to teach against the Roman church: we use the same divine Scriptures which the Pontificians themselves use. But we contend that those doctrines are not taught in that church which are recorded in the sacred Scriptures, but those which most greatly oppose them. To them we appeal: to documents which they themselves do not even dare to deny are worthy of the highest faith — to God's very oracles, namely — our men have never ceased through this time to speak, to cry out, to write, to publish books, and what have they not done? Experience itself also teaches that where the Gospel has been received, the same men rule there who ruled before, and no change has been made through us, nor attempted, nay, not even dreamed of: and yet this calumny could not yet be drawn out of princes' minds — that we are a seditious people, and hostile to princes and to all honourable institutions — much less has it been attained that what we really wish, what we are contriving, to what we are tending, what our mind is, should ever even reach their ears. Demented therefore by the power of calumny, those who are in authority are carried to oppress those who wish to make known the truth; and here one sentence and one judgment avails against all: upon truth the name of heresy is imposed (as I have said), as Lutheran or some other, and error obtains the name of orthodox truth, or of catholic.

So, if anyone shows himself to abhor any error and to favour the truth, here there is no place for disputing. As soon as the word is spoken the fires are ready; and those whom they cannot lay hold of, they compel through fear to consult for themselves by flight. But there are two kinds of calumniators: one, of those whose own mind bears witness to them that they lie and calumniate — of which kind were those elders, the accusers of Susanna, and Joseph's mistress: the other is of those who do not altogether understand that they calumniate, and are likewise deceived, as those of whom we have said that they do not rightly understand their own thoughts and counsels. And these are again divided into two parts: for some, although they do indeed understand that they lie and quibble somewhat, yet think him whom they accuse worthy to be crushed: and since they cannot convict him by true accusations (as if only witnesses of true crimes were wanting), they count it no crime if they burden him with false charges, and convict him by false witnesses. Nay rather, they seem to themselves both to act holily, and to be worthy of great praise. Others, even if they do not altogether understand that they lie in anything, yet when, kindled with hatreds, they descend to accusing — because the assertors of the truth diminish their dignity, advantages and gains, and lay open their feigned holiness — make a deception for themselves, so that they seem to themselves not to be pursuing their own affair, but God's.

Of this kind there must needs have been many of those who persecuted the Lord: since, as the Apostle says, "if they had known the Lord of glory, they would never have nailed him to the cross." This I hold as persuaded: that this kind of calumniators — which even makes a deception for itself, so that, indulging its own humour, it may seem to itself to offer sacrifice to God — surpasses all in inhumanity by many parts: others scarcely seem able to love so much their own advantages, honours and a dissolute kind of life, that the hatreds which they shall take up against those who seem to them to be a hindrance in those things should appear likely to have no bound. Let there be in them assuredly, if not a spark of humanity, yet at the last assuredly a satiety. These men, the more shed blood they see, the greater and the more ardent becomes their thirst for it: here Satan, as great as he wholly is, reveals himself. Let the recent commotions of France be a proof, and the savagery, unheard of since the memory of men, which Satan exercised in them. Nor indeed did such calumniators exist in the Jewish people alone, who raged uncontrollably against the Prophets, against the Lord himself, and against the Apostles: nor are they today among the Pontificians alone: there is scarcely ever a time when the church is in so good a place that it does not nourish some such serpents in its bosom: their venom does not always appear to such a degree, because the way to every savagery is not always open, an executioner is not always at hand.

But yet those who have eyes will not with difficulty know the lion, as the saying is, by his claw: in whomsoever there is any exaltation of mind, avarice, or love of a somewhat pleasant life, if there is joined to it any reputation for sanctity: whoever has touched any of these things even with the tip of his finger will most certainly find that his own age, and every place, has its Pharisees. Why do I delay with many words? Hardly anyone does not carry Pharisaism itself in his own breast: so that each man ought to examine himself — indeed, rather, everyone must needs do so — lest perhaps that which he abhors and detests in another, he himself labours under the same fault: otherwise, if occasion is not wanting, he will most certainly one day betray his own Pharisaism. At what time has the church been without such monsters, when in the time of the Apostles they were not wanting in the very assemblies of the godly? A certain Diotrephes, [Marginal note: Ioan 3.] a man of this flour, had crept even to such a pitch of reputation that he could exclude John, chief of the Apostles, from the assembly: and shall we doubt that every age will have its own, since that age did not lack its own?

Let us not think that that word was spoken to the Jews alone: Woe to you, who build the sepulchres of the prophets, whereas your ancestors hurried them to death: for it was spoken to men of all ages. And that word also is always true. But no one sees what of the wallet is on his back. But this all calumniators strive for most of all: that, no pleading of the cause being admitted, their authority should stand as a most certain proof; hence comes that: If this man were not a sinner, we should never have brought him to you, and, We have a law which requires that he die, and, though they are accusers, they wish at the same time to be witnesses: This man, they say, we found seducing the people. But, if it can do anything, the example of those whom a man has seen most ill treated for a like cause makes him unwilling to teach and disclose the things he knows. The reckoning also, on the other side, of those advantages, honours, and other things which man's foolish mind values more than is right, does the same: so it comes about that some, terrified by danger of death, and by the loss of the things they love too much, hide that light with which they have been endowed by God: wherefore those who give themselves to oppressing the truth partly strike fear into them by the taking away of their goods, by disgraces, and by most cruel punishments; partly they allure them to ungodliness by setting most ample rewards before them; and they catch them as fish with a hook.

But yet those who have been endowed with the light of truth, not only that they themselves may see, but also that they may teach, will not easily suffer themselves to be drawn away by these means from their office: for, that they may despise both all those terrors, and whatever reward for ungodliness and crime Satan can promise, they will think again and again of whose warfare they have been appointed to, how great a thing it is, how great, how certain, and how lasting also, are the rewards set before them. For so will each man reason with himself: Ho! what is this, that you should hesitate, that you should delay? Will it not come into your mind whose warfare you have been appointed to? He, assuredly, is the one who both created and governs the world and all things, with a power, and wisdom, and goodness greater by infinite parts than that any thought can comprehend it. But if you are ambitious, if you are desirous of honour: what could even be imagined more excellent than to serve such a Lord? And who is the leader in the war? Who but the only-begotten Son of God? What is there that you could wish for more? And is there anything for you to doubt about the victory? And yet it is in the hand of your commander, whose will nothing can resist: and so completely nothing, that not even a hair of yours can perish without the will of him whom you serve; he keeps the number of all the hairs of your head.

But if he suffers some of his own to fall, he does not do it from little love toward them, or from neglect: the most just and weighty cause why he does it is known to him, as most wise, and as most loving toward us: but he does not lack a way by which he may most fully make up, and that as soon as possible, whatever evil that may seem to be. And with what, pray? Not, assuredly, with any sum of money, or any other thing of that kind soon to perish, but with everlasting life, and that most blessed. Why then should you not be of the best courage? And see about what the contest is: namely, which of the two ought to reign, God or Satan. Often brave men, lovers of their country, lest it should come into the power of the enemy — though perhaps it would fare no worse — have exposed their lives to most certain dangers with most ready minds. Will you then, lest either your country or the whole human race should serve Satan, that most cruel enemy, and serve him for ever, not with a readiness equal to theirs rather give your life, which is owed to necessity, than reserve it for the evils of nature? What the rewards set before them are has been said; they are, however, of such a kind that no speech can express, or any thought comprehend, their greatness, even in the smallest part.

The warfare, likewise, is of this kind: that if only you have obeyed your most wise and best commander, even if you fall, you yet cannot fail to be the victor. What? Did not the Son of God himself thus once cast Satan down from his kingdom? Conquered, and given over to death, he conquered. Should it then either irk you or shame you to use the same condition which your commander himself, and so great a commander, once used? But disgrace, ignominies, and torments go before the victory: it is so. But the glory of the triumph will also be the greater for that: and while those things are of a single moment, this will be perpetual and everlasting. But to ardent thoughts of this kind there will also be added assiduous prayers to God, by which you may attain this unconquered greatness of mind. For it is certain that it can come to no one except as obtained from God. Nor indeed is it much easier to escape calumny, the handmaid of Satanic cruelty, than to repel force itself, by bearing it, by despising it, and by prudently and patiently waiting for time and place. But if, struck by fear of punishments or ignominies, or caught by the rewards of treachery, you have refused, when you could, to lend your service to the restoring of the truth, and have resisted the Spirit urging you: woe to you — with how great a severity of judgment will you have exchanged how great a glory!

It would have been far better that you had never been brought forth into the light. [Marginal note: But he strives above all to make the people obstinate in the errors they have received.] But if there are men who, for their part, both can teach and wish to do so, and yet there are none who wish to hear, many things bring this about. But the first is this: that, all discipline having once collapsed, men indulge, each one, his own temper. Some hunt after pleasures; some gape after heaping up riches; some are kindled toward a desire for empty glory; and, that each may obtain what he wishes, he leaves nothing undone: there is no regard for fair or foul, for honourable or base. But those who are of manners so corrupted will be deaf to every holy and godly admonition. For wisdom, says Solomon, has no entrance into a perverse mind. Next, it is a very great hindrance that they have been born in errors, and brought up in them. And when this has been added to a nature of itself most inclined to errors, it comes about that nothing seems more absurd than the truth, and that they cannot bear a man who speaks true things. Then, moreover, there comes in a certain opinion: that there is danger in a change of religion, but that the safe counsel is not to forsake the footsteps of our ancestors. And this, without doubt, those hold most firmly persuaded who think that, if indeed they were in any error, it would be to the harm, not of themselves, but of their pastors, in that, if they opened their ears to new things, they would be led astray, and it would be their own fault, and they would pay the penalty.

And that danger also seems to them the greater, the more time, labours, and expenses they think are needed to attain certainty in divine things. There comes in also that persuasion: that the things which come into controversy, and have also often been assailed by different heretics, have been most excellently defended by men endowed, namely, with the greatest sanctity and the greatest wisdom, so that they are now held as matters already judged. There comes in, finally, this: that in judging of things men use their own most foolish judgment, and are governed by those signs which we recounted above. For those doctrines cannot be approved by them which most of all oppose not only man's judgment, but also his wishes. Which condemn the judgment of our ancestors, and of many ages and nations — indeed, also of very many men most renowned for the name of wisdom. In which number are commonly reckoned those who enjoy ample fortune, and whom these very men exalt and make very much of: but most of all those to whom antiquity has added authority. But neither can those doctrines seem true for which very many and very great afflictions are straightway prepared for those who embrace them, and they become, as indeed it seems, hateful both to God and to men.

And finally, those doctrines which are not delivered publicly, but are instilled secretly into men's ears, in corners, and as it were by stealth, also shut men's ears to the truth, when they have been preoccupied with hatreds toward that unspeakable name of heresy, by which it has been defamed, before they know what is said. By what means this comes about has already been said. But much also lies in the fear which is conceived from the edicts and punishments of those who, when they wished to assent to new things (as they seem), were afflicted with most cruel punishments — not only for the reason that they prefer present tranquillity to the knowledge of the truth and the glory of God, but also for this, that those things which so great cruelty pursues may seem the more to be heresies. For (as has already been said elsewhere) what men would not have, that also they suffer themselves only with reluctance to be persuaded of: and since they would neither undergo everlasting damnation by resisting the truth, nor embrace those doctrines which would straightway be to them a cause of the greatest calamities, unless God does them great force, they cannot be brought to believe that those things are true. But this most of all makes men, struggling against the truth with most obstinate minds, utterly deaf: that among them both the Gospel, and those who profess it, are most ill spoken of.

The Gospel, indeed, while, defamed by the name of heresy, it is said to contain other things than it really contains — as, for example, licence for every lust and for an impure life, and other things of that kind — the professors, while they are accused of an impure life, contaminated by every kind of vice, and while it is said that they do not all think the same things, nor agree among themselves, but that there are as many opinions as there are heads. But they rage most of all against those who discharge the office of teaching, because they are unskilful, arrogant, rash, and of most corrupt minds, and because, led by envy and hatred, they strive not for the glory of God, but to overthrow all laws human and divine, and to break all the laws of justice and honour, and all the laws of modesty. As the report about Luther among the Papists is, that he rose up against the Roman church because he could not obtain the Cardinalate (as they call it): a thing which has also been said of certain others. And the reproaches which are cast against them partly have nothing at all of truth, but are devised by the utmost calumny; partly, indeed, they have something of truth, but, besides what the calumniators add of their own, they also interpret them amiss. For, as far as so great a disagreement of opinions is concerned, it is indeed true that among those whom the ungodly call by one name the dissension is very great and manifold.

But what, after all, is that? Except that, as soon as anything of evangelical truth is restored, in order that Satan may make it infamous and take away faith in it, he sows many absurd opinions here and there — tares, namely, among the wheat — and raises up many sects? On these he imposes a name that has something in common with the evangelical profession. Wherefore, since that multitude of opinions has nothing in common with the Gospel, nor does it belong to any just infamy of it: for let Satan make what, and how manifold, a sowing of tares he will, he will never yet bring it about that wheat is not wheat. But this also can happen, and does happen: that although some think rightly about the chief heads of religion, yet in some things — but of the least weight, however — they err, one in one thing and another in another, and so even among the godly servants of God there is some dissension. But who could wonder enough? If there are in any one city twenty or thirty thousand men among whom there is the greatest agreement about religion, five or six will disagree among themselves. Of that so great number of men thinking the same there will be hardly any talk; about those few the report will roam; and so they will be cast into men's little talk, so that it will seem that in that city there is nothing besides dissensions, and that among the whole number of the citizens scarcely two agree.

The same thing happens in men's life: for if in any people which has embraced the Gospel there has indeed followed a great amendment of manners, yet, because men are still men, it cannot be but that sometimes some crime is committed by someone. Here, therefore, the adversaries will have had lynx's eyes for noting a few crimes, but for seeing that amendment of manners they will have had no eyes at all. And it seems, I know not how, to come about in use, that in most things what is done amiss is noticed more than what is done well. Does anyone restore a deposit? Does he bear himself honourably with his neighbour's wife? Does he give a just measure to those who buy from him? Hardly anyone tells this to anyone, unless the matter carries with it some great novelty. As if a man contended that no more than ten coins were owed to him, and would not accept more; another contended that he owed fifteen, and wished to compel him to accept it. But if a man touches another with a single word, or in any way (as happens) has slipped, the report flies at once; complaints are heard. I pass over in silence that, where any people embraces the Gospel, there are many of the people who would just as readily have embraced any other doctrine than the evangelical: since they are touched by no concern for religion.

Wherefore, by their impure life, worthy of very many and very great rebukes, they are a disgrace and a dishonour to the Gospel, which perhaps with magnificent words over their cups — I will not say among harlots — they boast of. Who will one day pay to God the just penalties of their ungodliness and their shameful deeds. Indeed, there are not wanting some of the number of such men who, though in truth they care nothing for religion, yet pretend with great zeal that they love it: they give much labour to the divine writings, and come out learned and eloquent disputers in divine things, and at last come to the office of teaching and preaching, and — oh shame! — there is a great discord between heart and tongue, and they teach and do in very different ways: this, namely, is brought back to the greatest infamy of the Gospel. For it is wonderful how troublesome and unworthy speeches go out among the common people by the work of the enemies of the truth and of Satan, and how no forgetfulness can wear these things away. But although there may be many pretenders, there are also, without doubt, many who worship God from the heart, who yet themselves cannot but sometimes suffer something after the manner of men, which may furnish matter for whispering to the calumniators. And if these men can use, for the infamy of the professors of the Gospel, the testimonies of the professors themselves, while they — alas! — defame one another, that indeed would be a most firm bulwark for keeping off and driving far away the truth.

But that these men so defame one another happens in this way: when any controversy arises among them (as we have said), in the course of disputing they fall into those things which we said long ago happen in disputations; and with minds stirred up and troubled they take no care what they do, while they indulge their indignation — which happens the more, the more weight there seems to be in the matter in controversy, either to both parties or at least to one of them. Of such stumbling-blocks our own age has more frequent and greater examples than anyone could embrace even in thought, unless he were plainly a man of iron, with dry eyes: inasmuch as such is our blindness that we do not perceive how much the course of the Gospel is hindered by this, or such is our perversity that, if indeed we do perceive it, we would rather exhibit such spectacles to our enemies — to whom nothing could happen more joyful, nothing more desirable — than be cheated of that pleasure which we perceive by indulging the emotions of our minds. Nor does this happen only when a dissension has truly arisen among some men; it also happens not seldom that, when someone's words have been taken in an unfitting sense, so that he seems to wish something other than he truly wishes, cries are raised against him, and although he afterwards a thousand times calls God to witness that he did not wish that which offended others, and that he meant nothing new, it profits him nothing, but they go on to make him a heretic altogether, and to attribute to him things which he never said and never thought.

And so they have persecuted him as a heretic. To relate examples by name, lest I offend anyone's mind, I forbear. The cause of these evils is a disease of nature, which cannot be idle in us: namely arrogance and pride. Arrogance makes each man seem to himself alone to be wise, alone to have eyes and mind, alone to have been privy to God's secrets. Pride makes no one willing to seem to have erred, or to have fallen even a very little. [Marginal note: Wholesome warnings for those who desire to propagate the truth.] But if there are any who can teach the truth and are willing to do so, how shall it come about that the minds of men, occupied by the arts of Satan, refuse to hear it? Certainly, since Solomon testifies (as we have said) that wisdom cannot penetrate into a perverse mind, and since that man is most perverse who, having cast off all fear of God, gives himself wholly over to wickedness, men's minds must first be cured in some measure before all things: which is done by the recalling of God's commandments, and of the eternal punishments appointed for transgressors: and that the Lord's sermons had such beginnings, we may see: for so do wounded minds admit the physician; nor indeed is a beginning to be made only from the recalling of the commandments and of the punishment set before transgressors, but it must also be shown to the people that they have transgressed those commands, and that they have God angry with them: for when the mind of the hearers cannot but testify to itself that the matter is so, those who shall be God's seed will surely prick up their ears and conceive a good opinion of you.

But what is to be said first, what second, what third, can hardly be prescribed, and indeed hardly at all. Place, time, and occasion will teach it; but before all things, the Spirit. But it seems that, as each thing has the readiest approbation and is least exposed to calumny, so it is to be taught in the first place. As, if you were to treat of the righteousness of faith before treating of the impiety of the Mass, of the saints, and of the worship of images, and other things of that kind: and since, of the things in which error is made, some are as it were parts of the doctrine, others pertain to the judgment concerning the doctrine — as that faith is not to be given to men, and that the judgment of a godly or ungodly religion is to be sought from no other thing, not from prosperity or calamities, nor in short from anything else, than from God's certain, clear, and perspicuous oracles alone — which of them are to be set forth first does not sufficiently appear.

Things which display more of novelty sometimes cast into men a greater desire of knowing, sometimes also wholly alienate their minds. Things which have least of novelty — their beginnings are indeed instilled more easily, but they are also generally impressed very lightly: unless perhaps you can continue the work, so that day by day, as by a wedge driven in, you add greater things; but sometimes also we rightly begin from the repelling of calumny: in which it must be vehemently taken care of, lest, by attributing anything to grief or indignation, we seem to have wished not so much to repel injuries as to repay them. For the utmost gentleness and moderation become a Christian: let your aim be, not that you render to each man according to his deserts, but that you may win men's minds for the Lord. And that we may the less undertake anything rashly, or do anything with too little circumspection, the Spirit, that best teacher, is to be consulted. The Lord, I say, is to be prayed to assiduously, that he himself may show where, when, and whom we are to approach, may dictate the words, may open hearts: in short, that he himself, who alone can, may accomplish the work. And that men may not turn away from the Gospel for fear of any punishment or loss, or for the sake of any disadvantage whatsoever, they must be exhorted by the same reasons by which those who teach fortify themselves.

But care must be taken again and again, not only that those things be refuted which are cast both against the assertors of the truth and against their doctrine, but also, much more, that the ungodly have nothing which they may object truly, or even plausibly. That not only every crime, but also every suspicion of crime (if it can be done), be absent. Wherefore, although every offence is in itself most worthy of punishment, yet if he who has given his name to evangelical truth has committed it, and that in the sight of the ungodly, in such a way that an occasion is thereby given them of defaming the Gospel, or if the minds of the weak are thereby offended, as if those dogmas could not seem godly and holy whose professors live in such a manner — then indeed may God abominate it most of all. By what testimony, too, could the Gospel be more burdened, than if those who have undertaken to assail the same errors and the same kingdom of Satan with the same weapons and the same engines should destroy one another with mutual revilings, and one condemn the other as maddened by a satanic spirit? Or can we doubt that that onset, and that bitterness, with which some men thus rage against one another, is not from any Spirit of God, but from Satan himself, so that even by the testimony of the very assertors of the Gospel God's holy Gospel may be condemned?

By what tears could this madness of ours not deserve to be bewailed? Shall we then, of our own accord, supply arms to him whose most hostile and keen enemies we wish to seem to be, for whose destruction and ruin we undertake so many labours, expose ourselves to so many dangers, and shed so much of our own blood — arms by which he may defend himself, nay, even by which he may pierce us through? Or is this obscure? If the choice had had to be left to Satan, that he should obtain what he judged would be most to his advantage and what he most desired, what else could he have desired than that his enemies' cause should be cut down by the sharpest testimony of those same enemies? But this very thing happens: Satan obtains (I say) what of all things he had most in his wishes, and he obtains it from those very men of whom anyone would say that he had scarcely ever had stronger or more formidable enemies. And from this, if from any other thing, we may know what and how great an old deceiver Satan is, who not only contrives such things, but also accomplishes them with so great success, and so beautifully. But what will you do? He is so great an old deceiver, and man, whoever he at last may be, is never anything but a mere recruit in comparison with him, so that, if he is caught by his wiles, it is not at all wonderful.

But now that his wiles are detected, what will happen? Shall we nevertheless go on to fawn upon Satan's tail? But what before was the fault of a careless soldier, and easily to be pardoned, will now be the act of a most wicked and most impure traitor, worthy of no excuse at all. But doubt not: there will not be wanting other wiles of Satan, by which, unless you take care, he may still have you obedient to him: namely, he will suggest that it would be shameful, if, by changing your style, you should seem to confess that you erred before; that it would not be according to the dignity of your office; that very much would depart from your authority; nay, even that the faith of your dogmas would totter. In short, that all would be over with the whole Gospel, unless you seem, like some God, to have done nothing that was not most wisely done. But mark you, beware betimes: Satan (I say) is again tempting you with his wiles. Do not err: even if you go on stubbornly to defend your deed, you will never thereby bring it about that all men do not acknowledge your error; and the more you shall wish to hide it with your stubbornness, the more conspicuous will it become, and the more foul and hateful: besides that you will incur God's indignation, so great that, whereas you feared a small mark of disgrace, he will be about to overwhelm you on every side with ignominies of every kind. Let us learn therefore at last, some time or other, to conduct ourselves in our controversies with greater gentleness and prudence.

Concerning which matter, since enough has been said elsewhere, we shall not trouble the reader further here. [Marginal note: That they not destroy one another with mutual revilings.] For the refuting of the calumnies with which the professors of the Gospel are wont to be defamed — on this account in particular, that many wicked and utterly absurd dogmas are attributed to them, which they acknowledge in no way — this also pertains: what we now see happening everywhere, that any particular church of any place, or city, or province which has embraced the Gospel, and also privately some men, testify in writings published by them what the sum of their dogmas is, and by that means teach that the disgrace of the ungodly errors, which Satan never ceases to sow, does not pertain to them. How well this succeeds for them is to be considered. Certainly our adversaries, because they hear the names of confessions, although there is the utmost agreement in the chief heads of religion, nevertheless will have there to be just as many sects as confessions: they do this, I confess, by their own fault, without any just cause; but nevertheless the calumny has a colour. If there is one God, one Christ, one Baptism, one faith, what do these so many names of confessions mean (say they)? If there is among you an agreement of sentiments, if one faith, how does it come about that there is not one confession of the faith of you all?

And indeed in the old church of the Hebrews a restoration of divine worship was made more than once, and yet there remains no record (so far as I remember to have read) of any confession composed after our manner. The book of the law was read to the people, and the altars were cast down, the images were taken away, all traces of ungodliness were abolished. This was the form of restoring worship, that the confession should be the very book of the law: why then should we not imitate the same method? With what confidence do we wish our prudence to be greater than that of those most wise and likewise most religious kings, whose deed in this kind God himself approved? For that godly churches should not be burdened by calumniators with the disgrace of very many most foul errors, we do not attain by these confessions of ours. They calumniate us nevertheless; for they call all, by the common name of heretics, whosoever refuse obedience to the Roman church, and so, whatever impiety any sect defends, they defame all alike with it; nay, they even themselves invent certain monstrous errors to ascribe to us. But then there is added to the calumny this: that those who repudiate the Roman church are divided into so many sects that names are wanting to designate the sects.

And so that they may seem more numerous, they increase the number of sects in proportion to the number of confessions. And since Satan desires nothing so greatly as that those who have declared war upon the Roman church, his own most ample kingdom, should seem to be divided into as many factions and sects as possible, wavering hither and thither with certain uncertain and empty opinions only, having nothing certain, nothing firm. This indeed we attain by these confessions of ours, that we greatly favour his wishes: which I confess cannot be approved by me: for unless there is otherwise some excellent usefulness in them, which indeed I cannot yet see, I would certainly not wish even a very little labour to be bestowed in aiding Satan's counsels. If the churches among which there is agreement concerning those heads of doctrine which are necessary to be known for salvation had also one common confession of faith, so that, as they in truth pertain to one body, as it were, so they might also be seen to do so, I should not disapprove. But since that is not done, I would prefer there to be none, rather than so many. There is also something else which troubles me in these confessions: for whatever is asserted in them, no room is now left in that matter for that word: If anything be revealed to one that sits by, let the first be silent; nor indeed for that: Despise not prophecies; prove all things; hold fast that which is good.

For whatever has once been so written, they will have it to be already so certain, ratified, and fixed, that they will rather do any violence whatsoever to God's own word than suffer anything of it to be wrested from them; and so it comes about that the words which are men's acquire a higher authority than the words of God. In my judgment indeed nothing would be better than to abolish all other confessions by one Symbol of faith accurately framed. For certainly such an agreement of the churches would silence many little voices of men, and would take away many great stumbling-blocks, which retard the course of the Gospel in a wonderful manner. There indeed exists a most ancient Symbol of faith under the title of the Apostles', but all so approve it that nevertheless they do not acknowledge its use. For a symbol is a warlike sign, which in Latin is called a tessera, by which allies are distinguished from enemies and spies. Since it pleased the ancients to call that very brief confession of faith by this name, it sufficiently appears that they wished its use to be very like that of a watchword: namely, that whoever received it should himself be received, even if otherwise he were involved in many and grave errors; and that whoever rejected it should himself also be rejected. But this is not done: for not everyone is received who has professed that Symbol; and since this is not done, it appears that it is not used as a symbol: wherefore it will be worth while to see what kind of confession that ought to be which can rightly be used as a Symbol of faith among all Christians — those, namely, who in any way pertain to God's church.

And indeed a confession of this kind ought to be like a certain definition: for as it is required that a definition embrace neither more nor fewer things than the very thing which is defined — namely, that nothing be wanting, nothing superfluous — so in a Symbol of faith there ought to be wanting none of those things which are necessary to be known for salvation. [Marginal note: A common Symbol of faith to be desired, and of what kind.] Nothing also ought to be contained in it without the knowledge of which salvation can befall a man: for if anything necessary to be known were wanting, it would come about that one who ought not to be held a Christian, nor a member of God's church, would, because he professed that Symbol, be held for a Christian. But if anything not necessary to be known were contained in the Symbol, it might happen that those who confessed the things that are necessary, and who could be saved with such faith as they had, would, because they did not receive that thing without the knowledge of which salvation can befall a man, be rejected, although they ought to be acknowledged as Christians and members of the church. But if nothing were either wanting or superfluous, then none would be rejected except those who are to be rejected, and none received except those who are to be received.

From which things it is clear enough that to compose a confession of faith is a far different thing also from an institution or a summary of religion. For he who writes an institution ought to embrace not only those things which are necessary, but also whatsoever things have any use at all: but in writing a confession, as nothing which is altogether necessary to be known is to be passed over, so whatsoever is added that is not necessary (even if it be otherwise true and good) tends to the division of the church into sects, and to discords and quarrels. Whoever wishes peace and concord to be in the church must be content with the confession of the necessary things: for in vain would anyone hope that it will ever come about that it goes so well with God's church that all men think the same on all things. Wherefore that one way of concord alone remains: if any men embrace the necessary things, let them bear with one another, and treat their controversies among themselves lovingly and humanely, as brothers. Nor indeed is it enough, if nothing is wanting and nothing is superfluous, but those things also which the symbol shall embrace must be so expressed that he who has not acknowledged them cannot seem to acknowledge them, nor can he who has truly acknowledged them seem not to acknowledge them.

And indeed a man might seem to acknowledge something which in truth he did not acknowledge, if he himself understood the words in which it was expressed otherwise than he ought. Just as the Papists indeed confess in word the remission of sins, but since they understand such a remission of sins as is made by a compensation of the merits of good works, what they confess in word they do not in truth acknowledge. On the contrary, someone might seem not to acknowledge something which nevertheless in truth he did acknowledge, if he did not understand the words in which it was expressed, and therefore denied it. Although he would not have denied the thing itself, had it been expressed more plainly. What is to be done, then, so that each of these disadvantages may be avoided? Indeed I judge that it can be avoided in this way: some things altogether are to be asserted in a confession of faith — as, that there is one only true God, and he whom he sent, Jesus Christ — and some things may be denied or condemned, which is the case if anything fights against the confession of things necessary to be known. As if I deny that righteousness is placed in the law, or say with Paul that those who have placed righteousness in the law have nothing to do with Christ.

Take care, however, not to understand that it is the same thing to fight against a thing necessary to be known, and to fight against the confession of a thing necessary to be known. For since we have taught above that it can happen that a man believes things which fight among themselves and cannot be true at the same time, if anything fights against a thing necessary to be known, it is not necessary that it also fight against the confession of the same thing. But we are persuaded that the things which are necessary to be known are all contained in the divine writings, and that they are therein expressed openly and clearly, so that no one can fail to understand them unless he is blinded; for if anyone has been blinded, nothing can have been said clearly enough for him. Since these things are so, nothing seems to me more advisable than that the confession of faith which is to be used as a symbol should embrace nothing that is not necessary to be known, nothing that is not most true and most certain, nothing that is not most fully attested by the testimony of the divine writings, but also that it should embrace nothing unless expressed in the very same words, and forms of speech (so far as that can be done), which the Spirit of God himself has used. Not that any religious scruple is to be supposed to reside in the very words or syllables, nor that it makes any difference in what words a thing has been expressed, provided only it is both true and rightly understood; but since man's talent is exceedingly slippery — which often judges things to be most certain and most thoroughly tested, and incapable of being called into controversy, which afterwards not only come into controversy, but are also shown to have been false — I therefore judge it more advisable, in writing a confession of faith, to retain the words of the Holy Spirit rather than to use our own, lest, by indulging our own talent, we introduce something which is not only not certain and necessary to be known, but is not even true.

Moreover, even if nothing is introduced except what is both true and necessary to be known, yet it may happen that our words, without our noticing, have something ambiguous in them (for hardly anything is often so difficult as to express a sense in such a way that no place is left for a cavil), and so it will come about that he who would of his own accord have embraced the sense, as it is divinely expressed, and therefore ought himself to have been received, when he suspects that something else is said, rejects it as expressed in our words, and therefore is himself also undeservedly rejected. Which thing may be the origin and beginning of some sect: from which things it comes about that there exists a cause by no means to be despised why the very words of the Holy Spirit should be retained; whereas why we should prefer to use our own words, there can hardly be any cause.

For there is no one who does not confess that the things which are necessary to be known are contained in the divine writings, expressed in them most clearly and most openly. I do not now speak of the Papists, whose whole faith rests on Papal authority, but of those who wish the word of God to be the rule of their faith, and besides that, nothing. What else, then, do we wish, than to express all things openly and clearly? But we can accomplish this with the words of Holy Scripture, which we confess to be clear and perspicuous; shall we then perhaps use our own words rather than those of the Holy Spirit, in order to cut off from the sophists their occasions for shuffling? Forsooth we should be more cautious than the Holy Spirit, and should see, better than he, what is exposed to a cavil and what is not. What rashness would that be? I judge, therefore, that the things which are to be asserted as necessary to be known are to be asserted in the very same words in which they have been divinely expressed. But the things which are either to be rejected or to be condemned perhaps cannot even be expressed unless we use the words which those who assert them use. Only this is to be seen to: what we condemn; for not everything which is false is to be condemned in that confession, at least, which we are to use as a symbol of faith; otherwise, if an error is condemned which does not exclude the erring man from the kingdom of God, and because someone is unwilling to condemn that error he refuses to profess the symbol itself, he will be rejected, and those will be rejected who were not to be rejected.

But those things only are to be condemned which so fight against the faith of things necessary to be known that a man cannot at the same time believe both in what he ought to believe and in what is condemned; just as a man cannot have righteousness placed in Christ alone and at the same time also in the law; wherefore, since it ought to be placed in Christ alone, those are rightly condemned who place it in the law. But whatever error is of such a kind that it does not hinder him who errs from believing those things which he must by all means believe is not to be condemned; otherwise the true use of the symbol will perish, which, as we have said, ought to be this: that whoever has received it should himself also be received, but whoever has refused to receive it should not himself be received either. And that by that sign all who in any way belong to God's church — which in the whole world is only one, and has been so since the world was made — may acknowledge one another. These things having been thus set forth, it follows that we should try to frame such a symbol as we have hitherto described, with rude Minerva though it be: we shall make trial, nevertheless, for perhaps the Lord will at last give someone who shall both polish it and complete it.

What things are to be held necessary to be known was disputed by us in the second book at length, and these indeed we found, and no more.

1. [Marginal note: A form of such a symbol, according to the author's mind.] That there is one true God, and he whom he sent, Jesus Christ, and the Holy Spirit; and that it is not rightly denied that the Father is one and the Son another, because Jesus Christ is truly the Son of God.
2. That man is subject to the wrath and to the judgment of God; and that the dead shall live again, the just indeed to everlasting blessedness, but the impious to everlasting torments.
3. That God sent his Son Jesus Christ into the world, who, having been made man, died for our sins, and for our righteousness was raised from the dead.
4. That if we shall have believed in the Son of God, we shall obtain life through his name.
5. That there is salvation in no other, not in the Blessed Virgin, nor in Peter, nor in Paul, nor in any other saint, or in any other name whatsoever; and that there is no righteousness in the law, nor in the commandments or inventions of men.
6. That there is one Baptism in the name of the Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy Spirit.

Accordingly, this will perhaps be a not unsuitable form of a Symbol.

I know one only true God, and him whom he sent, Jesus Christ, his Son, and the Holy Spirit. [Marginal note: John 11.] I know God's law to be this: Hear, O Israel, the Lord thy God, God is one, etc. Accursed is whoever shall not have carried out all things whatsoever that are written in the law. I believe in the resurrection of the dead to come, and that the living and the dead shall be judged: those who have done rightly shall go into everlasting life, but those who have done wickedly, to punishment. [Marginal note: Matth. 20.] [Marginal note: 2 Tim. 4.] [Marginal note: John 5.] And since I, conceived in sins, and by nature a child of wrath, have grievously transgressed against God's law, I acknowledge that I am subject to God's judgment, and guilty of everlasting death. But since God, at the appointed time, sent his Son Jesus Christ into the world, who, having been made man, died for our sins, and for our righteousness lived again, [Marginal note: Rom. 4.] who has washed us from our sins in his own blood, through whom forgiveness of sins is proclaimed, and there is salvation in no other, nor is there any other name under heaven given to men by which we must be saved, I trust that through his name and grace I shall obtain life. I acknowledge one baptism, in the name of the Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy Spirit. If it so please anyone, there may be added a condemnation of those errors which fight against the faith written above, in this manner: I reject the opinion of those who deny that the Son is one and the Father another.

For I confess that Jesus Christ is truly the Son of God. I have righteousness placed, not in the law, nor in the commandments or inventions of men, but in Christ alone. Nor do I hope that I shall be saved through the blessed virgin, or any saint, or any other name whatsoever, but through Christ alone. But if it please that these things be omitted, whoever professes the symbol may be questioned — especially where there is any suspicion — whether he so acknowledges the Father and the Son, and so concerning any other thing whatsoever about which there is that suspicion, lest anyone, having embraced the words, should deny the thing which is signified by the words; in which matter, however (that the true use of the symbol may be retained), care must be taken that no one, in questioning, exact anything beyond the sincere sense of the symbol. But certainly, if such a confession of faith could be framed as would so satisfy all who belong to the whole church of God that they would think it ought to be used as a symbol, and that all ought to be content with it, and that a confession of more things ought not to be required, its usefulness would be greater than all expectation. For if anyone rightly weighs the matter, either this is the way of concord among Christians, or there is absolutely none.

For as long as each man wishes his own judgment on all other things to be the rule of believing, and a law which it is not lawful not to obey, it must needs be, I had almost said, that all are heretics to all: so that there will be no measure of sects, quarrels, clamours, and enmities. But if men were once persuaded that whoever should profess that symbol (whatsoever the remaining controversies might be) are nevertheless to be embraced as Christians and as brothers, and that the rights of the sacred things are nevertheless common with them, there would be great hope that even the controversies themselves would be treated with much greater equanimity; indeed, also, that, enmities having been removed, they would at last agree among themselves, and that so every occasion of calumniating would be cut off from the adversaries: which, that it may one day come to pass, is to be sought from God with the most earnest prayers. These things we set before all godly talents, to be considered piously and holily; if anyone shall have seen anything more in this matter than the Lord has hitherto revealed to us, we wish him to be asked again and again not to be willing to hide his talent in a matter of so great moment, but that, whatsoever in the meantime he may find wanting in us, he at least not interpret our attempt itself unfairly.

But that, when some errors have been detected and laid open, the rest also should not be laid open, may happen in this way: for if he who first began to catch sight of some light has not noticed all the errors — indeed, has once approved some of them as though they were true doctrines — it will be a matter of great difficulty for the same man afterwards to notice them, especially when he is warned by someone. The causes are the same which we were naming a little before: too great an opinion of oneself, and elation of mind. The former makes a man, however little he may see, readily persuade himself that he has seen all things at once; as he has found his judgment to have been right in some things, so he persuades himself that it was so in all, and that whatever comes into his mouth is an oracle. By the latter it comes about that no one who persuades himself of anything concerning himself is willing to seem either to have erred, or not to have seen all things; wherefore, if he has once asserted anything, he can hardly be brought to retract it when warned by another especially, nor even to suffer it to be torn away. And a thing which he would do unwillingly, he cannot easily so much as understand that there is a just cause why he should do it. Wherefore, as he would be unwilling that the things he has asserted should be false, so he cannot be persuaded that they are false. Excessive elation of mind also brings it about — which is joined with a disease of nature — that no one readily admits partners in his praise.

From this it comes about that, if anyone wishes to touch those things which the first man has passed over or has approved by his own reckoning, he so blazes out against him, as if someone were to come and snatch away, from a man wasted with hunger, the crust of bread which he had obtained. And just as those men themselves who first laboured at the overthrow of errors are a hindrance to another's touching anything further in that kind, so is it with all who have had those first men as their teachers: for since they have made gods for themselves out of those men, they judge that what those men were ignorant of, no one can know. Therefore, if anyone turns aside even ever so little from the space and the track beaten by them, they not only do not lend their ears, but are vehemently angry with him; and this happens much more in those matters in which the true opinion has been infamous under the name of some notable heresy, and those first men have not held it in any better place. For thus error is so established that it now seems unable to be torn away by any means; to which this also may be added, if any, when they have attempted to assail errors of this kind, have either done so by bringing forward absurd and foolish reasonings, or are infamous by the name of other errors, or have wished to substitute in the place of those errors, not the truth, but other errors, and perhaps fouler ones.

For whoever shall wish to attack the same things, there will be no one who does not seem to himself, at the very first word, to hold it as ascertained both with what kind of spirit the man ought to be — namely, an impious and most especially a sacrilegious one — and whither he is tending, what he wishes for himself, what he is about to say, and what he can even say: and so he will be altogether with a deaf ear; but that will happen much more, the more men have attempted anything of this kind, and the more often, and the more peevish and the more lost in their manners those who have attempted it have seemed to be. Indeed, about those things which might seem able both to recall men from too great confidence in their own wisdom, and from elation of mind, to modesty and equanimity, and also to be able to dissolve that excessive opinion about any men whatever, it has already been said by us elsewhere. Only those men are to be warned, to whom God shall have laid open something after the first men, that in bringing those things forward into the midst they use great dexterity, that they do not insolently attack others, as if charging them with a crime because they did not see all things, and that they do not malignantly interpret them as having wished, led by any private reasons, either to conceal something or to deprave it. A suspicious mind does not become a Christian man, nor indeed does it become him to be ungrateful toward those through whom God has conferred some great benefit on his church; nor are they at the very first word to boast that they have things which they may lay open in a quite new fashion, which no one has noticed before, or in which great men have gravely blundered, or to talk foolishly with other words of that kind.

And in this indeed I vehemently miss the prudence of certain men, who, although in some place their opinion had the least of novelty, yet, as if another kind of matter had been brought in, have in this way wished, by the name of novelty, to strike men's minds with terror; by which they have both stirred up various tragedies against themselves, and have vehemently disturbed God's church: which evils could perhaps have been avoided, if only some dexterity had been applied. But by what reasons we have said that old errors can be most especially established firm, we leave it to godly and prudent minds to weigh whether those reasons are worthy of any consideration or not. For what shall we say here? If anything of this kind has been done about any error, would it have had no weight toward confirming it more and more? No one, I think, will have said that. What then? That Satan had not, forsooth, so much ingenuity, that in a time when it was allowed through men's ignorance, or through minds already occupied with superstitions, he should make infamous, by the opinion of the foulest and most especially accursed heresies, many vehemently godly opinions? And that he should presently stir up men bold and arrogant, but of no sound judgment, at different times also, who, with errors badly attacked, shamefully defeated, should establish them firm more and more in men's minds?

Unless, if ingenuity was not wanting to him, daring, I think, or spirit was wanting to him? But if none of these things was wanting to him, I for my part do not understand what could move suspicion in a man who will not suspect that he attempted that thing in not one matter only, and not once only, and even accomplished it. But if there is a great cause why we should suspect anything of this kind, I do not see why, in whatever matter anyone says that something has been laid open to him, he should be condemned with his cause unheard, even if it is agreed that about that matter there are many names of heresies, and that many have said many things absurdly and impiously, and that often. Anyone will be able to be condemned in good enough time, when he has been fully and diligently heard, when the weights of his reasons have been examined to the nail. And from this indeed that question seems able to be resolved, by which it was asked whether he ought to be heard who, in a church in which the common prophecy had been restored, should say that he has, about an opinion which had been once or twice rejected, things which he would bring forward into the midst, not yet weighed before: for unless he ought to be heard, how will there perpetually be place for that word, that if something shall have been laid open to one who is sitting, the former is to be silent? And for that word, Do not extinguish the spirit, do not despise prophecies, prove all things, hold fast what is good? For what can we now treat of, which has not been treated of before? I do not say that that is to be endured, that anyone should wish perpetually to weary the church about the same matter with the same testimonies or the same reasons, and to be troublesome to it.

But from this also it can happen that remnants of errors and of superstitions remain, because, at the time when some restoration of divine worship and of godly doctrines is to be made, men think that all things cannot be restored at once at the beginning, but that those things are first to be taken away in which there is some notable impiety, and that afterwards, some time having been interposed, a whole restoration can be instituted with less hindrance. For the event has taught in many places that there is more difficulty afterwards in taking away the remnants than there was at the beginning in taking away the chief errors: so that, when it must come to that, it is much better to correct all things with the same labour. For Satan strives with all the arts and with all the forces he can as much to hinder a restoration of any kind whatever, as a full and whole one. That an exchange of errors should be made not with truth, but with other errors, seems able to happen by the same reasons by which we have said that errors grow from controversies.

But that men should come from errors to unbelief in all things, the first and especially the chief occasion is afforded by so many strifes and controversies about religion, as we have already warned above. The same cause formerly, when among philosophers there were strifes and quarrels about almost every thing, and when it was disputed plausibly on both sides about every thing, there were not wanting philosophers of great name who contended that all things are placed in opinions, but that there is no certain knowledge of any thing. There is added moreover also the impure and shameful life of almost all men of whatever religion. For certain men argue in this way, that that religion is not to be approved which does not make its professors good, and since in their judgment none does that, they despise all alike: but men are wont to argue in that way who, when they have cast all their thoughts into lusts and base and shameful desires, seek this, that at some time they may be freed from every sting of conscience, which is wont vehemently to diminish the sense of pleasures, and may be able with a looser mind to indulge their genius: and what they would greatly wish to be, they also most easily persuade themselves is. But that thing also brings not a few to that point, that whatever they are persuaded of about religion, they believe it for this reason, because they think most excellently of those teachers whom they follow; but those who teach contrary things they think to be very wicked and ignorant.

For when their opinion about their own teachers has been changed — which can happen sometimes from a very slight cause — all their faith collapses, like a building on a spoiled foundation. And that will happen the more easily, the greater the opinion they have had of their own teachers. But it so happens that, if anyone in good faith describes the life of a Christian man, however complete in all its parts he paints his model, those who hear him dream that he himself is of just so complete an integrity of life's holiness, and will not, not even when often warned, take any account of human weakness in him, nor consider how much easier it is to express true holiness in words than in deeds. Wherefore whatever of that kind they shall have heard and observed that is repugnant to the dream, they at once withdraw all faith from that man: and since they can neither return to the old errors, nor see anything better which they may follow, it remains that they cast away all thought of religion, and think that only is to be feigned which may be most apt for winning to themselves the minds of those from whom they see that they can hope for good for themselves: than which kind of men nothing can be made more pernicious.

Controversies full of contentions and quarrels are therefore especially to be avoided, and care must no less also be taken that we do not, by examples of an impure and dissolute life, and one alien to the profession of godliness, alienate men's minds from the truth, we who would otherwise have to pay the most horrendous penalties. But the same men must often be warned that eyes are not to be fixed on men, that the integrity of their life is not to be set up as the foundation of faith, that men's godliness is often feigned, often unstable, and indeed that the weakness of human nature is so great that, although a man can discourse most holily about religion and about morals, and does not worship God with a feigned faith, yet he cannot long sustain too great an opinion of his holiness. One example of David can teach that abundantly: I pass over the many unjust interpretations with which holy men are often burdened by Satan's industry, for which reason faith ought to rest upon God's oracles; if that is so, however men conduct themselves, it will be firm and stable: if eyes have been fixed on men, it will most certainly collapse as soon as possible. But if knowledge of the truth shall succeed to errors, what in the first place does not allow this knowledge to be firm and stable is if fruits do not stand forth.

For God is wont to exact penalties from the ungrateful; and the penalty is very often that he, as it were, demands back his own gifts. And this ingratitude is indeed discerned both in adverse times, when the enemies of the truth are raging, and in peaceful and tranquil circumstances: in adverse times indeed, when a man, lest he make loss of his goods, or be compelled to lack his country, the society of his most choice wife, and the embraces of his most sweet children, or even to give up his body to be burned by fires, and to be marked with the foulest ignominy, in the opinion of the common people. The truth which God has laid open to him, he does not dare to acknowledge, nor to impart to anyone, making, forsooth, more of the advantages of this life than of God's glory. In tranquil circumstances ingratitude is discerned, when we grow insolent, and indulge our genius, and bring all our studies, endeavours, and plans into the advantages, delights, and blessedness of this life, but leave scarcely any place, and sometimes not even scarcely, for the thought of divine worship. That this may not happen, men's minds must be deterred by frequent remembrance of the divine wrath, and examples must be brought forward of the desolations which God has often made, that he might avenge the contempt of himself, and forgetfulness of him. But for this reason also the sincere knowledge of the divine law is of but short duration, because, as soon as a people has obtained something of light, the same occasions arise by which, as we have already said long ago, a way is laid open to the depravation of godly and holy doctrine.

But since in that place we had said that the insolence, peevishness, and angers of the church's ministers and pastors are in great part the cause of those errors which arise and grow from contentions, lest it should perhaps seem to anyone that, at a time when there is nothing to be seen in God's church except afflictions, persecutions, ignominies, and want of all things, those things cannot fall upon ministers, let him not, I pray, forget in what season Diotrephes sought the first place in the Christian assembly, and would not suffer a place to be for John the Apostle in it; and it is indeed worth while to consider how beautifully painted the holiness of that man must have been, to whom the Lord's Apostle had to yield. The new errors indeed which succeed into the place of the old are either almost the same, with only their appearance changed, or are altogether others: of this kind perhaps was that one, that the Roman pontiff claimed for himself the power of forgiving sins: but of that kind, that holy men succeeded into the place of so many Gods, whom, after they have departed from this life, men venerate as, by the pontiff's authority, enrolled into the number of the saints — the names have been changed, and the rites of sacred things; the rest remain the same.

They call them indeed not Gods, but saints, yet in fact they hold them in the place of Gods: nay, even those who have wished to file the Latin language to its ancient purity and propriety, when they were about to speak of anyone, that he had been enrolled into the number of the saints, said that he had been enrolled into the number of the Gods: which thing, about Francis, who gave his name to the Franciscan sect, Peter Bembo left written in his Venetian history. Among the greater sacrileges for whose name the Roman Pontiff is deservedly accused by us, is this, that he claimed for himself the power of increasing the number of Christian doctrines, and of imposing upon men a religious obligation to believe them. But there are today in some thoroughly purged assemblies men who do not indeed attribute that same right to themselves in word, but in fact arrogate it to themselves, and do not notice it. For controversies arise: they themselves define which opinion ought to prevail: and although the heads of doctrine have never been reckoned among those chief heads which ought to be altogether certain and fixed among those who wish hope of salvation to be for themselves, yet whoever will not acquiesce in their opinion and subscribe to it, him they cast out, and persecute him as a heretic.

What, I ask, is this? Whither do these things tend? What do they wish for themselves? We have also seen somewhere men compelled, for the sake of ecclesiastical discipline, to make deprecation for sins that were not public, and stubborn men removed from the assembly who did not yet use the evangelical rule. And what is this thing too? If this example shall have passed into custom and practice, in comparison with such a thing the auricular confession, as they call it, was a very light yoke. Alas, how I fear, unless pastors take heed, and prudently weigh what they are doing, lest, through those very men who most desire Satan's kingdom to be utterly overthrown, Satan should either restore the same kingdom, or lay the foundations of some new one. But why do I say, lay? Nay rather, lest he have already laid them, while men wish to use their own prudence, and attribute something also to their own motions of mind. Search, I ask, diligently what were, as it were, the cradles of the Pontifical kingdom, from what beginnings, what rites, what laws, what institutions, what tyrannies proceeded, how beautifully painted pretexts each thing had, and to what it at last came out: do not allow it to happen that, when so many and so great examples are before your eyes, you do not seem to have profited at least something, warned by others' evil, and to have been made more prudent.
