*The Stratagems of Satan. Book Four.*

That, when doctrine is corrupted, yet no change seems to be made, there are several causes: of which some you are to understand to be in the doctrine itself, others in the manner in which the change is made. Some look to those who teach, some to those who are taught. In the doctrine itself these seem to me to be the causes: first, that it is, as it were, a kind of heap of very many grains, and those unlike one another, so that a change can be made part by part, and those the smallest parts. For just as, if from some great heap you were daily to take away a few grains, others being put back in their place, the heap would seem to remain the same, although it was gradually being changed, and beginning to be another: so, when the doctrine of religion consists of very many teachings, unlike one another, although agreeing together and tending to the same end, now this one and now that being corrupted and changed, the very body of the doctrine (so to speak) would indeed seem to retain the same appearance, but yet will not have remained altogether the same, for it will in part have become another.

Nay rather, a single teaching by itself can be of so great weight, that when it has been corrupted and changed, although it does not seem so, yet with the corruption of that teaching the corruption of the whole doctrine is joined. Because, namely, without that teaching, all the rest of the doctrine has no fruit at all, no worth. And of that kind indeed is that teaching, that by the blood of Christ alone shed for us, and by his sacrifice offered on the cross for our salvation, all our offences have been expiated. If you change that teaching in such a way as to say: our offences are washed away by the merits of our own good works, or by any intercessions of the saints, you will have made of Christian doctrine one altogether Antichristian. Nor indeed is doctrine exposed to hidden change, because it consists of many and unlike teachings, only insofar as the teachings themselves can be so corrupted and changed, but also insofar as some can be added, and others taken away. But I do not mean that a corruption of this kind can be made so secretly that even the sacred books are corrupted. For if that were done, the deceit could not lie hidden: but rather in such a way that, both in sermons and in writings, false teachings are used in place of true ones, and corrupted ones in place of sound ones.

Further, the greater the heap is, the less easy it is, if a change takes place, to notice it. For that reason the enlarging of doctrine contributes very much to that concealment: and it is enlarged indeed while it is handled in various ways, while it is extended by many and various questions, which spring up one out of another and are propagated to infinity. The cause of this evil is men's excessive curiosity, rashness, arrogance and ambition, crazed by which diseases we wish to commit to writing whatever we dream. Nay rather, if there is nothing for us to write, we unweave other men's work and weave it together again anew: and we prefer to do over again what has been done already, rather than seem to do nothing. But this contributes very much to covering a corruption: if the false teachings that are put back in place of sound ones, although in fact they are very different, nevertheless in appearance seem to be the same. [Marginal note: The causes of that change are partly in the doctrine itself, partly in the manner of the change, which is made gradually and by degrees, without being perceived.] Which can come about in several ways. And the first indeed is, when some true teaching has been expressed in the divine writings in words which admit several interpretations, not all of them however true: and another has been fashioned which agrees with those words wrongly understood: for so, although something very different is said, yet the same thing seems to be said, and no change seems to be made.

To this belong also cases where the speech is figurative: so that one thing is said, another seems to be said, and what seems to be said is what is taken: and that would indeed happen if, whereas it is written, "If your eye is a stumbling-block to you, tear it out, and cast it away," it should be so stated that, if the sense of sight is to a man an occasion of sinning, he should tear out his eyes, and become altogether blind, and so remove from himself the occasion of sinning. For those words seem to express this teaching, although it is in fact very different: for they mean this. Whatever it is that in the way of the Gospel either delays or hinders your progress, remove it from you as soon as possible: for it is better to do without it than, called away from such a course, to suffer the loss of the kingdom of heaven, and to be given over to everlasting torments. To the same place belongs this: if, when the teaching which the words express is the same, they are nevertheless expounded just as if they meant something else. Which happens with those words: "Go, and show yourself to the priest": from which that teaching has been taken, by which everyone is commanded to recount his own offences to a priest. Another way is, when in a true teaching something is understood (whether it is expressed, or not) and the false one is so expressed that no room is left for it. And it is either an exception, or a condition, or a circumstance, or any kind of explanation: or something is added, or there is set down either a condition or an explanation that does not belong.

A condition or an exception is altogether contained in those words of the Apostle: "Do not be deceived: neither fornicators, nor worshippers of idols, and so forth, shall obtain the kingdom of heaven," for the condition is to be understood, unless they repent. But if anyone should so state it, that the offence of unchastity is never forgiven, he would indeed set down something consistent with those words, but the teaching would be very different. In the same way those words of Solomon need an explanation: "The condition of men and of beasts is the same, as these die, so do those also: both breathe alike, and man has no advantage over the beasts." And the explanation which must be understood is, that it is so insofar as it is permitted to human judgment to attain, but not altogether. If that explanation is shut out, and anyone uses Solomon's words, he will overturn all religion from its very foundations. They add to the true teaching who declare that those will obtain eternal life who shall believe that their sins are forgiven them for Christ's sake, provided only they have recounted their sins one by one to a priest, and have obtained absolution from him. He changes something of the true teaching who says that the law is holy for this reason, that the knowledge of it makes a man holy.

For it is not holy for that reason, but because it makes a man despair of his own powers, and leads him to Christ, the only hope of salvation, and because moreover it shows what things please God, and what things displease him: so that we worship him not according to our own inventions and institutions, but according to his prescription. Finally, another way is, when from some true teaching a false one is wrongly drawn out, and then the inference is used in place of the teaching from which it was drawn. So when the Roman pontiff, from those words, "Whatever you bind on earth shall be bound also in heaven, and whatever you loose on earth shall be loosed also in heaven," has forced out this teaching, in which he lays it down that he can make new laws, and establish new heads of doctrine (they call them articles of faith), and compel men both to keep the former and to believe the latter: he uses that inference as if it were a teaching handed down from God. But there is a certain kind of false inferences wonderfully hidden, inasmuch as they are drawn partly from assumptions which are neither expressed, nor perhaps even grasped in thought: but are admitted without any dispute, just as if they flowed from the most certain and most evident things. Which is what happens in the case of that inference which has just been mentioned.

For in order for it to be brought about that the power of making laws was permitted to Peter, the word "binding" must signify the same thing as making laws. For so you would rightly infer: to bind is to make laws: the power of binding has been permitted to Peter: therefore also of making laws. And in the same way also one might infer: to loose is to repeal laws: but the power of loosing has been given to Peter, therefore also of repealing laws. And since it has been said, "Whatever you bind," and, "Whatever you loose," it will follow that Peter was allowed both to make and to repeal laws of every kind, even if he had set his mind on repealing the Decalogue. Then, in order for it to be brought about that that right belongs to the Roman pontiff, this had to be assumed: Whatever right has been given to Peter, the same has been given to the Roman pontiff: since therefore the right both of making and of repealing laws of every kind has been given to Peter, the same has been given to the Roman pontiff. But this conclusion has been gathered with no mention made of those assumptions, that to bind and to loose is both to make and to repeal laws, and that whatever right has been given to Peter, the same belongs to the Roman pontiff. Nor indeed is it given to just anyone to notice what, in the drawing of inferences, is passed over, and wrapped up in silence as if it were something that had been proved, although it is by no means true.

That it also comes about through the obscurity of the true teachings, that if any change is made it cannot easily be detected, anyone can understand of his own accord. And the obscurity of Christian doctrine comes first from this, that it treats of the things of the Spirit: and therefore they cannot be understood without the Spirit. If there is a lack of the Spirit, even if a great corruption of the doctrine takes place, it will nevertheless not be recognised. There is added then another cause of obscurity: that, since it was handed down in other languages, and into others into which it has for the most part been translated not very suitably, we have to learn it by study: and although someone applies himself to learning those languages, I mean Hebrew and Greek, yet since they no longer live in the use of any people, but lie only in writings and books, he never attains them so fully that the proper force of many words and phrases, and their manifold use, does not escape him: yet that obscurity is not so great but that, if some measure of the Spirit is present, [Marginal note: The causes of that change are partly in the doctrine itself, partly in the manner of the change, which is made gradually and by degrees, without being perceived.] at least those things which are necessary to be known for salvation can easily be understood. And since we have spoken of the causes which are understood to be in the doctrine, we must now speak of the manner in which the change is made. The manner of its concealment, then, has this cause for the change that is made being the less conspicuous, that the change is made gradually.

For if, as we were just saying, from some great heap of grains a few grains were daily taken away, and as many others put in their place, and mixed into the heap, who could recognise that exchange? Who also will have noticed how much is daily added to some boy's height? Or how much a man daily grows old? But exactly the same thing happens in the change of doctrine. For someone today will devise, if nothing else, some general name, to which he subjects several things whose nature or whose account is common, and afterwards the same name will be transferred to things very unlike them: and so it will come about that the notion and the account of those first things is obscured: which is what they did who began to call the sacred washing and the Lord's Supper Sacraments. For what harm could anyone even suspect in this? But after some time there were men who, since they thought that any sacred or holy rite whatever was called by the name of Sacrament, began to transfer that word also to the laying on of hands, to marriage, penance, confirmation, and extreme unction: by which it came about that, when washing and the Supper had obtained a name in common with very different things, their true account was obscured.

For together with the general name there remained also a general notion, in common with those things: the notion that was proper to them, and which ought never not to be before our eyes, fell into oblivion. From this it also came about that, just as it was agreed that the washing and the Supper had been instituted by the Lord, so all the things that were called sacraments were commonly believed to have been instituted by him as well. At last even bells began to be washed (as they call it): which, what else was it than to give the untaught common people occasion to think that there is as much in the washing of a man as they understood there to be in that of a bell, and so for the memory of the washing away of sins — which washing the washing that is made with water signifies to have been made by the blood of Christ — to be taken away? But this contributes much to covering the deceit, if there is a plausible pretext at hand: so that they seem not to be introducing something new, but to be wishing to keep the institutions they have received safe with a weaker observance. Wherefore, when the first Christians, or rather those who succeeded the first, had relaxed not a little of their former fervour of godliness (which generally happens when the exercise of afflictions ceases), and were therefore no longer so frequent in the breaking of bread, there was perhaps someone who thought it advisable if at least the priest, in the presence of many standing by, should himself more often take that bread and wine, and so celebrate the memory of the Lord's death: for it would be almost the same as if all who stood by had partaken.

In which indeed there could seem to be no innovation at all: rather, it seemed to be nothing else than to take care that the Lord's most saving institution should not fall out of use through disuse. When this had passed into a custom, because the whole was done in commemoration of that sacrifice which the Lord had offered on the cross for us, and the name of priest was not now wanting, this gave someone occasion to transfer the name of the thing signified to the thing that signifies it: so that he called that action no longer a commemoration of the sacrifice, but a sacrifice. Afterwards, as the thing obtained the name of sacrifice, so it began to be held for a sacrifice: who would wonder? So, although something new was introduced daily, yet because the change made at any one time was the smallest possible, and some most plausible disguise was never wanting, it could not be noticed: until at last the Lord's simplest institution of the breaking of bread was turned, by a kind of wonderful metamorphosis, into that most august and most elaborate work of the Mass. That the same thing has happened in all other matters, whoever looks more carefully will easily find.

In those who teach [Marginal note: Sometimes in those who teach, on account of their great reputation for learning and holiness.], if they corrupt anything, what chiefly keeps it from appearing is if they stand in a great reputation for godliness, holiness and learning, and if they are believed to be most favourably disposed toward the received doctrine. But such a reputation anyone gains either by some outstanding specimen of himself that he has given, or by the testimony of those who themselves stand in the best reputation, or by a kind of quasi-hereditary right. He gives a good specimen of himself who discourses acutely and copiously on divine things, and by citing many testimonies from the sacred writings, and not ineptly, seems to be much versed in studies of that kind: but especially if, in attacking those teachings from which the people shrink, he has often shown himself keen: and if those whom the people value most highly, he himself also shows that he greatly esteems and honours them. But the testimonies of approved men confer reputation: because they are believed, on account of their gravity and prudence, to have testified only about things which they had ascertained: although very often, by many pretences, flatteries, and other arts, the excessive easiness of good men is overcome, so that they are brought to commend those whose godliness and learning they have but little known. But yet, however testimonies may fall out for certain men, the simple crowd nevertheless gives them belief in no other way than as if they had been uttered from the tripod.

Some indeed gain a reputation by a kind of quasi-hereditary right for this reason, that they succeed into the place of men whose life and doctrine alike were thoroughly approved. For it is likely that none but good men were put in the place of good men, and so the reputation for integrity and learning is transferred from one man to others: no otherwise than inheritances from fathers to sons. But in truth, just as those who from a humble and slender fortune have by great labours and troubles, and by equal industry, gained ample fortunes for themselves leave sons who are heirs indeed of all the goods that were gained, but not of their industry and ability — since, born amid wealth, they have experienced no necessity of exercising their ability — so too those who are sometimes raised up by divine providence to purge the Church thoroughly of errors, since they must necessarily be exercised by greater and arduous difficulties, must necessarily also be furnished with the most excellent virtues, and thereby become very illustrious: and they afterwards depart, when things are now settled, at a time when Satan wages war no longer by force, but by wiles, which they least of all noticed, and those who come after them have no illustrious exercises of prudence and godliness.

But although they are much unequal in virtue, yet as by a certain outward appearance of their predecessors they hold their place, so they uphold their dignity and reputation: and the farther you have gone back from those first men, the less like them, generally, you see their successors to be. And while the same authority, or even a greater, remains, but there is very little of godliness, prudence, learning, and sensible diligence: who would not understand that it can most easily happen both that through ignorance they corrupt many things day by day, and yet that because of their authority no one notices? But in order that pastors of this kind may gradually be put in their place, from whom Satan may hope for a greater and speedier corruption of doctrine, it is of the greatest help to him if with the offices of the Church those things are joined which make them very much to be desired: as if pastors are very highly valued, and all rise up to them and give place to them: if they live at ease in some great abundance of wealth: if they are seen to exercise judgment over all: but if it is thought wicked to touch them. For who would not greatly desire to be in such a state? Wherefore if allurements of this kind are present, and some place falls vacant: do not think that the best man will obtain it; much more easily will the man who surpasses all in the arts of canvassing.

A good and godly man, even if he seems to himself that he would otherwise canvass honourably, with his whole mind set on God's glory, not on his own dignity or advantages, will nevertheless not canvass for this reason: because those in whom true godliness is present, since they are conscious, beyond others, of their own weakness, which is never not great, think modestly of themselves, and prefer almost everyone to themselves. But if the times are still so good that offices are conferred not on those who seek them, but on those who are chosen: not even then will the condition of the better men always be the better. For simple men, enemies of all deceit and of all evil arts, since they think they ought to give their effort more to being good than to seeming so, will very easily be beaten in gaining a reputation for holiness by artful men greedy for honours. For they will cover their own little faults less carefully: they will labour more to lay aside their faults than that they should not appear; they court men's favour by no arts. The man who has set his mind on honours will cover all his faults most carefully: the things he has understood to be most approved by those who can advance him, to those he will conform himself as carefully as possible; and he will leave nothing undone in pretence, concealment and flattery, until he proves himself to them exactly to the rule: and by this concealed way of canvassing, of so much greater artifice and effort, bad men sometimes win no less than if they were allowed to use that open way.

It remains that we speak of those causes which, since they do not allow men to notice that a change is taking place in doctrine, are placed in those who are taught [Marginal note: Sometimes the carelessness or ignorance of those who are taught.]. But there are chiefly two of them: carelessness, and ignorance. Carelessness creeps in, for the most part, from this: that the people trust their pastors too much, and are persuaded that they will fall into no error, and that therefore there is no need of great attention: indeed, that whatever they themselves give must be received, and not examined too curiously. To this are added the other occupations to which men give themselves. For that saying extends most widely, "Where the treasure is, there is the heart": and that, "No one can serve two Lords." [Marginal note: Matt. 6.] How it comes about, however, that after there has once been in a people a great knowledge of divine things, it as it were vanishes: besides the cause just stated, we shall recount some others also elsewhere. This one only we shall now add: that the people itself is in a kind of continual change, some departing day by day, others springing up and growing up in their place. Hence it also comes about that, since the change in each man's lifetime is very small, he either cannot observe it, or if he does look into it, does not think so much of it as to think that any dispute ought to be raised on account of it.

But this too is a very great hindrance to the people's noticing that any change is taking place: if men are persuaded that they cannot judge about the things that touch religion: as if "Is" and "Not", and other words, did not mean the same in the divine writings as they do in common custom of speaking, or as if nothing could be understood without great knowledge both of languages and of other things: and as if the Spirit without these aids had no power. Hence it comes about that, although they do not think they understand even those things which in a certain way they do understand — those, namely, which are expressed in the clearest words — they at last come to such stupidity that in truth they do not understand; so much so that, although they have before their eyes a teaching handed down from God so clear that nothing can be clearer, yet if those to whose authority they attribute everything say something plainly contrary, they will give them belief, and will think that they do not see what they most openly see. And by these means it happens that when a change of doctrine for the worse takes place, it is not noticed. But when a beginning has been made of corrupting doctrine, errors must at once necessarily be born from errors, and be propagated to infinity: and men gradually bring so great a darkness upon themselves, God blinding them for the most just causes, and they fall into errors so foul: that if God, having pity on someone, opens his eyes, so that he sees in what darkness he has been living, he can scarcely believe himself, or persuade himself that he was once wrapped in such blind errors.

And if that happened only to some foolish little men of the common people, it would be much less a wonder to be wondered at. But it happens equally to men endowed with the greatest learning and the greatest experience of things: if you read through the writings of some of the Scholastic Doctors (as they are called), you will in places find so much keenness that you wonder. What? You will often see them split the finest thread into many parts, and perform the anatomy of a flea: but presently fall so shamefully, and put forward things so absurd, that you cannot wonder enough. Therefore the poet's counsel must be obeyed: "Resist beginnings: the remedy is prepared too late." But there is need of a twofold caution: namely, that no change take place in doctrine while it is pure, and that if any has taken place, it be noticed. But whatever change of this kind takes place, those whose task it is to teach the people bear almost all the blame for it: for if they are not themselves its authors, at any rate it takes place while they sleep.

Pastors and teachers ought therefore to have lynx's eyes, and to have ready at hand and counted up those causes by which it comes about that changes are not noticed, and to watch carefully that they do not stumble anywhere. But the best caution [Marginal note: Cautions for preserving the purity of doctrine.] for preserving pure doctrine will be if they avoid all curious and empty controversies: if they never fail to have before their eyes the aim of all doctrine, and besides that the sequence of all things that tend to the same end (about which we have spoken above): if they love not only the things themselves, but also the very words and forms of speaking which the Holy Spirit has used [Marginal note: One must anxiously cling to Scripture's own words and phrases.], and hold all others in strong suspicion. Not that I would have them sound of nothing but Hebraisms — for their speech could not be plain and clear — but I would have all those expressions avoided which, beyond any necessity of expressing Greek or Hebrew, are wont to be devised by overly subtle disputers, and those opinions which men gather by their own ingenuity from the divine testimonies. How much this matters may be gathered from an example of this kind. The Papists think it makes no difference whether they say that the Church cannot err, or whether they set forth those words of the Lord, "Wherever two or three are gathered together in my name, there I am in the midst of them."

But how much difference there is can be plain from this: that if someone understands the Church to be the Pope, the Cardinals, and the Bishops anointed by the Pope, when he has heard that former opinion — that whatever they shall have decreed must be ratified and firm — he will think so. But if he instead looks in his mind at the Lord's own words, and it comes into his mind that men of that kind care for nothing besides their own advantages, wealth, and power: he will be so far from understanding the same thing, that perhaps, since he cannot approve their actions and deeds, he will come to hope from those words that he himself, with some good men who love God from the heart, if they come together and together implore God's help, can settle better what must be believed and done, for the sake of gaining salvation, than if they persisted in trusting such pastors. And this, indeed — that men should use the words comprised in the divine writings rather than any others — is especially to be done when something is stated as certain and ascertained, or as a rule of faith or of life, or as something from which something else must be concluded. For in expositions, as perhaps some greater freedom may be needed, so there is also less danger. For where both God's word and the exposition are at the same time, as it were, in view: there it cannot be hidden that the exposition is man's word, so that one may reject it unless it seems suitable.

But everyone must compare the teaching of his own age with no other than that which, beyond all controversy, has been without any stain: that is, with the teaching of the Apostles. Therefore, even if in our age the Gospel has, as it were, come to life again, yet let no one think in this way, that he has to examine whether anything has departed, or has not departed, from that purity which the Gospel had attained at this time: rather, on the contrary, it must be seen again and again whether any stain still remains, or whether in some part it has not been restored fully enough to its former purity and its former brightness; and persuade yourself for certain that you will hardly ever be scrupulous enough (so to speak) in rejecting every word that is not in the divine writings. For nowhere does man not wish to be more cautious and wiser than the Holy Spirit, and he can scarcely fail to mix in something of his own. Therefore whatever is from man can never be suspected enough. And since each thing is the better kept the more guardians it has, the people must be warned again and again that both the reading of the divine books and the care of religion belong not to the pastors of the Church alone.

But whoever wishes himself to be saved must also be a watcher, whether any stain is being brought in, or has been brought in: just as if he were persuaded that all the others are asleep, and that care must be taken that whatever is wont to lead the common people away from studies of this kind be altogether removed. Of this matter there will be a more convenient place of speaking a little below.

But since it would be of no great fruit if some private man noticed that something of error had been brought in, unless he opened it and made it public, there must certainly be some method by which this may conveniently be done. And indeed no fitter method can be made than that which the Apostle sets before the Corinthians. Let two or three prophets speak (he says), and [Marginal note: 1 Cor. 14.] let the others judge; but if something has been revealed to another who is sitting by, let the first be silent. For you can all prophesy one by one, so that all may learn and all may be exhorted. If any one man speaks continually in the church, and no one ever contradicts him, it will be very much a wonder if that one man is not puffed up, if he does not slip into such a persuasion about himself that he alone seems to himself to be everything, alone to have understanding, alone to be wise, and the rest to be like brute animals, which ought to depend on him, so that they only learn.

But if anyone should hope that he too can teach something, it ought to be thought a sacrilege. But what does the Apostle say to these things? [Marginal note: Ibid.] Did the word of God proceed from you? Or did it come to you alone? If anyone seems to be a prophet or spiritual, let him acknowledge that the things I write to you are the Lord's commandments. But if anyone is ignorant, let him be ignorant. Therefore, brethren, strive to this end, that you prophesy, and do not forbid speaking in tongues; let all things be done decently and in order. That this custom, and this practice of the Lord's commandment, is not restored in the churches is greatly to be lamented. But someone will say: there is so great rashness in this age, so great boldness, so great impudence, that if it were free for anyone to speak in the church, there would be no end of contentions and quarrels. Why so? Is man perhaps something else now than he once was? Yes, you say. For the human race has degenerated day by day to such a degree that it now seems to have reached the utmost. Is it really so? But grant that it is so: you who are the instructor of the people, are you not yourself also of the same clay? Were you not born in the same age? Moreover, since this institution chiefly aims at this, that pastors be kept within the bounds of modesty, that they understand that they are not the authors of the divine discourse and that they have not alone obtained the Spirit: the more the human race has degenerated, the more necessary it is, for the greater the rashness, arrogance, and pride there is today, that belongs as much to those who are in charge as to the rest of the people.

Are you a prophet? (for let us keep to this word) Or is there something of the Spirit in you? If there is not, it so far does not befit you to speak alone in the church that you are almost alone worthy to be silent. But if you are a prophet, if the Spirit is in you, attend to what the Apostle says to you: Acknowledge, he says, that the things I write are the Lord's commandments. Come then: on one side stands the judgment of the Lord, who wills that prophesying (for this word too we must use) be common to all, and that not to the destruction of the Church but to its salvation; on the other side stands yours, of a man who fears lest it produce contentions and confusion. By whose judgment, then, will it be more fitting to stand? If you think by yours, see what you take upon yourself, and what place you leave for your modesty. To be sure, the Lord did not understand what sort of animal man is; he needed, forsooth, your wisdom, to warn him of the danger; or it did not occur to him that the human race would be corrupted beyond measure, so that liberty of this kind would not be useful. But Paul answers you that God is not the author of contention, but of peace [Marginal note: Ibid.]: who, although he understands most perfectly what can sow contentions and what can produce peace, and yet does not love or will contentions, but peace, wills that this liberty of prophesying be in the Church.

What will you bring forward against this? What will you object to God himself? Will you accuse him of imprudence? With the tongue indeed no one will be so wicked as to dare it. But if you examine your own heart more diligently, you will doubtless find in it some feeling of this kind, I know not how, ready to go to law with God himself: which is not to be listened to, but to be sharply repressed, and altogether subjected to the Spirit. That indeed seems absurd, that after some most learned man has, as it happens, spoken, there should be a place for some despised person to contradict him. Or would he undertake such a thing without the greatest rashness? If I must measure the matter by human judgment, I indeed cannot deny it. But if we are seriously persuaded that the knowledge of divine things ought to be received not from our own vigils, not from our studies, not from our talents, but from God and his Spirit, and that he can in a moment give it even to the most unapt man of all, and indeed with no greater labour or difficulty than to him who has worn away the years of Nestor in studies: what reason is there why I should think this man, if he rises up to contradict, acts rashly? Or could the Spirit not have revealed something to him which he had hidden from you?

And if he has revealed it, and has revealed it to this end, that he should contradict, that through him that matter should become public to the Church: shall I say that he who obeys the Holy Spirit acts rashly? But if it seems otherwise to you, surely you are not persuaded that the Spirit is the author and teacher of this knowledge, but you think that all that praise is owed to studies, vigils, and talent. And if you think so, I say to you again: you are worthy not to speak alone, but rather to be silent alone. And so that you may understand the more that there ought to be a place of speaking even for the most unlearned man of all, God wills that he himself be acknowledged as the author of his own gifts: he does not will that his praise be ascribed to our studies or to our talents, but to himself. But if a man who has spent his whole life in studies speaks wisely, that is ascribed not to God but to studies: in word perhaps, but yet with our judgment vehemently resisting it: this, I say, God will not suffer. But if you hear a wise word from the mouth of some unlearned man, willing or unwilling, you must acknowledge God as its author. So too, when God was about to give victory to Israel against the Midianites, with Gideon as leader, and Gideon had gathered thirty-two thousand men, [Marginal note: Judg. 7.] so that Israel should not glory that it had gained the victory by its own strength, not by God's favour (which might have seemed so, if it had joined battle with so numerous an army), he willed there to be not more than three hundred, so that it might appear that he himself had been the author of the victory, not either the number of the fighters or their strength.

But with the glory of God there is joined the greatest benefit to the Church: for if the people see now this man, now that, endowed with the spirit of prophesying, beyond all expectation, many will be lifted up by such examples into the hope of obtaining the same gift, if they ask for it; many will be kindled to the reading of the divine writings; many will learn and make progress, and so it will come about that, when there is need of an election of a minister of the word, the Church does not need to summon foreigners, unknown to itself, but has men of its own, whose life is known and whose character has been proved, whom it may choose. And when the number of those who were made fit for prophesying was great, it would not be necessary to use as pastors those who, already from boyhood, set such offices before themselves as the reward of their studies, and betake themselves to the study of divine things no otherwise than they would have done to any other trade whatever from which they might seek their living: so that you could scarcely hope that very many of them would turn out to be any other pastors than hirelings. But that this custom of common prophesying existed in the old Church of the Jews can be gathered from this, that it is recorded in writing that the Lord, at Nazareth, on the day which is called the Sabbath, having come, according to his custom, into the synagogue, took the book and expounded the passage of Isaiah, [Marginal note: Luke 4.] and that, twelve years old, he sat at Jerusalem in the temple among the teachers and disputed with them.

For he could not do this by reason of any ordinary office, since neither did his age allow it, nor was it clear to those teachers who he was. Rather, the Lord must in this have used the power permitted to everyone. In the churches of the Christians indeed it lasted at least until the times of Constantine. For so Eusebius has left it written on this matter: [Marginal note: Lib. 9, Eccl. Hist.] If anyone, by the grace of God's inspiration, delivered an address to the people, all listened to him in great silence, with eyes fixed on him, as if he were about to announce something from heaven. So great was the reverence of the hearers, and so great was the order that was discerned among the priests: then another. Nor was it only two or three, as the Apostle said, but whoever had something given them to say. So that that which Moses had wished came about the more among them: Who will give it that all the people prophesy? For there was no envy; no one burned with jealousy; the gifts of God were dispensed; each one, as it is written, worked so as to abound for the building up of the Church; and all these things were done with love in such a way that they vied with one another in honouring each other, and each preferred another to himself.

But although this common prophesying can be useful to the Church, careful attention must be paid to what the Apostle warns us: for this is certain, that man's pride is so great that whatever has once slipped from him he wishes to be altogether ratified, and cannot bear that it be broken by anyone. Therefore, if judging could belong to him who has spoken first, it would be a wonder if in a whole lifetime it ever happened that a man was seen who yields to one who contradicts him. What then does Paul say here? Let two or three prophets speak (he says), [Marginal note: 1 Cor. 14.] and let the others judge. He does not wish, therefore, the same men to be parties and judges. And he adds a little after: And the spirit of the prophets is subject to the prophets, for God is not of dissension, but of peace. Therefore, when each has spoken his own opinion, he ought to acquiesce in the judgment of the others and not contend obstinately; [Marginal note: But let the lust of contending be absent; one must act with bashfulness and modesty.] if this is not done, it is certain there will be no end of contentions. But what if someone refuses to acquiesce in the judgment of those sitting by? I would say indeed that, having been gravely admonished not to disturb the Church, and not to act against the commandment of the Apostle, or rather of the Lord, who commands that the spirits of the prophets be subject to the prophets, he is to be cast out of the assembly, even if he held the chief place in the Church. The people must also often be warned that the Apostle does not give power of speaking in the Church to anyone in such a way that, as in a public square, whatever comes into his mouth is allowed; but that, when he gives power of speaking to one to whom something has been revealed, he wills that all rashness and impudence be absent.

He who has not feared God's Church should know that he despises God's Spirit, who presides over it, and will not do so with impunity. Before he brings anything forward into the midst, let him think what it is; let him think again and again with himself how certain a revelation of it he has; and whatever it may in the end be, let him remember bashfulness and modesty, without which certainly nothing can be done. But here it must be seen with attentive minds both how far each man ought to acquiesce in the judgment of those who sit by, and who can deservedly be held a disturber of the Church. I think indeed that one must yield so far that, after I have brought forward what I had in support of my opinion, if my opinion is still less approved by those sitting by, I cease to defend it and stop being troublesome to the Church about that matter; but that I should also confess that I had erred, and ask pardon for any fault, when I do not yet understand that I have erred, I ought not to be compelled, since if I obeyed I would do it with my conscience resisting, and so would offend against God. He, therefore, will be a disturber of the Church who refuses to abide by the judgment of others so far as has been said, and goes on being troublesome; but most of all he who would require of another what he ought not: namely, that he recant when he is not persuaded.

But for the most part those are held to be disturbers who refuse to approve by their own vote whatever, in whatever way, has fallen from the pastors. Again, it could be asked here, not absurdly, whether, after some matter has been dealt with once or twice, if someone, the Church's judgment being now known, should wish to call the same matter back again into controversy, he ought to be heard: or rather ought to be ordered to be silent, and to hold the matter as already judged. But there will be another, more convenient place for discussing this. What remains, therefore, is that we must strive from God by assiduous prayers, that he give us to use this so wholesome liberty, and to obtain from it a fruit as full as possible. And so that this may happen, that he tame and break our spirits by his own Spirit, and make them gentle: and that he not allow what he himself instituted for the confirming and establishing of his Church to be turned, by our obstinacy and the perversity of our minds, to its harm and ruin. But it has often happened that, if something rather grave came up for judgment, not one single particular church alone would judge, but a council either of the province, or even of the whole Christian world, would be summoned. Someone could ask whether this ought to be done, and what authority councils ought to have. That the custom of summoning councils should be retained, the Apostles' example seems to me to teach plainly enough.

For if it was expedient for men endowed with so great a Spirit to confer among themselves about controversies of religion: much more does it seem that this ought to be done where there is not so much of the Spirit: nor indeed is the Lord's promise to be made little of, who has promised that, wherever two or three come together in his name, he will be present: but about authority the question is more difficult. And since my business now is not with the Papists, but with those who want the evangelical truth restored: it is not necessary to demonstrate in many words that no council has the right either to make new laws, or to institute new kinds of worship. But what shall we say about the controversies of religion which arise over the interpretation of the holy Scriptures? Does a council have the right, at least about those, to determine what is to be held? And if it cannot do at least this, is there any other use left for councils? And is there any difference between the councils of the Apostles and those of their successors? If, as certainly as it is certain that the Lord will stand forever by his promises, it were also certain that those who come together always direct their thoughts to God's glory, and hold their hope of knowing the truth placed in the Lord's promises alone, and in his Spirit: there would be no reason why all the judgments of councils should not be held firm and ratified.

But since these things are the secrets of minds and hearts: to know them belongs to God alone. And since those things lie hidden which are joined to the Lord's promise, conscience has here nothing certain on which it may lean. Show me that bishops, or any others whatever, have come together in Christ's name: I will gladly acquiesce, but that they have done so — by what means will you show me that? Because they testify it themselves? As if indeed ungodly pretenders would not testify the same about themselves. But it is in no way likely that God imposed such a law on us: a law which would at some time, whether we wished to obey it or wished not, altogether subject us to guilt. But the law imposed on us would be of that kind: if, when we ought to obey a council, either a council could err, or, if it did err and we obeyed, our obedience would have no excuse to hold with God. And yet that councils can err we do not doubt: indeed, nothing is more certain than that they have very often erred. But if we have obeyed the ungodly judgment of some council, we are certain by no testimony of God's word that we shall be excused: indeed, we are gravely warned not to give faith even to a heavenly angel, if he should wish to teach anything beyond what has been handed down by the Apostles.

For the divine writings are set before us, from which we may know God's will: and so that we may rightly understand them, we are taught to ask wisdom and the Spirit from God; but that we should acquiesce in councils, there is, so far as I know, no word. But if our obedience toward a council will have no excuse, if it has decreed something wrongly: and it is not certain that councils never err: and it must not be said that God has subjected us to such a law that at some time we could neither obey it nor not obey it without guilt: it follows that no one is to be compelled to acquiesce in the judgments of councils. And since it is the same thing, whether you say that a council has the right to judge about controversies of religion, or you say that everyone ought to obey whatever a council has decreed; and we have taught that no one is commanded by any law to obey: it follows that a council does not have such a right. What, moreover, shall we say about the man who has been called to the office of teaching? Must he altogether obey a council: so that he teaches in no other way than as has been determined by the council? It would indeed be absurd that anyone should have to teach others a thing which he himself cannot safely believe. Or if, in teaching, he has followed a council's ungodly judgment, will he be more excused than if he himself were about to follow it by giving faith to it?

No, indeed, much less will he be excused. For Paul wills that a man who has taught anything beyond what has been handed down by the Apostles be accursed. Or is such a man not held, more than one who only believes a council and does not teach, in such a snare that, whether he obeys or does not, he will be at fault? Or will he rather give himself up to silence? "Woe to me" (says Paul) "if I do not teach the Gospel," and Peter: "It is necessary to obey God rather than men." It is therefore plain that a council does not have the right so to judge about controversies that its decrees must be obeyed. And since councils are indeed to be summoned, but not for this end: there must be some other end for whose sake they are summoned. But if councils have come together so that, the controversies which trouble the Church having been set out, and free power of speaking having been given to everyone, those to whom it has pleased the Lord to disclose something which bears on the matter speak, and say what they think: let others then judge: not so that, opinions having been collected, the greater number should make a law which imposes on anyone a necessity of assenting (for in that way the greater part would for the most part overcome the better), but so that each should weigh what has been said, so that, if he saw it proved by suitable testimonies of the divine writings, he should both embrace it himself, and try to persuade others of it, not by a council's authority, but by those testimonies of God's word which he had learned in the council.

Certainly, if those who come together had this aim set before them, even if among two hundred or three hundred hypocrites there were only three or four truly godly men, who had God's glory at heart, and who trusted in the Lord's promises, and asked that the truth be disclosed to them, those so few would not have come together in vain, and that so great a multitude of hypocrites would certainly not make the Lord's promises void: what had been disclosed to one man, all who were teachable, whoever they were, would embrace, and so Satan would in vain place his hope of victory in the number of the unskilled and of his own pretenders, or in the outward dignity of persons. There would not be so great a labour in buying up votes, and in filling up the number which could win: but rather that those foundations of the truth might be found on which everyone saw that he could safely rest. We should certainly see, not seldom, God, even through the most despised man, scatter great clouds of errors. But as long as the first and chief parts are given to number, and to the splendour of outward dignity, as long as the agreement of the greater part stands in the place of a law, nothing is more certain than that it will come about that Satan will for the most part win.

In what way, then, will anyone be able (you will say) to be deprived of the communion of the godly on account of some ungodly teaching? Or will the authority of some particular church be greater than that of the universal Church, so that the former can condemn both a teaching and a man, and the latter cannot? I answer: Any church, when it condemns someone, if it does not do so justly, does indeed separate him from itself to this extent, that it does not acknowledge him as its own: but it does not separate him from Christ: nor, because he does not give faith to it, does it subject him to any guilt with God: as to whether it justly rejects him, let it see to that itself. But when it justly rejects him, it does not justly reject him for this reason, that he does not embrace its teachings, or does not acquiesce in its judgments: but because he spurns the doctrine handed down from God, and indeed that doctrine which is necessary to be known for salvation. When also any church approves this teaching and disapproves that, it does not impose on anyone a law of believing which God altogether holds ratified, but only declares its own faith: so that one who does not think the same, at least in the chief heads of doctrine, may understand that he does not belong to that assembly — for there must be some order, so that the assembly is not compelled to have the rights of sacred things in common with a man whom it is persuaded has nothing in common with God's Church.

And from these things indeed, what difference there is between the councils of the Apostles and those of the men who make themselves their successors, whoever they at last may be, can both be easily understood, and be explained in one word. Since it was established that the Apostles had been chosen by name by the Lord himself for the apostolic office, it was established that they had been endowed with a great power of the Spirit, which Spirit was going to lead them into all truth: but about any successors no such testimony is extant: but only a conjecture is to be taken, from both their words and their deeds, in which there can be pretence, as to who they are and of what kind they are: in the Apostles' councils, what was to be looked at was not so much what had been judged, as through whom it had been judged: so that they did not need to give an account in many words as to why they decreed something. On the contrary, in the successors' councils it does not matter so very much who those are who have decreed something: but this is much more to be seen, what and of what kind is that which they have decreed, what testimonies are brought forward from the divine writings, and how openly, how clearly those testimonies teach that which is asserted. So what Luther taught, that we shall be saved by Christ's benefit alone, that we now hold ourselves persuaded of: yet we do not look at Luther here, but at the testimonies from the divine writings which he cites, which the Spirit himself, the inward interpreter, also testifies to our minds to mean.

Otherwise, let them be very many, let them be godly, let them be learned, let them be holy, indeed let them even be heavenly angels: unless you have understood what they assert to be proved by testimonies of God's word, there is no reason why the splendour of a council should dazzle your eyes. Yet it is fair that such authority be given to a council (unless it is plainly a council of the enemies of the truth) that its opinion should not be rashly rejected: but that everyone should weigh it diligently, having implored divine help for judging rightly. From this also one may gather: who ought to have entrance into a council, and who a place in it. For since they do not come together to make any laws of faith, but rather to ask from God the Spirit through whom they may be led into the way of truth, and, to say it in one word, to learn: it will not be necessary for a man to be a Bishop, or an Archbishop, or the Roman Pontiff, nor indeed of any illustrious name for learning; indeed, in whomsoever anything of godliness and of the Spirit is seen to be, there will be no cause why he should be excluded. So in the decree of the Apostolic council one may see the whole Church named after the Apostles and the elders.

For since the Holy Spirit is not bound to any outward dignity, or to a certain kind of persons: who knows through whom God will most especially disclose the truth? And since no number of votes is to be looked at, but only what is said: even if perhaps some unfit man, or a man with pretended godliness, has been admitted, he will not be able to do much harm: provided, indeed, that he is held to be such as his own speech has shown him to be. And indeed about doctrine, by what means it is corrupted, and what precautions are to be used for preserving it when it is pure and sincere, we have spoken: it follows that we should next speak of how the discipline of morals is loosened, and how care is to be taken that this should happen the less.
