*The Stratagems of Satan. Book Six.*

This is established, that the restoring of Christ's kingdom is placed in doctrine — that is, in the restitution of evangelical truth: for it both shows what God wills us to do, and partly allures by promises, partly drives by threatenings, to obey God. Where that is done, there Christ must reign, and Satan be exiled thence. Wherefore it is fitting that all the defences of Satan's kingdom should tend to this, that every approach should be shut against evangelical truth. Therefore what things can avail toward such a design must be investigated. [Marginal note: A modest liberty of opinions concerning religion is especially opposed to Satan's kingdom.] And this indeed occurs first: that if it be left free to everyone to practise and to defend religion just as he most wishes, since there is a very great diversity of minds and of judgments, it will come about that there is also a very great diversity of opinions and of sentiments: and that no one has not someone who dissents from him and contradicts him: and since almost no one is not contradicted, it must needs be that many come into doubt what they should chiefly approve and follow.

But he who doubts is stirred up to the inquiry after truth, and, with many inquiring, it would be a wonder if someone did not find it. And when truth has been found, if there is liberty of disputing, when sentiments have been compared together, it must needs come out superior. From which indeed it is brought about that Satan's kingdom cannot long stand together with liberty of opinions concerning religion. But if that liberty does not suit it, it follows that some definite law suits it, which may restrain and take away that liberty. And since the whole name of religion cannot be altogether taken away, and all easily understand that there cannot be more than one true religion: what other law of that kind will there be, than one by which some definite religion — but indeed that one above all impious and detestable — is commanded to be practised? And whoever shall have dared even to mutter anything against it, on him let all the most atrocious punishments be appointed? But whatever law is to be promulgated, there is need of some occasion: and what things are wont to furnish an occasion for taking away liberty of opinions concerning religion is not at all very difficult to know. For they are without doubt these: a too great multitude of dissenting opinions, contentions, suits, quarrels: which indeed Satan sows not only then at last, when doctrine has already been corrupted, but already from that time when it is whole and uncorrupted, for contentions and quarrels are a certain way and means toward corrupting it and shaking it.

And although those institutions cannot please Satan which do not suffer doctrine, when it is uncorrupted, to be corrupted, yet those certainly do please him which, although they were devised for that very end, nevertheless rather confirm and propagate errors, and furnish occasions to sects, than hinder any of these things. But what sort of things those are has already been shown above. And since the more there are who rise up against the received doctrine, and the more rash, insolent and peevish they are, and the more absurd are the things they bring forward, and the more authority those who preside over the churches have, and the more liable they are to wrath and indignation, the more ungovernably they seem likely to attempt all things against the disturbers of the public quiet: what is decreed must be looked to again and again, lest, while you think you are most beautifully resisting Satan, beyond all hope you be rather doing Satan's own business — about which matter we have discoursed in many words in the third Book. But since man is born to a desire of knowing, and is strong in judgment to some extent, by which he can not seldom discern what is true: and since both the desire of knowing and judgment can both be strengthened and be slackened, it belongs without doubt to Satan's interest that these should have as little strength as possible: and since inquiry proceeds from the desire of knowing, and inquiry is the way of arriving at a knowledge of the truth: partly indeed it does not profit Satan (as has been said) to allow liberty of inquiring in his kingdom; partly also it seems that, if it be taken away, it will be hurtful to him, for generally (as he says) "We ever strive after what is forbidden, and desire what is denied."

But if the desire has been great and intense, we cannot rest until, the hindrances being removed, we have satisfied it in every way by whatever means. Wherefore if, liberty of inquiring being taken away, the desire of knowing is strengthened, there is need of some other means by which a desire of that kind may be restrained: or even, if it can be done, taken away altogether. But every desire is placed in thought. For no one can wish for anything except by thinking. [Marginal note: He is busy to take away every desire of inquiring after truth.] Therefore if Satan could bring it about that we do not even think about the truth of religion as a thing to be known, he would in truth have attained that we do not wish for it. And since the desire of anything consists of three parts, as it were, of thought — of which the first is that by which we think that thing to be good; the second, that by which we think it to be absent from us; the third is that by which we think it can come about that we attain it: if one of these is wanting, the desire itself cannot stand. Much less indeed if two, or all, are wanting.

Come then: if anyone has been persuaded that whatever he does from a desire of pleasing God, at the exhortation of those whose part it seems to be to instruct the people toward godliness, God will take it in good part: for if he has been wrongly taught, those who taught will pay the penalties, not he: to this man indeed it would seem to matter not much whether he looks upon the truth or not. And since, if with any thing, even if otherwise good and to be desired, some evil seems to be joined: in proportion as that evil shall be great, by so much is that thing of less account: so that, if it seems to have more evil than good, he will hold it not among good things but among evil ones: if anyone first thinks it matters little whether he has a knowledge of the truth in the affairs of religion or not, and then at the same time has been persuaded that he cannot obtain that knowledge except by employing the greatest and most laborious studies, and great expenses also (which indeed hold the place of an evil), that thing indeed will seem to him not good to seek, but evil. One who has also been persuaded that he can in no way attain that knowledge — whether because he cannot bestow upon it those studies which it seems to require, or because there is wanting to him either such judgment as seems to be needed, or sharpness of mind, or something else — will be held by no great desire of inquiring after it.

One, finally, who holds for certain that the received doctrine is true, and whatever is at variance with it false, will think that a knowledge of the truth in religion is not wanting to him: and so he will not think it is to be inquired after. Since these persuasions seem to have the greatest force toward quenching the zeal for inquiring after truth, it will be worth the labour to know whence they arise and by what things they are confirmed.

It is likely that, when the people see learned men, and men of great name, not thinking the same things about various points of religion, but that there are great suits and contentions among them, they slip into a thought of this kind: that they judge the doctrine of religion to be a thing exceedingly obscure, hard to know, and uncertain, and that there is no end of inquiring after its truth: since neither a knowledge of the Latin tongue, nor of the Greek, nor of the Hebrew, nor any other learning, nor the writings of so many expositors who had believed they could illustrate the divine letters, nor, finally, anything else, can avail so much toward a knowledge of those things that at last all obscurity and ambiguity should be taken away, and at the same time all suits should cease.

Which perplexity certain other things also increase: as that, if, when an infinite number of books written in explanation of the sacred writers is seen, learned men disputing about religion have been accustomed to cite the sentiments of those who have written on that science, and to use them as certain witnesses, so to speak. For it must needs be that an opinion of this kind is begotten from this, that no one seems able to have anything ascertained unless he has turned over innumerable books, and holds well what, on every matter, has been the sentiment of the most excellent writers. But it is wonderful how much some men have written: for Origen alone is said to have left six thousand books. So many of Augustine's still remain that one may doubt whether a man's lifetime would suffice for reading through those alone: Jerome, Chrysostom and others also left many: and our own age is so fruitful that it seems not so much to envy the Fathers the palm in that kind as already to have snatched it from them. [Marginal note: Especially that the people should cast off onto the pastors the care and reading of the sacred things.] But most men, when they despair of being able to turn over so many volumes, and many also have not wherewith to buy them for themselves, and when they think they can profit nothing without a diligent reading of them, what is left but that they abandon all study of divine things, all hope of profiting being taken away?

And since for that reason they cannot cast away all hope of salvation, they must needs slip into this thought: that they judge it is not for just anyone to have ascertained what he is to believe, but that it is enough if he obeys those who have the care of religion: at whose risk it will be, if there has been any sin anywhere; that it is theirs to see what they teach the people. Nay rather, since this way seems most expeditious, and far from all trouble and at the same time from danger, those seem to act foolishly who by laborious and very troublesome studies, as if on purpose, seek something in which to be mad. Which persuasion is also confirmed the more from this: that when all busy themselves with sacred reading, since not all can be endowed with the same spirit or the same sharpness, the same thing cannot indeed seem so to all men about all things. Nor indeed, just because someone is of little strength in judgment, will he forthwith acknowledge that he sees little, so as to yield to one who thinks better. Nay rather, the less strength each man has in natural ability, the more he pleases himself: and since that happens to very many, and very often, many controversies must needs arise, not among the learned only, but also among any men whatsoever; on whose account, when the ecclesiastical pastors have many troubles to undergo, and those things befall which we have said are generally joined with controversies — wraths, that is, indignations, reproaches, evil-speakings — the pastors are driven to seek remedies by which they may stop the mouth of all of them.

From which it comes about that, when afterwards it is not left free to defend whatever you will, they slip at last (so that they may have some excuse, namely, for their ignorance) into judging that, just as it belongs to the most learned men alone — those, namely, who preside over the churches — to decree what is to be held, so the study of those things belongs to them alone. To which error the pastors so often do not oppose themselves that they even give it their support in the highest degree, when, overcome by those troubles which they must otherwise take from frequent controversies, they beat back the arrogance of the somewhat bold too bitterly, and command them with anger either to sew shoes, or to weave cloth, or to give themselves to hunting, and to leave questions about religion to those whose whole life is with books. By which rebukes, when some are marked with some disgrace, or are even affected with some other graver disadvantage, the rest learn from their example that it does not profit to be involved in troubles of that kind. And so they prefer to abstain from all reading of the sacred letters; nay, they even persuade their own people to abstain from it, from which it must needs follow shortly after that there is ignorance of all things, and with the ignorance a firm and stubborn purpose of persevering in it.

What then would you have done here? you will say. Certainly indeed, since the Holy Spirit in so many places exhorts all equally to the reading of the divine writings, whatever is of such a kind as to lead away and dissuade any class of men from it must needs be from Satan, not from God, and therefore is to be avoided. Wherefore if the excessive rashness of some is to be beaten back — because those who understand nothing wish to act the doctors, and despise all men in comparison with themselves — it must be looked to again and again what they do amiss and what you rebuke; for the rebuke ought to be not headlong or wrathful, but prudent. But a man does not sin, not even if he is a porter, in that he reads the sacred writings, in that he inquires after the sure foundations of his own salvation, in that he confers with friends, whether his equals or even those more learned than himself: nay rather, he deserves the greatest praise from this, and all are to be exhorted to do the same; but neither indeed will he have sinned if, the truth not yet being known, he has been unwilling to approve the better sentiment, not even if a man of great name bears witness to its truth: seeing that what is set before him to believe is the word of God, not the celebrated name of a man; nay rather, demonstrations of the truth so convenient and so open will have to be sought out from God's word that he cannot fail to see the matter; he will have to be exhorted to seek the light of truth from God, and your own prayers will have to be joined together with his; and if he bears himself in these matters with too little reverence, if rashly, if insolently, he is to be rebuked on that ground alone, but yet with such caution and moderation that first you separate his sins from the baseness of his person and his kind of life, and then that you do not, by too great strictness of rebuke, make deserters of the divine study both of himself and of his equals; nay rather, if the people seem to set no very great store by these studies, they will have to be inflamed toward them by every means.

And since there are altogether two disadvantages, both of which you can scarcely — indeed, scarcely at all — avoid, great care must be taken lest you choose to turn away from that which is by infinite parts the lesser. For certainly, if the studies of the divine letters flourish among the people, suits and controversies cannot fail to be present — for, to pass over the diversity of minds, the unequal measure of spiritual gifts, and that fault of nature by which it comes about that his own things please each man more than is right — Satan in truth will not be able to rest without striving by his emissaries to disturb all things, and such suits cannot but bring great troubles to the pastors.

And this indeed is one disadvantage, and that one in truth great; the other is that, if the people have abandoned these studies, and the ecclesiastical pastors alone have claimed them for themselves, those few being corrupted by Satan's arts, a corruption of all things will shortly follow. This choice, therefore, is given to godly pastors: whether they prefer to endure whatever that trouble is, so that a knowledge of God and of his law may remain among God's people; or, so that they may consult their own tranquillity and live blessedly and pleasantly, to open a window to mist and darkness, to abandon the care of the church, and to give over all things to Satan's dominion. Although indeed this desolation will not follow immediately (while you, that is, are alive), certainly it cannot be far away. But so that, on account of the controversies which there are among all the most learned men about religion, the Gospel should not seem so obscure that there is no hope of understanding it rightly: first indeed pains must be taken that the controversies either be put to sleep, or be handled with as little noise as can be, about which matter we have discoursed above in many words. Next also, since altogether (as we have said) it is necessary that there be controversies, the people must often be warned about this necessity, and that no conjecture of obscurity or of uncertainty of doctrine is to be taken from this, that there is no agreement about it among the most learned men: that it had already been foretold by the Son of God that it had pleased the heavenly Father to hide his secrets from the wise, but to reveal them to little ones. [Marginal note: Matth. 11.] What wonder, then, if we see that come to pass which the Son of God himself bore witness of? Or ought not hope rather to grow from this for the simple, if they have surrendered themselves to God and have implored his Spirit, that those things will be revealed to them which the wise can attain by no studies, helped by no aids of the human arts? Nay rather, that those wise men cannot perceive them for this reason: because they ascribe more than enough to aids of that kind? Which, since God cannot bear it — seeing that he wills all wisdom to be ascribed to himself as accepted from him, not to anything else — he deprives them of all power of seeing, so that they can plainly see nothing. Or were not unlearned men for the greatest part those who followed Christ when he was among men? But learned men, such as the Pharisees were, persecuted him, and were his greatest enemies? Let us wonder, then, that those do not see, and therefore fight in darkness after the manner of blindfold gladiators, who have been deprived of their eyes.

[Marginal note: The custom of citing writings or authors is to be disapproved, and is dangerous.] But what shall we say about the custom of citing the authorities of writers? Certainly, where it has prevailed, men must needs think that what is to be held about any matter cannot otherwise be ascertained than by having sifted the sentiments of all the better writers; as to those who have been entangled in that error (unless, all things being laid aside, they have given themselves wholly to the reading of them — who will be very few) I do not see what can be hoped, except that they abandon all reading, and come into that opinion, that they judge it belongs to none to search into divine things except the ecclesiastical pastors. Wherefore even if this custom had no other disadvantage, yet on this ground alone it would seem most to be detested. But there are not wanting also other causes, and those indeed the gravest, on account of which it is to be disapproved. And first indeed, the honour which was to be paid to God's written word — I say that by this custom it is transferred almost wholly to the expositors; for what each man ought to have been persuaded of, because the Spirit of God had left it so written, he is now persuaded of because they so interpret it; for although he holds that what is written ought to be true, yet he does not believe what is written for this reason, that it is so written: for he distrusts that he understands it himself; he believes it, rather, because those so expound it whose authority he thinks he must acquiesce in, so much so that, if the exposition is contrary to what is written, he will follow the interpreters, not the written sentiment of the Holy Spirit.

Who then here does not see that more faith is given to the expositors than to the divine writings? Or at least certainly just as much? For the same thing happens: as if envoys had come from somewhere to a king, whose tongue he does not understand, so that he must use an interpreter: for if he believes what is said, he assuredly believes it not because the envoys so report it, whom he does not understand, but because the interpreter testifies that they so report it, so much so that, if the interpreter has expounded it wrongly, he will give faith to the interpreter, not to the envoys: so that it may easily appear that the king's credulity rests on the interpreter's faith, not on the envoys' words, and since the authority of writers is not a firm and stable thing — inasmuch as some are in greater esteem at one time than at another, as not so long ago the Summa of Thomas Aquinas was of authority with all men, whereas with us it is none — it will come about that, if men of little sincere doctrine have obtained authority, that doctrine may by their testimony be believed as divinely delivered which perhaps stands directly opposed to the divine, a thing which we see has happened in the Roman church. Besides, since the Son of God testifies that the Father willed to reveal his secrets not to the wise but to little children, it must needs be that even the things that are written were written to little children, not to the wise: but by that custom it will come about that only the wise seem able to understand them: those, namely, who by the Lord's testimony alone cannot understand them — not that all the writers are of the number of those wise men of whom the Lord speaks — but yet, however you take the matter, there cannot but be bred from it an opinion contrary to that which little children ought to have from the Lord's words.

Besides, as it is to be wished that no strife or controversy should arise, so it is to be wished that, if any arise, the way to settle them should be as ready as possible; and yet, if the opinions of expositors must be brought forward — since they themselves may seem to set down things conflicting much more than the Spirit of God, and to speak ambiguously and obscurely, so that some places of the expositors themselves must be explained by others — who does not see that the way of ending dissensions is made longer by infinite parts than if the divine testimonies alone were cited? For here at last there is some end both of citing places and of examining them, but there there is absolutely no end; or do we not see that sometimes a very great volume has been written on a single question, and yet all the handles for cavilling have not been taken away, so that rather the widest field for cavilling seems to have been given?

Luther, and others, the earlier champions of evangelical truth, had restored that most beautiful and most necessary custom of seeking testimonies from the word of God alone: but certain others, thinking they would do most beautifully if they pierced their enemies with their own weapons, returned to this, that they filled everything anew with the authorities of the Fathers — which would to God they had done with as prosperous success as the good hope with which they undertook it. Certainly, since before, their adversaries were driven into a very narrow place, so that they no longer had anywhere to turn themselves, by this means they have obtained a very ample space for exercising the arts of cavilling, of shuffling, and of eluding; I for my part judge this custom to be most pernicious, and altogether to be fled, and that ears are to be accustomed to acknowledge nothing, to which faith is to be given, except the oracles of God alone, comprised in the sacred books. But since the variety of talents is very great, and the natural curiosity of some men is by far the greatest — men who think there is nothing of which they have not a certain account — let them suffer themselves to be persuaded that their curiosity of this kind contributes nothing to such men toward the finding of the truth: there is need of some provision for Satan's kingdom, to blunt in them all force of talent and of judgment.

Truly, however, a man's judgment then indeed chiefly has its own strength, when his age is mature: and when it has itself been accustomed to right meditations and thoughts, contaminated by no errors before it came to its own maturity, and free from every emotion of the mind; but, on the contrary, it has no strength in boyhood, nor even in the following ages, if it has been accustomed to depraved thoughts: if it has been preoccupied by errors, and when it is subject to the emotions of the mind. [Marginal note: By what arts Satan strengthens errors in the minds of the common people.] If therefore any religion has been established and settled in a people, and that a false one, when children drink it in as it were with their nurse's milk, at which time there is absolutely no judgment: and afterwards hear their parents, their households, their neighbours, in short all men, speaking of it as with one mouth: when that opinion is imprinted on tender minds, that if anyone has believed otherwise he is most certainly to be tormented with the everlasting fires of hell: such juggling illusions do not suffer judgment to grow, but oppress it and utterly destroy it, so that it can behold no light. And so it can neither search out the truth by itself, nor bear it if anyone wishes to reveal it to it. But besides that habit of so believing, which has now become, as it were, a kind of second nature, there also comes in this, by far indeed the greatest hindrance to the knowledge of the truth: that our ancestors seem to have believed so, whom all men most unwillingly suffer themselves to be persuaded to have been in error.

And these things indeed are of such a kind that they cannot be guarded against except by the preservation of doctrine; only something may be of profit for that which has been said about our ancestors, if this be often inculcated: that this must perpetually be observed, that we look into what God's word teaches us, not what our ancestors believed, except perhaps for the sake of inquiry; which when we do — when we have found what they themselves believed, and in what manner they worshipped God — we must not stop there, but must still inquire further by what testimony of God's word, relying on it, they either so believed or so acted; for perhaps, if this counsel has been most frequently inculcated, some trace of it may be able to remain even when the rest of the doctrine has been depraved, so that someone, warned by it, may be roused to the inquiry after truth; but since depraved exercises of talent also are of very great force for the depraving of judgments, to this indeed belongs that custom of disputing, in which each man aims at this, that he himself should conquer, not that the truth should conquer, only for a display of talent: from disputations of which kind endless strifes are propagated, all the liberal arts are depraved and corrupted, and it comes about at last that the more pains anyone spends in thoroughly learning them, the less he acquires of any solid knowledge; which custom, now springing up anew, must be met at the very first opportunity, for there is hardly any pest greater.

Concerning the emotions of the mind which deprave the judgment, although we have already touched something above: yet in this place, where another thing is proposed, we must treat somewhat of them again; for there the treatment was of those emotions which could lead away from the truth once known: here the disputation is of those emotions which do not suffer a man who has grown up in errors to lend his ears to the truth as it comes to him. It is certain that man so far loves the true and hates the false that he neither wishes the one to be set before him in place of the other, nor wishes either to slip or to be deceived; whereby it comes about that he loves those whom he is persuaded speak true things and teach true things; but those whom he believes to speak or teach false things he shrinks from, and pursues both with so much the greater hatred the more good or evil he judges to be placed in the matter in question; but a greater good than everlasting blessedness, or a greater evil than everlasting torments — matters which are in question when the doctrine of religion is in question — cannot even be formed by any thought; whereby it comes about that whatever doctrine of religion he has embraced as true, he loves it, and also those who teach it and who favour it; but he holds in hatred whatever is opposed to it, and both its teachers and its favourers.

Wherefore a man who indeed has been born amid errors, since he holds them in the place of truth, cannot but love them, and hate the truth which is opposed to them; and although, if no memory even of true doctrine remains, no hatred can be exercised against that thing of which there is no thought, yet such a seed of it already lies hidden in his mind, that as soon as the truth emerges from anywhere, the hatred itself will also emerge. And since the more goods anyone seems to have in a thing, so much the more is it loved: if those dogmas in which a man has been born show such a way of obtaining everlasting life that they do not much draw him away from pleasures, nor indeed require those things which are performed with any great difficulty, trouble or labour — that is, in short, if a religion of such a kind has prevailed as a man, if the power had been given him of fashioning for himself at his own pleasure the religion he most would, would have fashioned not at all unlike it, for he would think it would be approved even by God himself — he will be so much the more inclined to the love of it.

Wherefore it is not at all strange if men suffer themselves to be torn away from the Papist darkness with so great reluctance; for although the Papists require certain things which a man does unwillingly, and forbid the things which he desires to have allowed him — which is after the precepts of the Decalogue, which they still in some sort retain — yet if anyone has offended, they set forth very easy ways of expiating the offence, such as frequent hearing of masses, and the buying of them, certain prayers pronounced up to a number, the redemption of Papist diplomas, by which sins are remitted at a very small price, and other trifles of that kind: but not even here does there seem to be any other way of meeting it than if, the doctrine being not yet corrupted, the people be warned as often as possible that the evangelical law is not such a law as flatters human judgment and sense, but most of all opposes them, requiring those things from which man most of all shrinks: such as these, that he love his enemies, despise his own things, care most of all for other men's, patiently bear reproaches, revilings, and every kind of injury for Christ's sake, and other things of that kind. And since the more vehemently anyone is persuaded that what is objected to him is false, with so much the less judgment can he weigh it, and the more does he shrink from it: that will have had the greatest force for confirming and establishing error, if some heretic of most infamous name is thought to have been the author of that doctrine which is true; for thence it will come about that, if anyone has called that matter into controversy, he will be held guilty of the greatest sacrilege; for the temper of man is generally of such a kind that he thinks it must be regarded not what each man has said, but who has said it; but by that reasoning, indeed, there would be hardly any point of Christian doctrine that would not have to be rejected, for there is hardly any of which some heretic has not been the maintainer.

The people are therefore to be warned as often as possible: that they care not who says anything, but what that is which is said. For even if Satan has asserted what is true, it still remains true; but, on the contrary, if a heavenly angel has asserted what was false, it cannot thereby become true. It also matters very much when men think they hold signs by which they may discern true religion from false, and those signs agree with their errors and are opposed to the truth; for that confirms men's minds in their errors in a wonderful manner. [Marginal note: False and deceptive signs of true or false religion.] But first — what we said long ago — man fashions God for himself, as if he were some man of human temper, who delights in gold, gems, little stones, sumptuous preparations, and who loves tales and trifles, whom great and proud buildings please, who is willing to give up nothing of his own right — as, for example, in forgiving injuries without any compensation — and he dreams many other things of that kind about him besides.

But in this matter he places him even below man: in that, although he does not believe that he is willing to give up anything of his own right, it is a wonder what trifles he dreams that he will take in good part, in return for things of infinite weight, while he thinks that by things of absolutely no value, and emptier than emptiness itself, he can make compensation for his sins. Nor do I contend that men speak thus; but yet if anyone observes their pursuits — what dogmas they readily embrace and hold fast with their teeth, from what they start back and shrink — he will assuredly find that this is their sense; wherefore, the doctrine of religion being depraved, if anything is opposed to imaginations of this kind, they judge that it must forthwith be altogether false. In the second place, they gather the truth of a doctrine from this: if the men of their own nation seem to have approved it for a long time, especially if it has had men distinguished by the name of wisdom — although what nation has not? For whoever sees even a very little among the blind would seem most sharp-sighted; wherefore whatever savours of any novelty, which the truth may produce after errors have been received, must needs seem false: by which reasoning it would have to be confessed, most of all, that of all men those were in the way of truth who worshipped Jupiter, Saturn, and the rest of that crowd of their gods, to whose religion very many nations clung for a very long time.

But they also measure both the truth and the falsity of any religion by the things, whether prosperous or adverse, which befall its worshippers: but yet under this law, that what they wish to hold good against another religion, they will not suffer to hold good against their own. For as soon as hail has brought some harm to men foreign to their religion, or something of that kind has happened, they will have it that that is forthwith the most certain proof of their ungodliness. But if they themselves have suffered the same things, or even much greater, then they will have it either to have been done by chance, or they will confess anything whatsoever rather than that it was a sign of divine wrath on account of any ungodliness. And with the same fairness, indeed, they also draw conjectures from evil morals. For no one wishes his own religion to be brought into suspicion from the crimes of his own people. For the disgrace of another's religion, those offences suffice by which he judges. Lastly, men think that by this sign also they discern a false religion from a true one, if it is taught in secret: nor is that altogether foolishly thought.

For since thefts, adulteries, and many other crimes are committed only in secret, it is no wonder if in a doctrine also which is taught in secret there seems to be some crime; but although this reasoning can hold for some time, it cannot hold always. For just as, when the magistrate favours the true religion, whatever is taught in secret, in corners, or by night, must needs be false and ungodly: so if he is an enemy of the truth, it will be no wonder if what may not be done openly is done in secret and as it were by stealth. But was sacrifice formerly offered in secret to Jupiter, Saturn, Juno, and the other gods of that flour? Was not an account of their sacrifices openly given, unless perhaps religion itself stood in the way? What of the Turks? Do they not today publicly train up their own people in their ungodliness? Are they therefore to be said to teach true things? On the contrary, what of the first Christians, when tyrants were raging against them? Did they not very often give themselves by night and in secret to prayers and to reading? Was their religion therefore to be condemned? In good time, therefore, before the light of truth is withdrawn, the people are to be warned as often as possible: that they not think that by signs devised by themselves, or in any way by the judgment of their own reason, they can rightly judge about any doctrine, either as to its truth or as to its falsity: but that the one rule of judgment is God's oracles, comprised in the sacred writings: that these alone are to us the Lydian stone, by which we may discern gold from brass: [Marginal note: Deut. 6.] that there is absolutely no other true judge; wherefore this precept is to be committed to memory, this is to be delivered to children, and to him who sits at home, and to him who journeys on the way, and to him who is about to lie down, and to him who rises up; this is to be bound on the hand with some sign, this is to be written on the walls of the house: in short, it ought always to be before the eyes; if forgetfulness of this precept has once crept in, it cannot but be that a depravation of all doctrine follows forthwith, and follows in such a way that afterwards the truth will hardly find a place, though great effort be applied.

And since hatreds are also so much the more vehement, and oppress all judgment with greater force, the more often men's minds are provoked and troubled: since hatreds cannot be exercised against godly doctrine at the time when it has already been taken away and has passed into forgetfulness, men's minds must, in order to be accustomed to hatreds, be exercised against some false doctrine. And since the same people cannot both love the same doctrine and pursue it with hatred, there will have to be several false religions, and those different; and since they can hardly grow together in one people, they will also have to be in different peoples: in such a way that each man approves his own, and whatever departs from it he disapproves and holds in hatred, and those also at the same time who shrink from anything of it; wherefore, when sect exercises hatreds against sect, it will come about that from whatever quarter the truth shines forth, the greatest hatreds are prepared against it on every side; and as the followers of every ungodliness will have been accustomed to be received by other sects, so will the assertors of the truth be received, and so much the more harshly, the more wickedly Satan will then inflame men's minds to the hatred of them.

For this cause the people ought often to be warned: that those who are foreign to the true religion are to be held in hatred only so far that we are enemies to their ungodliness, and to those actions and deeds by which they either resist the truth, or in any way are injurious to God and to his church. Otherwise it so little becomes us to exercise any enmities against them, that we ought to pray for them, and that they are to be won by us to a better mind by every kind of kindness. [Marginal note: How those who are set over the church are provoked against the professors of the truth.] But since it is Satan's interest that the judgments of men, of whatever kind, should be so depraved, and that they should be exercised in mutual hatreds among themselves: then indeed it is most of all his interest if these things happen in those whose authority is great: as in those who are set over the churches, or who can apply force: as in magistrates and all powerful men. And as far as the pastors of the churches are concerned, in order that they may be much more bitter against the professors of the truth, whensoever anyone is stirred up, and may be provoked more readily, it will be of very great moment in that matter if from the depraved doctrine they perceive much advantage and veneration among the people: so that when the doctrine is called into controversy, a suit seems to be moved against them about their whole estate.

But in magistrates [Marginal note: And those who are over the commonwealth.] and in other powerful men, if anything of this kind can be, it will go most excellently well with Satan. But that thing chiefly serves his purpose, if they are persuaded that their office will then only be approved by God, if in those things which in any way touch religion they accommodate themselves to the nod of the ecclesiastical pastors, and are never not ready, at their discretion, and without any knowledge of the cause of their own, to shed blood. And these indeed are two great fortifications of Satan's kingdom: for we see how their power, dignities, wealth, and advantages, which they were wont to receive from the traffickings in souls, arm against the evangelical truth the Roman pontiff, cardinals, bishops, monks, and other men of that flour; and how magistrates, princes, and kings, made their executioners, rage against us. Therefore, while any godliness is vigorous, the people must take heed that they do not suffer their pastors, under any pretext whatever, to institute anything of trafficking: not even in such a way that at the beginning they themselves do not mix themselves in it.

It will come about, indeed, that when it has been instituted, they afterwards seize it to themselves: but this caution is now too late, the last day being now at hand. But magistrates, who otherwise, at whatever time, could sin even most grievously, ought to be warned not to make themselves the executioners of the ecclesiastical pastors: but to know that, whatever they have done, an account of it must be rendered by them; and that it will not be heard, if they allege that they were so persuaded by the pastors. For it has not been commanded them to obey any kind of men, but to read the volume of the law themselves, and from it to learn what in each matter needs to be done. About which matter we have disputed above in more words. And since Satan's kingdom cannot be in safety, unless there stands some means by which all those may be detected and known who are fit secretly to bring harm upon anyone, as by laying open errors, and by bringing in some light of truth, it greatly avails to that end, if together with the errors a custom has grown strong of doing something so commonly, that it may without difficulty be possible to observe those who do it, and those who do it less: and it be of such a kind that it is joined with some grave offence against God. Of this kind many things are to be seen in the Papacy: For there the mass is in the first place in such honour, that they think no holiness more excellent than if a man is present at it daily: and so nothing is so customary for anyone as to be present at mass: from which comes that thing, that when a friend meets a friend at the very time when it is celebrated, he asks him, as about a thing which no one is believed to omit, whether he has heard mass, or where he has heard it, or whether he wishes to hear it together with him in this or that church. And here indeed, if anyone understands that it is an abominable thing, and an invention of Satan, it is necessary either that he betray himself, or that he come under suspicion, so that thereafter whatever he says, does, and almost, I might say, thinks, is diligently observed, until at last they have ascertained who he is. So everywhere images are set up, both of Christ fastened to the cross, and of the Virgin, and others, which very many are all wont to venerate: in which, unless a man follows the common error and ungodliness, it will be a proof that he does not think about religion with the rest. So there are also many other things: but especially that institution of unloading sins year by year into a priest's ear, on which occasion those Pontifical spies fish out whatever they think is to their interest. Which was a most ingenious invention for enlarging and establishing that Pontifical kingdom.

For where there are institutions of this kind, it is most difficult to hold the truth as known, and not at once be marked, and hurried away to punishments; or certainly, which is much more frequent, gravely to offend God and conscience. And so heed must be taken again and again, lest Satan again institute something of this kind — which may be as a kind of net for catching the godly, or one in which those who fear God are compelled to offend — if by chance (which may the Lord avert) something of errors should again be brought into the church, and a tyranny not be wanting for thrusting them even upon the unwilling. And indeed every error is either joined with some action, as the worship of images — and that a means may not be brought in of marking those who have abhorred them, you cannot otherwise take heed than if you take heed that the errors themselves be not received — or they have no action joined with them: and those who detest them will not be able to be known by those men in any other way than if they betray themselves by their own confession, or if they refuse to approve the received errors. And indeed, at the very time when the doctrine is most pure, a custom of this kind can be introduced, which afterwards, when that doctrine has been corrupted, may afford Satan the same use for ensnaring the godly: as if (which has already begun to be done in some places) from anyone a confession of his own faith should be demanded.

If indeed the doctrine is gradually adulterated, and that custom is nevertheless retained, it will come about that, just as now those who think anything ungodly may perhaps be detected by that means, so that they may be excluded from the communion of the church, afterwards those rather will be detected who have thought rightly, or will be compelled against conscience to approve, by their own vote, the received errors, and those known by themselves to be such. But I do not say that no confession of faith at all is to be admitted: but I would not have such a one as that by which anyone is compelled to make mention of any doctrine which is not among the chief heads of the Christian religion, necessary to be known for salvation. For whatever controversy has arisen, especially a recent one: the opinion which those who preside disapprove — unless a man condemns it, he is not admitted. For my part, I cannot hope the best of this institution: for I scarcely see what advantage it can have, while I seem to myself to scent very many disadvantages, and very great ones indeed. [Marginal note: The confessions which are demanded of individuals: very many and great disadvantages.] What indeed is sought by this diligent exaction of confessions? Namely, that if anyone is in any error, he be compelled to betray himself: what then, when you have known it, what advantage will there have been in the matter? Very much (you will say): since there will be place for admonishing him. And if he has come to his senses, I shall have gained my brother: if he has been obstinate, he will be excluded from the church, lest he corrupt others.

But that those who err may be taught, there will have been no need of such an institution. For if tyranny is absent from the church, and if there is in the pastors true charity joined with great gentleness and humanity, and learning: those who dissent from the church in anything will of their own accord betray themselves: they will come, they will ask questions, they will call the matter into controversy: they will bring forward either their arguments, or testimonies from the divine letters. Nor will they refuse to hear the answers which proceed from charity equally and from solid learning. And if matters are handled skilfully, humanely, and prudently, truth will never fail to conquer. But if the church has already been seized by tyranny, nothing can be done with such institutions more apt for exercising tyranny, and they are nothing other than mere butcheries of consciences. But since tyranny is generally adorned with very specious titles, and sells itself as the lawful and salutary government of the church, we must deal with tyranny as with such as it wishes to seem, or perhaps even seems to itself to be: for those are not the only tyrants who are tyrants in mind and purpose, but those also who, deceived through imprudence by some false appearance of good, oppress their brethren with every kind of servitude, and abuse their authority in that matter.

Come then, freedom of opinions in the church seems to be pernicious: [Marginal note: A modest liberty of opinions about religion is especially opposed to Satan's kingdom.] and so you do not allow it to be, and you think it advisable to inquire diligently whether anyone by chance thinks anything amiss, so that they may either be drawn to a confession of the truth, or be excluded from the church — add, if you please, even that they be deprived of life — and for that purpose the means seems suitable, that confessions of this kind be demanded. It is likely, therefore, that if any danger or disgrace threatens from the church those who dissent in any matter, most men will gladly conceal their own opinion. Wherefore, if they can make a confession by which they do not express that which they wish to conceal — whether by making no mention of the matter, or by employing wrappings of words, by which they may seem to express another thing, and may express it — you will not have attained what you wished: but if it is necessary to express it, then, if anyone is of a cast-down and timid mind, what will happen? He will testify that he believes what in truth he does not believe, and, seeming to himself to assent to a false doctrine, he will certainly offend God. And if the matter is not of such weight that he could not have been saved even with that error, by that butchery of conscience his salvation will be cast into peril. But it will be a much graver sin, if any error is of that kind, and grave, to which he has assented in order to please the pastors: as if it should tend to a great lessening of God's glory.

But if a man is of a great mind, and endowed with the fear of God, he will indeed rather undergo every danger than lie. Wherefore, if it is punished, and he has steadfastly suffered death, how many disciples, pray, will that blood make! But if he has withdrawn himself, and is compelled to lack his country, perhaps even his wife and children, and his own goods: what hatreds will arise against the church? What curses will he cast upon those who preside? How earnestly will he strive (when he has found any place of refuge) to approve his own opinion, and to tear up the doctrine of the church from which he has been cast out? So men's diverse inclinations will come in, the sect will gradually grow, errors will spring up by those means of which we have spoken above, without measure, and a most pernicious thing will be brought into the church: the greatest and most bitter discord. But it is ascertained, both by experience itself of things, and by the most certain testimony of him who could neither be deceived nor deceive, that there is no kingdom so firm, no house so stable, which dissensions will not overthrow. So, since from the very beginning of the church there have always been in it so many, so frequent, so bitter sects, strifes, and discords: at last a ruin, itself collapsed, and most lamentable in the highest degree, has followed.

Of these evils the chief and almost the only cause was, that on account of any dissension, even a light one, some had condemned others, and were divided into sects. Wherefore, if we imitate our ancestors in this, who can doubt that the utmost destruction again threatens the church, scarcely yet a little restored? It is therefore much better, no laws or institutions having been devised by which men's consciences can ever be ensnared, to be content with a simple confession of faith, which embraces only the necessary heads of religion: and, as to other doctrines, to overthrow by suitable and apt demonstrations the errors which shall arise of their own accord, and be brought in. Let pastors leave the secrets of hearts to God: let them not usurp to themselves dominion over minds, nor set their mind to making any laws. That is the office of God alone, who alone can certainly see what good things, or what evil things, can flow from any institution: men cannot, without the light of the divine word, turn aside to their own prudence even in the least, without Satan, having taken the person of a heavenly angel, joining himself to them as a counsellor. What Satan suggests at the beginning, what poison they have, often not even a most sharp-sighted man will have noticed: but, as we have said, occasions are born, and some addition is made day by day, so that that thing which at the beginning you would have sworn could have no disadvantage will at last turn out into some horrid monster.

If controversies arise in the church from day to day (as they never fail to arise, godly meditation on the divine law having been turned into curiosity), the number of doctrines, which they call articles of faith, will also grow. Nor will the church always embrace, perhaps, the better part: for which cause God is wont to surrender men to seducing spirits, to their own lust, [Marginal note: 2 Thes. 2.] and that most just thing — namely, carelessness about salvation — we most certainly see not to be wanting in almost all. Why then should not vengeance again be expected, namely blindness and darkness? Nay rather, when the Lord shall come to judge the human race, it has been foretold that scarcely any faith will be left. When it has been observed that not a few, after making a confession of their faith, fall into errors, it will perhaps be instituted that it be repeated more often: an oath will perhaps also be added. But who could have divined in detail what Satan is about to devise? The best precaution of all is, in good time, to give notice of a new work, and so indeed to hinder foundations from being laid: but come, let it be pleasing to the judgment of reason.

But God's word is not set forth clear and certain. Away then, if you please, with that judgment of reason of yours: that is Satan's instrument, and destruction itself, no help toward any wholesome counsel. But you still press: If, on account of the disadvantages which can be received from the abuse of the institutions of good men, they are to be rejected, then not even the brazen serpent ought to have been raised up in the wilderness, to which the people afterwards began to pay divine honours; nor the Lord's supper to have been instituted, which afterwards was turned into so great an abomination: which can be said in the same way of many other things. But who are you, who dare to compare your deeds with God's deeds? But I nevertheless answer: God, whatever he institutes, knows what he does: what good, what evil will follow from it, he holds as ascertained. And why nevertheless he prefers this or that, a certain reason stands firm with him. And all things are his, so that by the best right he may use his own discretion in his own affairs; nor may it be right or lawful for anyone to ask him why he does anything. But we are blinder than moles, to whom it is not allowed either to hazard other men's affairs, or to be bold in another's game of chance. Nay rather, we have been commanded not to use our own judgment in those things which in any way pertain to his worship.

And so, if there is no other sin, this certainly will be no light one: if we refuse to acquiesce in this command, even when we know the many great evils which our ancestors brought upon themselves and their posterity, while they never make an end of devising institutions, rites, and laws. Therefore let that ancient serpent go, let it go at last, and depart to the greatest evil — namely, our own most foolish, most inept, and most rash prudence. But what are the heads of the Christian religion necessary to be known has been shown in the Second Book. Finally also, if not to preserve the whole kingdom, yet at least to retain some remnants, whatever happens, it seems to Satan to be greatly expedient that he make no thing at all not in some way his own, some mark of ungodliness having been impressed upon it, so that, wherever you turn, something meets your eyes. You could scarcely utter a word, but that the tongue, by corrupted custom, in some way does sacrifice to it: there is no water, no air, not time itself, no other thing at all, in which some perpetual badge of his kingdom does not remain. Nor can anyone take heed that these things do not happen, unless pastors and godly men, and other prudent men, watch in good time, lest they suffer the light of truth that has been obtained to be snatched from them again.
