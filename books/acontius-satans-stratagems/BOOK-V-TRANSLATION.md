*The Stratagems of Satan. Book Five.*

Those who offend against the law in part indeed understand that they are offending: but either, carried away by a certain impulse of their corrupted nature, they offend, in a manner, unwillingly; or, with all the reins of fear broken, they make light of the law; and in part they so offend that they either do not understand that they are acting against the law, or do not notice it.

And indeed those are to be said not to understand, who either have no knowledge of the law, or have it corrupted by great and ungodly errors: about which matter we seem to have said a good deal in the earlier Books. But those do not notice, who, although they have the law in some measure known to them, yet do not apply it to making an examination of their own actions, pursuits and thoughts — as one who indeed had a mirror, but would not look into it, and so would not see the spots he had on his face — or, if they do apply it, use so corrupted a judgment in it, and indeed none at all, that they cannot see what sort they are in their actions. Just as if someone did indeed set a mirror before himself, but, since he used a corrupted sense of sight, could not see his own spots. That those who have the law known to them do not apply it to judging of their own actions can come about from several causes. [Marginal note: The devices for feigning holiness.] And the chief of them is this: that, being occupied with other things — these with their own pleasures, those in their own trading, others in seeking their living by their own hands, others in the studies of human letters, indeed even of divine letters (so given up, that is, to contemplations of them, that they forget what use they should serve) — others again, engaged in other occupations, do not take time for themselves to make an examination of their own life, and to measure it by the rule of the divine law.

Others pass over this examination, because they think they have already made so much progress, and have the law so ready at hand, that they now judge it hardly necessary. As if they could no longer slip and be deceived by the appearance of what is right. Led by this persuasion, they live carefree, and commit many things after the manner of men, in which, although there is very much blame, yet it never even comes into their mind to suspect that there is even so little in them. But to most of them this indeed happens: that they look only either at those evil works which in their own judgment they do not commit, or at those good works which they think they are doing, so as to please themselves in them, while what evil they commit, or what they leave undone, they do not so notice. And so it comes about that everyone flatters himself, with the Pharisee, "as if he were not such as other men are." And that carelessness of theirs that error indeed is generally wont to follow, that, very like men dreaming, they place all holiness of life in some one thing which is utterly at variance with God's institutions, and, with their eyes fixed on it, think that things go exceedingly well with them; or also, intent on some few of those things which are comprised in the divine law, just as if God required nothing further from us, they take no thought of other things.

For my part, although in the Papacy (to use this word) there has been a certain strange darkness of the foulest errors, I could never have believed that it had come to such a pitch of blindness that, if you had asked anyone whether he believed or not that the observance of God's ten commandments is necessary for a Christian man, he would not have answered that he altogether believed it necessary. And if you had further asked him: What then? Would you not have counted among good and godly Christians a man who, the observance of those ten commandments being neglected, was nevertheless seen to frequent masses, to abstain from eating flesh on those days on which it has been forbidden them by the Roman Pontiff, once or twice a year to confess his offences to a sacrificer, to venerate the images of the saints, and to do everything else of that kind? I do not doubt that he would have altogether denied it; and yet it is more certain than certain that this commonly happens: that if a man is seen to frequent masses, and to keep the other things which have just been spoken of by us, even if it is established that he is an adulterer, and given up to other vices which are condemned by God's ten commandments, yet both in others' judgment and in his own he seems to lead a Christian life most beautifully. A persuasion of this kind, then, that holiness of life resides only in those things, so lies hidden in men's minds that they do not notice that they judge in that way: nay rather, if anyone is asked, he answers in such a way that he would wish to be thought to believe nothing less; but yet that most men are led by that error can be most certainly known from everyone's judgment, both about others and about himself.

In the same way, if you were to ask very many of our own people whether they think that Christian godliness is placed in this — that a man should never make an end of speaking abusively of the Roman pontiff, of cardinals, monks and sacrificers, should never cease to be indignant at their impostures and at their errors, and should have no choice of foods (for religion's sake, that is) — they would all, to a man, so confess that godliness is by no means placed in such things alone, that they would wonder that it could ever have come into anyone's doubt; but nevertheless it will be possible to see clearly that a persuasion of this kind lies hidden in the minds of many, if anyone observes more diligently, on the one hand, what sort they are in those actions which are truly worthy of a Christian man, and sees, on the other, how great an opinion of their own holiness they display, for no other cause than that they profess great enmities against the Papacy, and speak magnificently of the Gospel in their assemblies and over their cups, and extol with wonderful praises those who have become renowned in the restoration of the Gospel.

Very many also are too intent on those things — things which rather follow of their own accord upon a true lowliness of mind than that true godliness pursues them with any effort — of which kind are: all neglect of adornment in dress and in all things; contempt of all music, unless it has some exercise of godliness joined with it; a certain severity of countenance, and in every action a gravity such as is seen in all those who are turning over great and weighty matters in their minds. Being therefore occupied with these things, and with others of the kind, they so please themselves in them, and set so great a value on them, that they do not even remember those things from which these ought of their own accord to flow; and while they satisfy themselves abundantly in them, they do not doubt that they are living in a Christian and holy manner. And this happens to them not because, roused only in part from the sleep which holds their minds, they could not judge better about things, but because they give little heed to what they are doing. But what remedy will all these need, except that they be roused from sleep, and be warned often how brief is the enjoyment of this life, and what penalties they will pay for sloth, for carelessness and for yawning negligence, unless they meditate on the Lord's law with attentive minds day and night, and prudently order their life by its rule?

[Marginal note: Some, in order to feign themselves holy, rigidly and sourly censure and condemn others.] But there are also others, and very many indeed — indeed, even of the number of those who think they stand far ahead of others, nay rather, who despise all others in comparison with themselves — who likewise, not attending to what they are doing, have fashioned for themselves a certain strange kind of holiness: for each of them most diligently spies out his neighbour's deeds, and brings them under his own censure. Nor indeed are these of those who are commonly given up to drinking-bouts, gluttony, drunkenness, fornications, luxury, wantonness, and other most abandoned habits of that kind; nay rather, they are of those whose life, in other respects, seems much more reformed than that of the rest. But since it belongs to a man who loves God to shrink from all baseness and licence of life, and to delight in deeds done rightly, honourably and holily, this godliness, which each ought to have shown in his own life, they are willing, for the most part, to show in examining the deeds of others. And so they do indeed most curiously pry into what sort of life this man and that man leads: what he does, what he says — indeed, perhaps even what he thinks; whom he loves, whom he honours, whom he loves less; nor is there anything so small that it is not fit enough to make them begin to think ill of their brother.

Whatever is of such a kind that it could be done to either an evil or a good end, they will never interpret it otherwise than to the worse part — thinking, namely, that they will err less often if they have always thought of another that which is worse. And if anyone has once come into a less favourable opinion with them, it is all over with him. For they will hold him for ever in the same place, as if they held it for a thing ascertained that he could never return to a better course of life; and as soon as anything about a neighbour has reached their ears, do not think that they will go to him, lovingly warn him, and exhort him to remember his duty: anything but that. Nay rather, they will sooner have plied a backbiting tooth about him with anyone whatever than have made even one word with him, and will sooner have carried the matter abroad among the common people; nor will they make an end of whispering until some other subject offers itself to them for exercising that excellent godliness of theirs. And so far are they from thereafter doing any brotherly duty toward him, that they will rather take pains that he should be able to find no kindness from anyone; and when they do these things, they think their holiness is set in a wonderfully lofty place, and those who do not imitate them they despise and count for nothing — a thing which is the more to be lamented, because these men are of those who did not shrink from becoming exiles for the sake of the Gospel, and from leaving behind goods, honours, advantages, kinsfolk and friends; and how fair their beginnings were.

For my part, I know many — what shall I say? — and in no one place, nor of one nation only; but yet they were all exiles for Christ's sake. Ah, brothers, this way which you have entered is indeed not the right way: you did indeed once enter the right way, but you have turned aside from it. And certainly that on which you now persist will lead you to destruction: return, then, to the right way, while you may, while some part of the day still remains to you, and do not let it come about that night overtakes you as you wander that way. But certainly the eyes of charity are so far from being lynx-like for seeing another's spots, that it has no eyes at all: it is plainly blind. It covers another's sins, and does not allow them to be seen. And would you like to see clearly how that comes about? [Marginal note: 1. Cor. 13. 1. Pet. 4. Prov. 13.] Look at a mother who loves her little daughter with a singular love: although she is of a form not at all beautiful, yet to her she seems most exceedingly lovely; if her manners are unformed, boorish, and even perverse, they cannot seem such to her. When she is in sickness, if those things happen to her which could turn anyone's stomach, the mother shrinks from none of them. If, when she has grown older, she turns aside to manners that are not very honourable, only at last, after everyone else, does the mother suffer herself to be persuaded of it: whatever ought to have made her suspect her, she interprets to the better part; nor can she suspect any evil of her before she has had her own eyes as witnesses of the sin: this, plainly, love and charity do.

But (you say) it is a foolish and silly charity that you are describing. I say indeed foolish and silly: for charity, in man at least, will not exist unless it has been foolish and silly. [Marginal note: 1: Cor. 13.] What then is that? "It thinks no evil; it bears all things, it believes all things, it hopes all things." Does the Apostle seem to you, in these words, to depict charity as very clever and cunning? Does it not seem to express the feeling of that mother of whom we were just speaking? [Marginal note: Prov. 20.] What Solomon says also beautifully agrees with it: "Hatred stirs up strifes, but charity covers all offences." But, I beg you, if you have forgiven someone an offence, and he, on that very same day, when he has offended you again, comes, says that he repents, and asks that he be forgiven: what would be more silly (in our judgment, at least) than to give him credence, and not rather to suspect that you are a laughing-stock to him? For the same wisdom will have dictated this, which dictated that saying: "Whoever is once bad is always thought bad in the same kind of evil." But yet, what does the Son of God command here? [Marginal note: Luc. 17.] "If your brother (he says) has sinned against you, rebuke him; and if he has repented, forgive him. And if he has sinned against you even seven times in one day, and seven times on the same day has returned to you and said that he repents, forgive him."

Consider here, I beg you, all you rigid and sour censors, wherever on earth you are: consider your own feelings — whether he will not seem to you to be about to act most foolishly who obeys this command. Him — seven times? And on the same day? You cannot deny it; but since that is your settled feeling, that it judges what the Son of God commands to be most foolish, and since you are so far from opposing that feeling, that you rather please yourselves in yourselves wonderfully on that account: what, I ask, is that judgment of yours? Does not the thing itself openly declare that the Spirit of the Lord is very little in you? And if it is so, what is it that puffs you up so greatly? Why do you despise all others in comparison with yourselves? I have no need to point out here to whom I am saying these things. Those to whom this address belongs, their own conscience will point them out to themselves (if they read it); and if they themselves have understood, it is enough: for we do not write these things to this end, that we should brand anyone with any infamy, but that we may draw our dearest brothers to gentleness, to the duties of charity, and to a true, not a painted, kind of holiness. These things, therefore — to be blind to a neighbour's sins, to interpret all things to the better part, to hope nothing that is not good of another — are so joined with charity that, where these are not, there charity cannot be, no more than fire can be where there is no heat; and accordingly, since God requires charity of us, and plainly wills these things together with it, which cannot be separated from charity, it is not indeed that that saying of certain men is not most true, "If you wish to err less often, always think of anyone that which is the worse," since it agrees most excellently with that word, [Marginal note: Ps. 13.] "There is none who does good, not even one," and with others like it; but we have need of this blindness of charity for this reason, that a man can bring himself only with the greatest difficulty to bestow any benefit on one of whom he does not think well, or to follow him with love.

But if, with a lamp applied, we should wish too curiously to inquire into anyone's life, we shall at last all be found to be just what we are: so that we seem to be of the same flour, namely those whose every most excellent work can, as he says, [Marginal note: Cap. 64.] be compared with the most unclean cloth of a woman who suffers her accustomed flow. And since, in extenuating his own faults, everyone acts the part of a most merciful and most indulgent judge, but in judging other men's we are all more severe than is fitting, there will be nothing which can reconcile one man to another; but occasion for hatreds will never be wanting.

But since God wills our goodness to be like his own — he who makes his sun to rise alike on the good and on the evil, and, however great anyone's perversity may be, does not suffer him to be without a share in his immense beneficence — he does not wish there to be in us so great a zeal to know one another as we really are; nay rather, even that very thing which you may hold as ascertained about another, he in a manner wills you not to believe, so that we may thus be able to bear with one another in charity. For, in order to call a brother back to a better course of life and draw him away from some evil course he has entered upon, it is not unbecoming either to have his actions known to you, or to bestir yourself to know them. But only then — if you have made so much progress in Christ's school that you can love a brother even overwhelmed with innumerable offences. If you are not yet of this mind, know that it is not your business to inquire into another's life: that you have still more than enough to do in your own house: that that saying can still rightly be said to you: Physician, heal yourself. For when you foolishly take offence at something in a neighbour which does not please you, and on account of that thing judge him unworthy of your favour, what do you think you actually see? Even if the thing is exceedingly unworthy, do not doubt that what lies hidden is much worse still. What indeed you do see is only a certain speck in his eye; but there are still very great beams in it which you cannot behold.

And nevertheless he to whom you owe all things requires of you that you love him. Or do you think that there is no speck in your own eye? Without doubt there are many, and others see them just as you see other men's. Nay rather, there are beams in it too, and great beams, which no one except God, and perhaps in part your own conscience, can see. The Apostle indeed understood that he had beams in his own eyes, and he so understood it that he groaned deeply: "Ah, wretched man that I am! Who will deliver me from the body of this death?" Since therefore you have in your own house what you may look at, what you may rebuke, what you may pursue with hatred: have you so much leisure left over that you should inquire into other men's affairs? With what face do you dare to assail them? How do you dare, for that cause, to turn your mind away from your brother, and to stir up ill-will against him among others as well? And if you judge a brother unworthy of your goodwill because of the many and great offences you have noticed in him: what a judgment, and how severe a judgment, do you pronounce on yourself? How is it that you yourself have need of God's mercy — if not so greatly, yet certainly altogether? [Marginal note: Mat. 18.] Does that word not terrify you? "You worthless servant, I, at your entreaty, forgave you all that debt: ought you not in turn to have pitied your fellow servant, just as I also had pitied you?"

But you, wretched man, when by the wantonness of your tongue you shall have robbed your brother of that which everyone holds dearest of all things — a good name, namely: in what great matter shall you ever have profited him? When by your whisperings you shall have spoiled him of his fame, what shall you have left him? It seems to you, I believe, a light thing to open your mouth and blurt out words. But does the matter seem to you just as light, when someone does the same thing to the defrauding of your own honour? But if even then you should do it only when your brother's sin had been ascertained by you — since it may happen, indeed to charity, which believes all things, it is most credible, that he will come to his senses and be drawn away from that evil practice — by bringing it out, and parading it through the mouths of men, you would do a thing in no way worthy of a Christian man, as may be learned from the example of Joseph. It was most thoroughly ascertained by him — so far as human judgment could reach — that Mary, his betrothed, whom he had found with child although he had not yet touched her, had committed a great crime, and one worthy of great punishment. But what does the writer of the sacred history say? Did he at once cry out, spread the matter among the common people, flee to the magistrate with a complaint of the injury?

So surely would those men have done of whom we are now treating — those holy men. But see how Joseph thought nothing of the kind: how wholly different were his purpose and his mind. Joseph, says Matthew, since he was a righteous man — mark it — since he was a righteous man, and was unwilling to expose her, was thinking how he might put her away secretly. Therefore, if it was the part of a righteous man to cover that sin, it would surely have been the part of a wicked man, and of one in whom there was neither the fear of God nor any charity, to bring it out, although the certainty of the offence was so great, so to speak. But if, when a man has ascertained another's sin, he nevertheless does not do best by publishing it, what will it be if he has had nothing besides suspicions, and light suspicions too? Or can there be even a grain of Christian charity in him who, upon any suspicion whatever, even the lightest, has not shrunk from rashly depriving his brother thereby of that thing than which, according to God's grace, he can have nothing more excellent, and which he afterwards, even if he most desired it, could not restore to him? [Marginal note: Matthew 1.] But of a man in whom there is no charity, and who does not love his own brother, what judgment can you form, except that he has not yet known God? What? Would you dream that the law was given to this end, that everyone should curiously observe how holily this man fulfilled it, how wickedly that man broke it, and should rather find fault with his neighbour and expose him, than strive to bring him back to soundness?

If you think so, you are indeed dreaming. That, I say, is not the end of the law that was given, but rather this: that it should be to everyone a rule of life, in which, as in a mirror, he might look upon the pollutions and the foulnesses, not of his neighbour, but of himself, so that he might first amend his own life, and then also his neighbour's, if he could.

There is another kind of delusion, into which those who fall commit many sins, while they think they are doing nothing of the sort. And it is of this kind: since many actions and deeds deserve either praise or blame for this cause alone, that they were undertaken for a good end or for an evil one, it happens to many — a thing which all may rightly wonder at — that, least of all understanding their own thoughts and feelings, they in reality undertake a thing with one purpose, and seem to themselves to undertake it with another. I know that this will seem absurd to hear; but nevertheless, believe me, nothing is more true. And, not to go far afield: James and John, the Lord's disciples, when in a certain village of the Samaritans they had not been received into lodging together with the Lord, because they were bound for Jerusalem — a city toward which the Samaritans were hostile because of the controversies about religion which they had with the Jews — asked the Lord thus: Do you wish us to command fire to come down from heaven, that it may consume them, as Elijah does?

But what does the Lord say? You do not know, he says, of what spirit you are. Those men without doubt seemed to themselves to have conceived that thought out of zeal for the divine glory, and out of that charity and godliness with which they followed the Lord; otherwise they would never have questioned the Lord in such a way. But the answer which they received shows openly that that thought had proceeded from the offence taken at the lodging that had been refused, and from the inveterate hatred which there was between those two peoples, and from a certain secret desire of vengeance besides. And as for the time when the Roman pontiffs began to acquire reputation, authority, and splendour: I for my part would never believe that they were all destitute of godliness, a few perhaps excepted, since in their writings there may be seen conspicuous traces of no common godliness. When therefore they not only allowed themselves to be worshipped with the greatest honour, to be exalted, and to be gifted most liberally, but also applied their diligent endeavour to acquiring daily greater authority and power, it is in truth very likely that it seemed to them that that splendour would be very useful to the church of God: so that, when they were in greater veneration among all orders, their admonitions, and rebukes, and ecclesiastical censures, by which they kept the people in their duty, would have much more weight.

But nevertheless, an inborn and insatiable greed of having in man, and a surpassing haughtiness of mind — by which it comes about that everyone wishes both his dignity and his authority, if he can, always to be in a high place — persuaded them of those advantages to the church for scarcely any other cause, even though they perceived nothing of the kind. In our own age also, in the attack upon the Gospel, although many aim at nothing else than that nothing should go from them of reputation, of advantages, of wealth — men, indeed, who think all thought of religion foolish and worthy of mocking laughter — yet I cannot but persuade myself that there are still many who, in what they do, seem to themselves to act from love of religion, and in order to approve their godliness to God. Indeed, the Lord himself foretold [Marginal note: Matthew 17] many ages before that it would be so — oh, these men, who yet, if it did not privately concern them in some measure which opinion should prevail, would not be engaged in these controversies with so great an onset and such contentions of mind! What shall I say of those who take up arms to defend the cause of the Gospel? I fear, in truth, that many are led more by hatreds conceived against their adversaries, and by zeal for vengeance, and moreover by a desire of preserving their native home, and their goods and advantages — so that they may worship Christ at home at their ease, and not be forced, exiled, despised, and destitute, to follow him — than by any very ardent and true godliness.

God grant that this suspicion of mine may be wholly groundless; certainly it must be allowed to no man not diligently to examine his own feelings, lest perhaps he practise a deceit on himself, lest, while he pursues his own cause, he persuade himself that he is pursuing the cause of Christ. But I do not dispute here what in this kind is lawful for a Christian man, and what is not; I only earnestly wish that every man's false pretences should be known to himself.

Here a greater consideration [Marginal note: Indeed, even the highest men here often deceive themselves: they plead their own cause, while they think they are pleading God's.] presents itself, in which I have very great need of the equanimity of those whom it concerns. I beseech them therefore, by Christ Jesus, not to be stirred by anything until the matter has been fully known; then, if we shall be found to have acted rashly or unjustly, there will be place for indignation; but if we shall seem to have said something, it will belong to their dignity and their godliness to take it in good part. [Marginal note: So greatly do they boil up, if anyone dares to contradict them.] Those who discharge the office of teaching in the church, and for that cause have gained a famous name and reputation, if anyone touches some doctrine of theirs — we see, indeed, with how great bitterness they are wont to rise up against him.

And I do not doubt — for we are treating here only of godly teachers — that in this they seem to themselves to aim at nothing else than to plead the cause of Christ. But whether there may nevertheless lie hidden beneath it something else, beyond all their own opinion, is to be searched out with great earnestness of mind, because it greatly concerns the Christian commonwealth that such men should slip in no matter, especially a great one, so that what they ought to beware of may not lie hidden from them. When the Lord, setting out for Jerusalem, had foretold what things were to befall him there, and Peter had exhorted him to spare himself, what did the Lord say to him? Begone, Satan, he says; you are a hindrance to me, for you do not savour the things of God, but the things of men. To what end is this? Surely, that it may appear that such a thing could fall from an apostle of the Lord — and that apostle, too, to whom the Son of God had given testimony of the highest love toward himself, and for whose faith, that it should never fail, he had promised that he would pray — a thing worthy of so grave a rebuke that the Lord, who was of incredible gentleness, called him by the name of Satan. And yet it is altogether likely that Peter seemed to himself, bound by that godliness with which he worshipped the Lord, to have exhorted him in that way. Wherefore, even if there are some men in the church of God most excellent, and prophets, and apostles sent for its restoring, yet some of their deeds will not absurdly be called into doubt, whether they could have proceeded from any other source, or can proceed, than from the Spirit of God.

But certainly, so long as their doctrines are held in such common consent of all that they are free of all controversy, their name stands in a more illustrious place, and their dignity also; their authority is more respected, their whole life more pleasant and more tranquil. If those doctrines are called into doubt, it seems to them that a suit is at the same time raised against their whole estate — a thing which cannot please human nature. And although these men are servants of God, and men of great holiness, it is nevertheless certain that they are still men, in whom there still remain some remains of the inheritance which they received from our first parents; which remains, in such times, cannot be idle, and if they cannot well come forth openly, they must at least secretly and treacherously be contriving something, without even the man perceiving it in whom they attempt it. But certainly, if whatever these excellent men do, they do rightly in that kind, and from zeal for God's glory alone, no other account being mingled in it — since we cannot even think anything good as though by ourselves, but to whatever thing we are sufficient, that is from God [Marginal note: 2 Corinthians 3] — it must needs be that they do it with the Spirit of God as their guide; but it is the doctrine of him who cannot lie, that the Spirit wars against the flesh — that is, against human sense — and human sense against the Spirit: so that whatever you do under the impulse of the Spirit, you will do it scarcely and unwillingly, your whole nature struggling against it.

We experience this in loving our enemies, in forgiving injuries, in acknowledging our errors and offences if anyone rightly rebukes us; in exposing our life, for the glory of God, to curses, to disgraces, to persecutions; in short, to torments, and other things of that kind. Come then, you to whom this indeed pertains, weigh it, I beseech you, in your own minds: when you rise up against someone with whom you have some controversy about doctrine, and assail him with great bitterness of words, how unwilling, how resisting are the feelings with which you are driven to it. For so it ought to happen, if indeed you do it under the impulse of God's Spirit. And so it is likely that you come to it as though compelled, with no other feeling than that with which, as is credible, Abraham was ready to sacrifice his only son Isaac: that he indeed judged that God must be obeyed, but that his whole nature cried out against, and grieved at, the very hard thing that had been commanded him. It is likely that you do not easily descend to it, nor at the very first impulse, but at last, sharply and often goaded — I do not say by the provocations of an adversary, but by the inward motions of the Spirit, teaching that the glory of God and the safety of the church altogether demand it — so that such men are treated in the roughest ways.

It is likely, finally, that you perform all things more coldly, more sparingly, and more meagrely than that inward force of the Spirit requires; that the words with which you command another more harshly are hardly to be had; that you slide again and again into a gentler and milder style; that your speech can scarcely come back to reproaches and curses. Consider, I pray you, in your own minds, first, whether it is likely that a man who does things by the impulse of God's Spirit should in those things bear himself with so difficult, slow, and shrinking a temper. But weigh at the same time whether you experience any such struggle and shrinking in this kind. But if there is any force here, I wonder indeed whence there is so great an abundance of words, and in some men, too, so elegant, so adorned, and so beautifully and neatly composed. [Marginal note: Most ready for revilings, and eloquent in them.] For if anyone looks into the writings of certain men, in which they wrangle with someone, he would be ready to swear that nothing could be done more eloquently, nothing more copiously. Wherefore, if for assailing a man there is at your command a certain inexhaustible abundance of the choicest stings; if your speech runs like some most rapid river; if for making a beginning the slightest things are cause enough for you, and occasions in plenty offer themselves to you everywhere; and after you have once begun to twitch someone, as when matter is daily added to flames, your onset seems never able to be calmed: here there appears no trace that the business is done with a reluctant mind.

Wherefore see to it, I beseech you, again and again, lest no Spirit of God should then breathe upon your sails; rather it seems that the Spirit of God, shaken off by these commotions of your minds, has altogether left you to yourselves, so that by you indeed it might appear what manner of men you are — unless this seems to you to be the case, make trial of your dexterity in handling controversies with gentle, humane, and mild words. And we shall see whether there will be here the same abundance of words, the same adornment, the same force. I fear, in truth, that you cannot furnish as much here. And what else is the cause, except that in assailing adversaries you follow your own tempers, and do what is most suited to your nature? But to employ gentleness, there is need of charity, and of a great force of the Spirit: of which, take note, your store is not over-great. Nor yet do I on that account deny that you are servants of God, indeed that you are chosen even for the greatest things. For if I can hardly recognise the Spirit of God in so great bitterness of yours, or if you were to use him even against Satan, not to say sometimes against men who are not the worst, there are other things which I see, and which I cannot persuade myself have proceeded from anywhere else than from the Spirit of God; but those men of whom we were just speaking were servants of God and apostles — although in the meantime neither that desire of vengeance, nor the counsel of avoiding death, was from God.

For my part, I hold you to be servants of God, for whom the greatest thanks are owed to God also: through whom, namely, he has put to flight so much and so foul darkness, has brought so much light to the Gospel, and has freed so immense a number of men from the most cruel tyranny of Satan. These are great things, and such as ought not easily to be blotted out by ungrateful forgetfulness. But nevertheless it does not follow from this that you are not men, and that you do not sometimes do something after the manner of men. Wherefore you ought to separate those works which you perform with the Holy Spirit as guide and author from those which are your own; and to acknowledge the former, so that for them we may give God, as I have said, immense thanks, and strive to imitate it; and as to the latter, to pray the same God that he would lay open to you what they are, and not suffer you to persist in them, and that he would grant to us prudently to avoid them. So one judgment was to be formed of Jonah's preachings which he delivered to the Ninevites, which were from God; another of the accursed words which he himself spoke in anger to God.

But why was he angry? Oh, how detestable a thing is the temper of man, if God withdraws his Spirit from him: for although by Jonah himself he had threatened the Ninevites themselves, he was unwilling to destroy that most ample city; nay rather, having perceived the repentance of that people, he preferred to spare it — an example which has not been committed to the divine writings for this reason, that by reading it we should only be delighted, and beguile the time with the ridiculous folly of a foolish man, who brought it into his mind to be angry with God himself, and in a wonderful manner, and to whom it seemed that God, in exercising so great clemency, did not act wisely, and that he himself understood better what ought to be done. Nay rather, as all things have been delivered for our instruction and teaching, so the Spirit of God left this example written: that in it, as in a mirror, we might discern what our feelings are, and what our temper is — we who, since we cannot obtain salvation otherwise than as it is gotten by the highest mercy and clemency of God, are so affected that every one of us wishes to have this clemency and mercy for himself alone, and for a few whom he loves, but toward all the rest would have God act with the highest right, severely and cruelly. And such as he would wish God to be seen to be, such, surely, he himself bears himself.

Indeed, this example has been recorded in writing chiefly so that it might be a lesson to you who act as God's messengers and ambassadors, in whom this feeling is wont to be peculiarly strong. Not indeed as though you were of a worse temper than others, but because, in you great men, by the opportunity of your office, unless it is repressed by a great force of the Spirit, it betrays itself, so that you might understand that your feelings have nothing in common with the nature of God; and so, in exercising your office, you would think that those feelings are not to be followed as guides, but the commandments of God; and you would learn by his example — such as he showed himself toward Jonah — that each of you should so bear himself toward all those who shall seem to have turned aside from right institutions. And see how Jonah turned aside, to whom God's judgment did not give satisfaction, who set his own before it, and so set it before it that he himself burned with anger against God: is there anything that can even be thought more wicked? Was it not this, to set his own most rotten folly before the immense wisdom of God? But where are the revilings, then, and the reproaches worthy of so great arrogance? Are you not, says the Lord, greatly moved? As if he said: What is this matter, Jonah? Are you angry with me? And does it not come into your mind who you are, and who I am? Do you think, then, that you have to deal with me just as with some man who is your equal?

Do you not know that I am the maker of heaven and earth, and of all the things that are contained in them? That I govern all those things with the highest wisdom? That this wisdom of mine, contained by no bounds, by no measure, is set far above all thoughts: when you very often do not even understand where to set your foot? Therefore restrain yourself, and consider what you are doing. Here there is no reviling: no insult: but rather a certain highest gentleness. But what a method he then takes, to bring Jonah to a recognition of his own folly: he makes either a gourd, or an ivy, grow, which would shade Jonah's little hut, and when Jonah had taken great pleasure in it, he sends in worms which would destroy it: [Marginal note: Jonah 4.] so that, when Jonah grieved at the loss of a worthless thing, which in one night, with no trouble spent on it, with no labour, had grown, by that example he might teach him how much weightier a cause he himself would have had for grieving at the destruction of so great a city, in which there were, besides the cattle, more than a hundred and twenty thousand men, who could not even discern their right hand from their left. He does not, therefore, insult him: he does not overwhelm him with revilings: as you generally do: but with the greatest dexterity he lays open to him his own follies. But if God can so bear with a man, shall men not bear with one another, when there is no one who is not held by some error, who does not labour under his own disease, who does not himself also need another's gentleness, clemency and mercy?

I pass over that sometimes a man pursues another with great indignation of spirit, and afflicts him with insults, who himself is far more deserving of those insults. Nor is it in vain that, in the place where the Lord answers Peter that a brother who sins must be forgiven not up to seven times, but up to seventy times seven, he adds the likeness of that servant who, when he had obtained from his king release from a debt of ten thousand talents, soon did not hesitate to rage against his fellow-servant, who owed him a hundred denarii: for the preceding words easily declare that this example belongs most of all to you: from which this must be said to you, that, although you make light among yourselves of the multitude and the greatness of the offences which God has forgiven you, yet, if your debt, by which you were bound to God, is compared with a sin or error which you sometimes so bitterly pursue in another, the proportion will be the same as that between ten thousand talents and a hundred denarii. That example ought surely to have some force for breaking so great a pride in certain men. And since you seem not to have noticed these things sufficiently of your own accord, you ought not to take it ill: rather, you ought to have and to give great thanks to God, that he revealed them to someone who would lovingly admonish you: not in any way to stain your reputation, but rather that you, having bid farewell to this least praiseworthy custom of yours, might join to it another, more worthy of your persons, together with your other greatest praises: and so it would come about that in the kingdom of God you would be a light of such a kind: that no deed of yours, no action unworthy for all posterity to imitate, could be noted.

But if he whom the Lord has raised up to admonish you is not possessed of that celebrity of name, or that authority, which might seem necessary in one who was to admonish great men in God's church: consider how this is nothing new: that God uses the most despised men for accomplishing the greatest things. What deeds did God accomplish through the Apostles? But what sort of men were they? And how great a man was Moses? Yet he needed the counsel of his father-in-law, a foreigner, and in no way to be compared with him in reputation for wisdom: and when that counsel was given him, he so far did not despise it, or think his dignity lessened by it, that he approved it most humanely, and made use of it. Judith also — and a woman — and a young man rebuked the senate of Jerusalem: nor does it yet refer that to any disgrace of its own: rather it most humanely lends its ears to the warnings, and obeys them.

Therefore, if you have not been able to attain that praise, that of your own accord you avoided what you ought: since, namely, you are men, not gods: at least take care again and again that that praise which is next to it does not escape you: and than which perhaps none greater can fall to a man: namely, that your modesty be seen, in that you are willing gladly to obey reason, through whomsoever it is laid open, and however great the authority of your name may be; and that you be seen to have a heart not of stone, but of flesh. It as much becomes you to do this as anything whatever: since you are the light of the world, on whom the faces and eyes of all are turned. Nor indeed do I condemn every sharpness of words, with all distinction of persons, things, place and time removed. For so I should also rebuke John, surnamed the Baptist, who called certain Pharisees a brood of vipers, and the Lord himself, who sometimes received the same race of men with words so hard and atrocious. But this I do contend, that there is need of a great and weighty, and most certain, cause, in order to come to that. As, for example, that you have ascertained that what you are attacking is an error, and a great one, and that its author is of a perverse and stubborn spirit, who will not acquiesce even in truth once recognised.

But that, for any fault, even the slightest, unless a man yields to you at the very first word, and prostrate worships you as certain gods, you should flare up, come to insults and curses, render the disease incurable, drive men to forming sects, and make the highest equal to the lowest: and that you should sometimes do these things when it is you who are hallucinating, not those whom you pursue: who could bear it? And who, when he sees you, like unskilled physicians, always administer the same medicine for every disease: who (I say) would not be utterly disgusted? Do you wish me to say what the matter is? It must be said outright, that you must lower your spirits, that you must hear with great patience him who seems to you far inferior in wisdom and learning: that you must be willing to fish out what those traps are which deceive a rude and unskilled, but perhaps arrogant, talent: that you must be willing to devise reasons so open and so conspicuous that those with whom you have to do can see and feel their own errors: to do these things, I say, you must with great effort resist nature, which is moved by every smallest thing, is impatient, and is most prompt to injury: and therefore can scarcely be contained within the bounds of modesty and humanity; there is need of a certain unconquered patience, and of a great force of spirit.

So that this may rightly be called a narrow door. But for human nature to come to angers, to insults, to reproaches and curses, no spurs are needed. Nor, indeed, is great industry needed for you to obey it. Which is so true, that even if you resolve to employ this gentleness which we praise, and to avoid bitterness, you will nevertheless never lay aside so much of your ferocity that you do not still retain more than enough. But if to a nature in itself fierce beyond measure we should also add spurs — attributing what belongs to it to the Holy Spirit, and flattering ourselves in it — to what will the matter come at last? Surely the most beautiful building of the restored Church will necessarily be made once again an irreparable ruin. But one may see that, if a man dreams he is doing something for some good end, in which, however, he indulges his own desires, there are in it no bounds, no laws, by which a lust so painted over may be restrained. Thus the Roman Pontiffs, when they thought that their own splendour and wealth belonged to the glory of God and to the best state of the Christian commonwealth, deceiving themselves therein: so great increases of power, dignity and pride were made for them day by day, that they could be an envy to any of the most powerful kings and Emperors.

Let whoever wishes to see the things he may most wonder at read through the book whose title is *Ceremoniale Romanum*, printed not so long ago at Cologne. There he will see with what pomp the Roman pontiff celebrates a solemn Mass: with what pomp a solemn banquet, with what pomp he rides in solemn procession: as if God, about to reign among men in human form, and, after the manner of men, to use pomp according to his dignity: what greater thing could now be thought of for him, nothing seems left. From one thing one may conjecture what the rest are like. For among other laws there is also this: that if, at the time when the pontiff rides in solemn procession, any king is present, or even the Emperor himself, he too must perform some office. But what office, I beseech you, after all? Perhaps that both by his wisdom and by his authority he should take care that all things are done decently and fittingly, according to the dignity of the play: and certainly that he should hold the first place after him? O shameful deed. Who would have believed it? Nay rather, by that pontifical law, the Emperor's office will be to attend the pontiff at his side, like a footman on foot: which Sigismund did at the Council of Constance, when Martin V had obtained the pontifical crown: what could be added even in thought to so great pride?

Thus, if any men seem to you to be advancing Christ's cause, and serving God's glory, by pursuing others with any means and in any way whatsoever: while meanwhile that belongs to their own advantages, or dignity, or interests, they can devise nothing so bitter, nothing so savage, nothing so cruel or atrocious, that it does not seem to them to have too much of gentleness and of clemency: so that to lie to the defrauding of those whom they think must be harassed or destroyed, and to load them with the greatest and most unjust calumnies of every kind, seems to them a thing far holier than to speak the truth in any other matter: and to take life from one of these men, than to keep unharmed ever so many others, even of those whom they themselves favour. [Marginal note: The feigned trust of very many in God, and also their gratitude.] Another prevarication, very like the one written above, occurs both in the placing of whatever trust of ours in God, and in the acknowledging of God as the author of whatever benefit we have obtained. For let there be a man so furnished with the necessaries of life that he lives comfortably, whether from his own estates, or from a craft, or from trade; let this man's life be honourable, his character such as befits a good man; let him love religion, let him frequent the assemblies, hear sermons, call upon God as publicly as privately: in short, let him perform all the things by which a not careless observer could commend a pious man.

Nor indeed let him think he is doing anything in pretence. If, then, you ask this man thus: Where does this living of yours come from? From your estates? "What," he will say, "from my estates? They would quickly cease to be mine, unless God by his highest goodness brought it about that they are preserved for me. Nor indeed, even if they were mine, could they feed me, unless he himself made them fruitful for me." A very fine and holy speech indeed; to which perhaps he will add further, and will say: that not even that bread which he now has ready at home, no, which he holds in his hand, can feed him: unless God grants him to enjoy it, and to be nourished so that he can: and that therefore it must still be asked of him nonetheless. What could be thought either holier, or more religious, than words of this kind? Nothing (you say), provided only they contain nothing feigned, but express in good faith the man's sense. Suppose that man speaks no otherwise than he himself seems to be persuaded. What if nevertheless we should prove that the man's trust in fact is other: that he feigns another for himself beyond all his own thinking? It would seem (I think) difficult to do: but it is nevertheless resolved to try. Come, therefore, let us enter on this way: let us snatch away from this pious man his goods, every art of making gain, his friends, the society of all men: in short, whatever is not God: and let us place him in some desert place, far from towns, villages, and all intercourse with men, with only his wife, and the little children whom he singularly loves, without any provisions.

Let him hear his little children crying out and asking for bread: what will this man of ours do, left with God alone? He will (I think) patiently await the time of supper: and when it has come, he will pray God to supply him food: or perhaps he will even delegate that office, as is his custom, to his elder little son, certain that God will be no less rich and bountiful here than he had found him when he was at home. Alas, how I fear that this man will appear far other, namely, that, disheartened, scarcely drawing breath, he will be ready to beg God with great groans not to desert him. But what do these groans mean here for themselves? When he was at home, indeed, his prayers did not have this accompaniment of groans. Is he perhaps afraid that God has not so much here in the desert to supply, as when he was at home? Or has the good God become less now than he was a little before? Where is that former trust, so firm and so tranquil? How does it happen that the same trust is not here which was at home?

God is certainly the same. Here the thing itself speaks: when this man of ours was at home, that trust which seemed to be placed in God rested almost wholly on the abundance of the things which were at hand. But that the man thought he had it placed in God alone was nothing but a kind of illusion and a dream. Namely, he trusted God (as is commonly said) with a pledge received: while he saw God in his store, in his barn, in his cellars, he trusted him abundantly; after he has withdrawn himself from bodily eyes, so that he can now be discerned only by the mind, it is a far other matter. Hence, therefore, those tears and those groans: and from an example, or likeness, of this kind we can learn what was said by the Lord. If you have as much faith as is a grain of mustard seed, if you say to this mountain, Move from here to there, it will move: this is not to be taken as if there were some excess in the words, such as can be understood in those words of John's in which he says: If all the things which Jesus did were committed to writing one by one, the world itself could not contain the books that would be written: but it is to be taken as the words sound: for our faith, even when it seems greatest, if the eyes, and every thought of things that are not God, are drawn away from all those things, is so small that it can scarcely be perceived.

But it is most profitable here not to be deceived, lest, when we think we abound in that in which we are most in want, we ask that it be granted to us, not with that intention of mind with which we ought. And as little as we are accustomed to place trust in God, so little also do we refer to him, as received from him, whatever we attain: and what happens in the things necessary for life, the same happens in all other things. For who of us is there, who has any knowledge, however small, both of other things and especially of divine things, who does not seem to himself to refer it to God as received, and to acknowledge him as its teacher? But if here, as in the example above, you could as conveniently draw away your thought from every thing which is not God, it would become plain that we attribute to God so small a part of this honour that it can scarcely be perceived, indeed scarcely even that. In word, indeed, we confess God as the author: nor do we seem to ourselves to be pretending: but meanwhile our talent, our watchings, our labours, our books, our teachers are before our eyes. As if we had to be called back from the finish to the starting-place, and, our knowledge having been snatched away, it had to be acquired anew: but in such a way that these aids are wanting to us, and God alone is left to us, from whom to obtain it, and his oracles: I do not know how certain a hope we should have of obtaining it.

From distrust of this kind flow all those provisions which men are accustomed to devise for preserving the purity of doctrine, so that posterity may not be able to corrupt it: I mean those provisions which, anxiously, without any command of God, they devise by their own human prudence: and which surely they would let go, if they were seriously persuaded that God is the dispenser of these things: that his Spirit lives perpetually and keeps watch, to guard them and to protect them from the arts and snares of the adversary. But since they place their safeguard in their own inventions: and God wishes to teach by conspicuous experiments how foolish all our counsels are: it comes about that, his protection having been withdrawn, he lets us wage war with Satan by our own strength: that is, to succumb as soon as possible, and, as we have deserved, to perish. If we truly acknowledged God as the author of all wisdom, such words would nowhere be read: Unless they had learned from us, they could not make even a word about the Gospel. O good man, the Gospel history is never not in your hands, and does that word not occur to you, If these should hold their peace, the stones will cry out? Surely it was of your talent to attain a sound understanding of the Gospel: and you alone of all men were born who could do that: that is: if you had not been, there was no hope left for the salvation of the human race: therefore in so glorious a deed of the restored Church the Holy Spirit will have had no part: to you alone, therefore, must it be referred as received: to you all glory must be owed.

But if that was God's work, if it fell to you by his gift that you should see the truth: could not God have opened the eyes of anyone else as equally as yours? Or did he perhaps need the sharpness of your talent? [Marginal note: 1 Corinthians 14.] Or did he owe you anything more than to anyone else? What is it, therefore, that you say, Unless they had learned from us, they could not make even a word about the Gospel? But lest that should happen, that whatever comes into the light which has not issued from your workshop should at once be thought suspect: as if for that reason it were certain enough that it could not be but evil, it would immediately come into your mind to think: Aha, what new thing has Satan devised? And you would not headlong set yourself against a thing not yet known. Rather you would think thus with yourself: The Lord has indeed dealt most kindly with me, who has revealed to me very many things, and those very glorious: what if he has also revealed to this man something which he has still hidden from me? Let us ask of the Lord that, if what this man says is from him, he grant us that we too may attain them with a grateful and reverent spirit. But if they are human inventions, and therefore alien to the truth, may he grant us to despise them as empty and of no worth, and not suffer them to slip into our minds.

But if the matter stands so (as it certainly does), that unless the Spirit of God had shone upon you, you could have seen no more than any other man: and that by that benefit he could have bestowed favour on any other man just as much as on you: and moreover that it is equally easy for him, whatever he wills, to teach this man or that without any distinction, nor did he owe you anything more than he owed anyone else: how does it come about that you are so inclined to despise whatever proceeds from another, that you at once hold it suspect? Nay rather, that you hold it as condemned? And that you think it ought altogether to be cast aside, the matter being still unexamined? Nor can you bring yourself to so much patience as first to listen, and to weigh it? Assuredly there is no other cause, except that you attribute everything to your own talent, your judgment, and your watchings. And since you are, in your own eyes, like a kind of phoenix, with whom no one can be compared, the talent of any man whatsoever must necessarily be held in contempt by you. And since we have fallen upon a subject very necessary indeed to be known, it will be worth the effort to pause here a little. It happens in other times, and in our own age also, that among men whose godliness, rather than their learning, had been well proved, controversies about the doctrine of religion have arisen: and indeed that no concord could easily be joined, so that disputes have rather daily grown, and broken out afresh, more and more.

What then is to be said here? Where the same thing is both asserted and denied, the truth cannot be spoken on both sides: so that one of the two parties must necessarily be in error. What, I say, shall we say of the party that is in error? Does it never flee for refuge to God, that he may bring it help and impart his light to it, so that, with him for its guide, it may judge rightly of things? That indeed is not likely: nay rather, it is agreed that this is done, if nowhere else, at least in public prayers. What then? Perhaps God does not stand to his promises, in giving his Spirit to those who ask? Not even that can be. What, finally? Do those men ask without confidence? It may perhaps be done without the greatest confidence, but that it should be done altogether without any is not credible: and yet it is a wonder that they never obtain the light of the truth. Assuredly there can be no other cause of this thing, except that, although they seem, while asking light and the Spirit from God, to acknowledge that they lack it and have need of it, in fact they do not acknowledge it. Nay rather, they seem to be in that persuasion — a hidden one indeed, but yet most firm and constant — that they think they have already made so much progress that they can no longer err, and that nothing remains for them still to learn, at least certainly nothing for which they need be helped by the aid of any man.

Hence come those words which are told of a certain man: "Let no one enter here, except for the sake of learning." Finely said. Did this man seem not only to have tasted something of the tree of the knowledge of good and evil, but plainly to have devoured the whole tree itself, as great as it was? To this man indeed, who is already, in his own eyes, the likeness of God, and who asks from God that in which he thinks he already richly abounds — what Spirit, what light, would God give? It can also happen that these men do indeed ask, but not for the sake of the matter which has come into controversy — since they think they know it most certainly — but in general for the sake of all things: and not so much because they understand that anything is lacking to them, as because, from a certain inveterate habit, they pronounce the words not so much attentively with the heart as perfunctorily with the tongue; and what they do upon one point placed in controversy, the same they would do upon any other whatsoever that should afterwards be called into doubt. Whence it comes about that, although these men seem not to deny that in general they have need of greater light, yet a time can never come at which they understand that in some matter present before them they lack it, and on that account ask for it with some earnestness of mind.

Persuasions of this kind, moreover, we have received by inheritance from our first parents: since we are born to arrogance, to pride, and to rashness. There is added to this, that man uses such industry in all things of this kind, that if he has observed anything to happen frequently in a certain way, and has never noticed it to happen otherwise, he makes the conjecture that it always happens so. Wherefore a man whose eyes have been opened, so that he can discern many errors, both ancient and recent, persuades himself that he can judge of all things in the same way, without any suspicion of error. And since that conjecture, taken by the same reasoning by which it is taken in all things, tends whither by his own nature man is most of all carried — namely, to his own praise and dignity — it ought not to seem a wonder if it strikes the deepest and firmest roots in the mind. But if there also comes some breath of popular favour (as indeed it cannot fail to come), as it were a certain and firm testimony to the man's divine wisdom, that indeed comes to pass — unless divine prudence meets it in time — utterly to drive a man mad in his opinion of himself. But what shall we say of the peoples? O a thing altogether wonderful! God will raise up a man who shall show them in what errors they are engaged: and every discourse of that man shall have this for its opening, that trust is not to be placed in men — since they can both deceive and be deceived, and indeed for the most part both are deceived and deceive — nay rather, that not even much is to be granted to the judgment of a whole people, nor of many nations together, nor of many ages, since you may nevertheless be deceived if you put your faith in it: but that there is one voice of God, in which you may safely rest without any suspicion of error. Those who at last give ears to that divine man — what will they do? What?

"'Pape!' they will say, 'this man, who wills to transfer all our trust from men to God alone, and that faith be given to God alone, must necessarily be a holy and a godly man. How worthy is this man, this very man, for us to follow: to him we can safely give full faith: in him we can trust: not in those who were wont to lead us astray, so as to carry on their own business.'" And so, while they both think and say this, they nevertheless seem to themselves to be obeying the right counsel of the holy man, in transferring their faith from men to God, and they do not notice that they are transferring it from men to a man: whose discourse was not of this kind: "Do not put faith in those men or these, who teach you only human inventions: but in me, who will teach you nothing but divine things" — but this: that you are to believe no men at all; and since he himself also was a man, on that condition not even he was to be believed, unless he had confirmed the things he brought by clear proofs from the divine writings; for so they would have believed not so much him as, much more, those divine testimonies which he had brought.

But if this man could in no way see all the errors that were in the church, or perhaps fell into some new ones (which it is agreed happened to certain men), the people — led on by almost the same reasons by which that man had slipped into excessive confidence in himself — will on that account have made a god to themselves out of him: and will now have no ears with which to hear those who wish to turn aside even a foot's breadth from his teachings. But if anyone objects to him that so much is not to be granted to any one man, whoever he may be, but that all his teachings must be judged by the word of God, and that the very man whom he follows taught so, he will profit nothing. For although he can now hear nothing besides the decrees of his own teacher, you will nevertheless never bring it about that he perceives that he rests wholly upon one man, not upon the word of God. But this is as yet nothing. Let there be some other man who has noticed some fall of that first man, who has marked his moroseness and his excessive confidence in himself, and also the delusion of the people — who, while they seem to themselves to have transferred their faith from men to God, have transferred it from men to a man — and who has also learned by experience how much inconvenience that thing carries with it: wherefore he will have done everything to the utmost to free that people from such a kind of enchantment, as it were, and to open an entrance for the truth to them: finally, he will have shown all these things to his own people, and so shown them that they wonder at nothing more than at that so great power of error.

But what, in the end, will he himself do? He will, I think, take care much more prudently — namely, lest he himself commit the very thing which he condemned in another. He will warn his own people not to give him more faith than is fitting, since he is a man, and therefore liable to error — nay rather, since it can scarcely be that he is not ignorant of many things — and therefore to give him faith only so far as he shall have brought certain and clear testimonies from the divine writings; but if he notices anyone holding him in excessive honour, he will rebuke him: as Paul and Barnabas did, when divine honours were offered to them. Alas — will he do anything of these things? Unless a great force of the Spirit compels him, do not persuade yourself that he will. Nay rather, he himself also, when he thinks that he is the man who knows all things, who cannot err, into whom God has so poured out the boundless treasures of his wisdom that he has left himself nothing remaining wherewith to endow others — who, in short, equals God himself in wisdom — will on that account wish to shut heaven, so that not even a drop can rain any more: he will suffer no water to be thought good, unless it has flowed from his own fountain.

Yet although sometimes some word escapes him which displays something of modesty — as when he asks that whoever catches some error in his sayings or writings should warn him of it, that it will be very pleasing to him, or something of that kind — if ever it comes to the test, you may straightway see that those words proceeded only from the surface of his lips, however much he perhaps thinks he uttered them very much from the heart, and seriously. Those moreover who have followed this man will make a god to themselves out of him, just as others had done out of the former; and those who wonder at this with the greatest astonishment, as if nothing more absurd could be thought, will nevertheless in the end themselves do the same, or even much worse: so great a labour is it for any man not to set either some other man, or himself, in the place of God — although he thinks that he is doing anything rather than that. And these examples indeed occurred to me, by which I might show that it comes to pass that a man does one thing, and seems to himself to do another; that he has his trust placed in one thing, and persuades himself that he has it in another; and that, in discerning his own thoughts, he is blinded in a wonderful manner.

And I do not know whether something else is to be added: that those very men who, discoursing of the Christian religion, confess that salvation comes to men by the grace of God alone for Christ's sake, can afterwards, when their trust is to be placed, scarcely cast down their eyes from their own dignity and their own merits. This casts that suspicion into me: partly, that the natural sense of man bears it most unwillingly to be pressed down, and eagerly pursues its own praise and glory; partly, that I see — since it is a matter of so much difficulty to order one's life without disguise by the Evangelical rule — that man is so prone to devise other sanctities, by which he may please himself, as in gathering together (as we were saying a little before) the sins of others and condemning them with a severe brow, in delivering many and very wordy sermons on the Gospel, in treating insolently those who turn aside a little from the course and track of the received doctrine, and in the neglect of all care of the body, and in other things of that kind. For as to the Papists, who have devised so many ways of gaining eternal life, it is no wonder, among whom that opinion had already openly prevailed, that every man must be saved by the merits of his own good works.

Moreover, I strongly suspect that very many little notice that it is a far different thing to discourse on the doctrine of religion, and to say that salvation is obtained by faith and freely, and to be persuaded of this, from another thing: for each man privately to settle his own hope, and to hold himself persuaded that God, for Christ's sake, forgives him his offences and graciously receives him as a son. Since it can without doubt come to pass that a man holds one thing indeed as ascertained, and can discourse splendidly upon it, while the other he has not yet tasted even with the surface of his lips. Whence it perhaps comes about that not a few are deluded concerning their own trust itself, claiming for themselves a trust of which they have no taste. And would that the number even of these were not too great. Now, that we may in no way be deluded in any of those ways which we have so far recounted, there is almost one single safeguard: not only not to be ignorant in what the slippery error lies, but also to have it ready at hand and considered beforehand. Wherefore it will not be enough to have learned these things once: nay rather, they must never fail to be revolved in the mind: in such a way that on this side those snares of Satan — in other respects most hidden — are before your eyes, and on that side your manner of life, and your condition, and your own talent; and then also the times into which you have fallen; so that, whatever there is of that kind in which the depraved sense of man can have what it seeks, what it desires, what it may delight in, what it loves, what it values — even if you seem to yourself to be of such a mind that you think nothing of the sort can fall upon it, yet another is the spirit by which you are led, another the mark toward which you direct your counsels — do not flatter yourself, do not readily believe yourself: shake yourself out often, and again and again.

For you will find, more than once, that what you would have sworn was nothing but spirit was anything at all rather than spirit: and if you can scent out anything in any other way, let there come into your mind some deceitful dissembler, or a covetous man, or a proud, or an ambitious, or an envious man, or one desirous of vengeance, but one well known to you; and consider with yourself, if that which you are meditating, or attempting, or doing, that man had done — what mind would you suspect to be his, what counsel, what sense? Then return to yourself, and search out most diligently the secrets of your heart, whether there is in you any trace of such a sense; and if any trace appears, impressed even most lightly, do not despise it: but search more and more, for you will assuredly find what you will wonder at. And by as much as you judge God's glory, and the best state of the church, and the hope of everlasting life, to be worth prizing, by so much must you have applied yourself seriously to meditating upon all these things.

But that a man, knowing and prudent, should offend against the law of God [Marginal note: How it comes about that many sin knowingly and prudently.] is brought about chiefly by the nature, depraved by the fall of our first parents and spread abroad into all men, which in all things is utterly hostile to the divine law. For first, the providence of God does not satisfy it: since he seems to it both to act somewhat unwisely, in bestowing ample wealth and empires on those who can neither use the former nor rule the latter prudently, and somewhat unjustly, in that he prospers wicked men, presses down the good, allows them to lie exposed to the injuries of abominable men, and does not avenge, as soon as possible, the injuries inflicted on them. And although God would have him lift up his eyes from the humble things of this life to heavenly things, and be intent — in heaven, not here — on heaping up treasures for himself, yet he himself clings wholly here: here he would fain be well off: here he would be furnished most greatly, and in the best way, with those things which can not so much be discerned by the mind as handled by the hand. God commands him to cast away all his lusts, and to tear them away from himself: but he himself would much rather fulfil them, even most abundantly. God requires of him love toward his neighbour: he himself would much rather measure all things by love of himself, and think of his neighbour in no other way than how he might subject him to himself, and use him, and all his goods, for all his own conveniences, at his own mind's pleasure, as though they were his own goods.

And the same thing happens in all other things. But besides the fault of our common nature, there is also to each man his own proper fault: which, although it is joined together with the depravation of our common nature, can nevertheless be called each man's own for this reason, that different men are seen to be carried to different things with a certain greater force and impulse. But if there are any seeds of virtue in a man — as there altogether are — you will commonly see that whoever has partaken of one or another of them is destitute of the rest. The causes of this difference we have touched upon elsewhere. By a diversity of this kind it comes about that, if anyone has abstained from rapine and dishonest gains, he cannot command his lust; or, if he has tamed this, he succumbs to covetousness; if he is in any way free from these vices, pride will deceive him, or some other vice. Moreover, a man who in a humble fortune, in afflictions, has been high-spirited, when he has grown rich, shows himself somewhat timid. And on the contrary, a man who in prosperous circumstances has given many signs of godliness, whatever adversity is seen to threaten, will lose heart; nor will there be wanting one who fights indeed most strongly for a while, but at last, the conflict lasting too long, throws away his spear, as it were.

Very much also is placed in the opportunities of places and times: and great is the force both of provocations and of allurements, which either drive or draw us now into this crime, now into that. But this, beyond other things, makes a man negligent in resisting vice: that he is not altogether persuaded that God is as hostile to sin as he is, and that he will truly avenge it, and that another life remains besides this; of this life, however, a man never seems to himself that he will ever see any end. He may see many carried out to burial every day: he does indeed remember death, and discourses about it very subtly and very gravely. But he has nevertheless in his heart some spirit or other, he knows not what, which so flatters him: "Your time will by no means have been so near at hand: you shall not die." Though the number of his years grows, and though his strength wanes daily more and more, that evil spirit never ceases to promise a longer life, nor the man to open his ears to it. We seem indeed to ourselves to believe far otherwise, and our discourse on that matter is most elegant: but to human nature nothing of all things is harder to believe. Wherefore we must labour here day and night, so that at last this so great hardness of our heart may be broken.

But by what means chiefly shall we have brought this about? Certainly by no other than assiduous, and most reverent, meditation on the divine law, with most ardent and never-intermitted prayers to God added. And since danger never hangs over us so greatly as when we enjoy prosperous circumstances, it comes about that at that time also there is chiefly need of such meditations and of such prayers. But certainly, unless we join holiness of life with the godly doctrine of religion, it will come about that the knowledge of the doctrine will be snatched away from us, and that we shall again be delivered into the power of the seducer, [Marginal note: 2 Thessalonians 2.] who will impose on us, to our greatest disgrace and our utter destruction, whatever he wishes. And if the example of that darkness, out of which we have only just emerged, is not enough to deter us, I for my part do not see what could be enough. But we must strive, while we stand, not to fall: since, once discipline has begun to grow cold, it will scarcely be possible for each later day not to be worse than the former, until all fear of God is abolished and all shame perishes, so that at last vice obtains the place of virtue, and virtue that of vice. But among the other things that corrupt men's morals, I do not know whether bad examples do not hold the first place — of all men indeed, but especially of those on whom men's eyes are most of all turned: as of church pastors, and of others who, whether on account of the offices which they discharge, or their studies, or their age — add also, if you please, their fortune; but certainly, whatever external dignity or gravity is theirs — are thought to surpass others in wisdom.

But the best remedy for preserving discipline of morals is to be reckoned ecclesiastical censure. [Marginal note: Ecclesiastical Censure, useful for preserving purity of morals. Matthew 18.] For why should it not be, since without doubt it has the Lord for its author? Yet in this matter care must diligently be taken, lest Satan either mix anything in, or have anything of his own counsel in it. And a twofold end is not obscurely discerned in the Lord's institution, one of which either the admonition of a brother or censure is to attain: namely, either that you gain the brother himself, or that he thereafter be held for one with whom the church of God has nothing in common. In that place were formerly held foreigners, and publicans. And so let it come about that his dissolute life cannot be transferred to the disgrace of the Church, or of the doctrine divinely handed down, that no one think he can imitate him without contempt of his own salvation, and so that he cannot harm by his example, but rather be of benefit. Moreover, what the Lord wishes to be done for the sake of these things is by no means obscure: for him whom a brother has offended by some bad example, he wishes to be corrected by that brother, with witnesses removed; and if he has heard him, he will have been gained; but if not, he wishes one or another witness to be brought in; if he has heard not even them, he wishes it to be signified to the church; and if at last he has heard not even the church, he wishes him to be held for a foreigner and a publican.

Which things indeed are of such a kind that, if anyone has even the least of the Spirit, nothing can seem plainer. But Satan nevertheless seems to have found a knot in a bulrush: [Marginal note: But in the exercising of it, many are the frauds of the impure spirit.] when, if a man who had been rebuked had denied that he had committed what was objected to him, and the admonisher had called witnesses in whose presence — they being ignorant of that part of the deed which was objected — he should be rebuked again, and even so had not acknowledged his fault, and therefore the matter had come to the church, there were not wanting those who wished him to be convicted by such witnesses. Which indeed is very strange, that it could have come into anyone's mind: for since the accused does not deny that he was rebuked, but denies that thing — that he was justly rebuked — who does not see that there is need of witnesses who have certain knowledge of that very deed which is in dispute, not of that which no one denies? It also sometimes happens that another thing is alleged, in which the man who is accused does not understand that there is anything of sin, and, unless he acknowledges the fault, they think he is to be marked with censure — that is (as they speak), to be excommunicated.

How this can be done on any ground, I for my part do not understand. Shall I then, when I do not understand that I have sinned, be compelled to lie, and with my tongue acknowledge that fault which my heart does not acknowledge? And would it not be enough, if I obey those who admonish me so far as to abstain from that which they say is not lawful? What use, I ask, would the church ever have for a pretence of that kind? If you can, teach me that that thing was not free to me; but if it is such slowness of my understanding that I cannot attain it, where is your charity, who cannot bear me in my weakness? Nay, what too does that acknowledgement of sin mean? By what right, I ask, do you demand it? For certainly those words of the Lord, "If he will not hear you," do not seem to signify this — "If he will not acknowledge his sin" — but rather this: "If he will not obey," that is, if he will not thereafter abstain from that which is rebuked. And when he does not refuse to do that, what is there that you should require further? Nor indeed does that acknowledgement of sin contribute anything to the end for the sake of which admonition ought to be made. You could perhaps say: [it shows] in what a man sins, if that is done which ought not to be accounted free. What then? Must he therefore testify to that, who is not himself yet persuaded of it? A splendid testimony indeed, and one worthy that no one should put faith in it! But if not even this is a suitable cause for which such an acknowledgement is demanded, to what end then is it demanded?

Certainly no other cause remains, than that regard be had to the dignity of the rebuker: lest, namely, he seem to have rebuked in vain, or that those who are set over others seem to have gained the victory in whatever way, and that their authority be made formidable. For to this certainly man's nature aspires, even if sometimes he seems to himself to have nothing less in mind. Whatever it be, this butchery of consciences is certainly unworthy of Christian gentleness. And since, if a man, moved by the church, has repented of his sin, the Lord wills that he be forgiven, and that does not seem to be to be done unless there has been a suitable sign of repentance, almost all interpret this sign to be that he should publicly deprecate his fault, and this they call public penance. [Marginal note: Public penance neither necessary nor useful.] And so great was the severity of the ancients, that they would not fully restore some men, unless they had persevered for many years in that signification of penance; on some, penance was imposed even to the end of life: in which one may see how well they remembered that readiness to forgive which the Lord had commanded, who wills that a brother who sins be forgiven not seven times only, but seventy times seven. Since therefore that so great severity of the ancients was most of all repugnant to the Lord's teachings, we must diligently see to it, not that what we institute be less repugnant to them, but that it be in nothing at all repugnant to them.

But how can we have it assured that what we shall institute is in nothing repugnant to him? Certainly in this way only, if we judge that nothing at all ought to be instituted, without which the Lord's institutions cannot be fulfilled. But certainly, in order that he be restored who, having despised the admonitions not only of two or three brothers, but even of the church itself, refused to abstain from a thing unworthy of a Christian man, it is necessary that it be evident to the church that he has repented; and, in order that it be evident, they think that that public penance is necessary. But what if that could become known to the Church by some other means, and that a customary one, and approved by the divine voice? It will certainly be brought about that that public penance is not necessary for the sake of signifying repentance. But it can become known to her, if the neighbours, and those who keep company with the man who has been cast out, testify that he not obscurely shows forth repentance, and accuses his own deed. These things, you will say, can be feigned. What cannot be feigned? But I ask you, with what church do you wish the matter to be? Not with that in which there is some charity? You will not deny it, I think; but charity indeed, as we have taught, is not so very cautious, is not so suspicious: nay rather, it knows not how to think evil; it believes all things, it bears all things; but you nevertheless still laugh at such great readiness, and think it will be most foolish.

But I, if indeed this judgment is mine, do not refuse that you should laugh; yet charity was left depicted with such marks, certainly, by the Apostle, or rather by the Holy Spirit. But if your judgment is another, and indeed a contrary one, the thing itself speaks, even if I am silent, from what author that ought to be. God's Spirit certainly is not contrary to himself. What therefore is contrary to the judgment of the Holy Spirit must necessarily be from Satan. But besides that it is a sign of repentance, some allege another cause why public penance is demanded. For just as, if anyone has offended one man, he ought not to offer his gift before he has been reconciled to him, so, when he has offended the whole church, satisfaction ought to be made to her as a whole. And this indeed I freely confess here — unless satisfaction can be made to the Church in another way. Since she can hardly lay aside her offence unless she sees the sinner suppliant, it is the sinner's part, by whatever means, to soften the minds of those offended. But, just as no one would attribute Christian gentleness to a man who, when he has been offended by a brother, cannot forgive him before he has seen him prostrate on the ground before him, and suppliant, nor would anyone at all say that he does what befits a Christian man, whose readiness to forgive ought to be the greatest, so I for my part fear that that does not befit the church, in which, if those virtues which are required in the individual members are not discerned in the whole, it would certainly be in no way praiseworthy.

There, I say, the man who offends another is taught what he ought to do, that he may please God: there is no treating of the duty of him who has been offended, whom it is clearer than that there should be any need of citing testimonies that he ought to be most ready to forgive, and to cherish in his mind nothing less than triumphs. Thus we do not yet see any necessary cause of that public penance. But someone will perhaps add that it will nevertheless be useful, and even necessary, because a by no means common shame seems to be joined with it, so that by it, as by a somewhat firmer bridle, men are restrained from licence to sin. But here nothing besides human counsel is heard; the voice of God, which alone cannot deceive, is not heard. Or is it not in the same way that the Papists wish to persuade us of their auricular confession? For what reason do we reject it there? Certainly for that reason, that we think no place ought to be left, in matters of this kind, for human inventions, or for reckonings of advantages and disadvantages: I do not mention that he whom the thought of being cast out has not restrained will not be restrained by the remembrance of public penance either.

Well, you will say, let it indeed not be necessary: what evil, however, is there in it? I answer: even if we do not see what evil there is in it, it does not therefore at once follow that there is none in it. Moreover, even if it has no other evil, yet, since it was neither instituted by God, nor is necessary for the sake of any divine institution, there is evil enough in it, insofar as men usurp to themselves the authority of imposing any yoke upon men, which belongs to God alone: and it is to be feared lest, if they have transgressed the limits of their office even in some very slight matter, they gradually turn their mind to greater things, or become, by their example, authors to posterity of undertaking greater things: so it often happens that he who has once violated the laws of shame and honesty by stealing a thing even of the least value is thereafter gradually made more and more bold. That public penance is also certainly suspect to me, because it seems to have some weight for nourishing a Pharisaic spirit. For there is altogether in man, by nature, a certain elation of mind, by which each man seeks a kind of dominion over others, and authority, and eminence; and for that cause it seems a fine thing to each man to behold men suppliant, and humbly begging pardon.

For which cause certainly it will not seldom happen that, when a man seems to himself to demand these things for the sake of God's glory, he demands them led by private reasons or by private affections. In short, I think that nothing at all is to be added to that which has been commanded, but simply that what the Lord taught is to be done: that him who, having been rebuked by the church, has not obeyed, she thereafter not acknowledge as her own. But if he afterwards gives some signification of penance, and men worthy of faith testify to that, when that has been signified to the church, let him be in the same place in which he was before; and let that be done even with as little pomp of words as possible. But for what causes will there be place for censure? Certainly, whatever is rebuked in a man by the conspicuous testimony of God's word, unless, having been admonished, he is willing to abstain from it, seems to be cause enough for censure. In which matter, however, regard is to be had to human weakness: which I for my part think no one will rightly have, unless he who, while he is held by a true desire of obeying God, has also not been negligent in assiduously exploring his own weakness: here there is need of great charity, and also of prudence, without which nothing in this kind can be rightly done.

But I marvel that, although there is scarcely anything so unworthy of a Christian man as that by petulance of tongue he should harm another, or lie in wait for his reputation, and in no matter is sin more committed, yet I have never heard that anyone has been marked with censure on this account: and yet, as no other vice was to be tolerated, so neither is this in any way to be neglected, by which infinite quarrels, contentions, and enmities are daily wont to be stirred up in the church. But since those whisperers are generally very strong in the arts of flattery, they win for themselves the favour of those who have some power by authority; and by reporting now this man's sins, now that man's, and by detesting them, they hunt after a good opinion of themselves among them; and so they provide for themselves. Moreover, whom shall we say are subject to censure of this kind? The common multitude alone? What if those whom the Lord calls the Salt of the earth should be worthy of censure? Since it has been said of them, "If the salt has become insipid, its use thereafter will be none, except to be cast out, and to be trodden under foot by men," it easily appears that they also are subject to censure: and indeed it would be very absurd, if, when in the foot, or in another despised part of the body, no stain were to be tolerated, it had to be tolerated in the face, and in the very eyes.

But who will be the censors of the censors themselves? For suppose the whole ecclesiastical senate labours under infamy: would the church be compelled to connive, not only so far as to suffer them to be, or to be held, her members, but also to bear their unjust dominion? Certainly, since they now preside only in order to destroy the church, it must be possible for that to happen which the Lord says, that the insipid salt be cast out, and be trodden under foot by men. And since there are only two parts of the church, one of those who govern, the other of those who are governed, they certainly will not condemn themselves: it is necessary, therefore, that they can be condemned by the rest of the church. Moreover, this also must be known: whether it belongs to anyone whatsoever to rebuke a sinner, or whether there is some exception? But certainly he who himself has a beam in his own eye is not fit to try to draw a speck out of his brother's eye, unless perhaps in such a way that, when he has also accused himself to his brother, he exhorts him, together with himself, to amendment of life: for if there thus follows a not obscure amendment of the other whom he exhorts, such an admonition will be so far from useless, that perhaps no other could be more suitable. But neither indeed is he fit, who could be suspected by the sinner of a mind estranged from him, or who labours under any infamy at all: nay rather, in him who approaches to rebuke another, a good reputation is altogether required, but especially that he be greatly commended in the name of Christian charity and gentleness — a man to whom not a haughty brow, not a gloomy and severe countenance, not a slow gait, not any external help, but conspicuous holiness of morals, has won some authority.

If these things are absent, the use of this discipline will be more for irritating minds, and for filling the church with enmities, and for fortifying a road for church pastors to tyranny, than for amending morals. Yet magistrates ought not to make so much of the danger of these abuses as to hinder a thing instituted, without doubt, by the Lord himself. For what else would this be, than, as it were, to accuse the Lord of imprudence, as if he had instituted a thing from which more evil could emerge than good? That they should fear that a very salutary, and even necessary, institution will pass into abuse, there is great cause; and in that they will indeed have acted most wisely. But nevertheless, for a cause of suspicion of this kind, they ought not wholly to hinder it, but to examine from time to time how decently, how holily, it is used, and to resist the unjust and rash attempts of whomsoever, with great severity applied, and not to think the care beneath their dignity: let them themselves only repress the insolence of those who wish their own authority to be greater than it ought to be; and it will necessarily be that all things are right.

But many magistrates also do not permit the use of this discipline for this reason, that they themselves wish to live in the utmost licence, and meanwhile also to enjoy the name of Christian piety — men who will not escape the divine wrath. For whatever of disgrace overflows upon evangelical truth from the wicked life of false Christians — men from among whom the church cannot be thoroughly purged without the use of discipline — of that certainly they themselves will pay the penalties. And concerning the corruption of doctrine and of morals, in which the whole overthrow of Christ's kingdom, and the establishment of Satan's kingdom, is contained, let this much have been said thus far. But since it is not enough for Satan, if Christ's kingdom has been overthrown, to set up his own, unless he fortify that his own for himself, and strengthen it, in the next place it will have to be said what are the fortifications of Satan's kingdom.
