*The Stratagems of Satan. Book Three.*

Since we have said that the authority by which errors and false teachings are resisted is one thing in the Church, and another in the civil Magistrate: let us speak first of the Church. If therefore anyone [Marginal note: On authority, and first on the Church's.] teaches such things as are at variance with the teaching of the holy Scriptures, the Church can mark out two things, in order that they may not prevail: that it condemn the teaching as erroneous and ungodly; and that it cut the man off from the rest of the body of the Church, as a dry branch: in which things a fault can be committed in three ways. First indeed, if either a teaching or a man be unjustly condemned [Marginal note: But care must be taken that neither a teaching nor a man be rashly condemned.]: then, if it be done otherwise than is fitting: then again, if it be done by those by whom it ought not to be done. Something is indeed first unjustly condemned, if it is not false: by what means care must be taken that this does not happen can be known from the things that were said in the first Book. Then too it is unjustly condemned, if the judgment is heavier than the matter in question will bear.

For all the heads of true doctrine are not of one kind. Some are so necessary to be known that they must be fixed and undoubted for all. There are others which are controverted among the Churches, yet do not tear apart the agreement of faith. But if any error, since it is of this kind, is condemned just as if it took away the hope of salvation from those who err: the judgment will not have been right. [Marginal note: The necessary and certain heads of the faith are to be carefully distinguished from the controverted ones.] At this place this must be seen: whether it makes any difference if in this kind a judgment is given that is less than right. Most men, unless you are willing that every error, whatever it may be, is most grave, will say that you are giving protection to errors, and pleading the cause of those who err; whose godly zeal, indeed, for preserving sincere doctrine I praise: but it nevertheless seems to me worth the labour to know more certainly whether that is, or is not, to give protection to errors. Certainly indeed everyone, about every matter, either inclines in judgment to one side, or is in doubt. But he who is in doubt, if he is persuaded that in error there is placed a certain peril of salvation, when there is not, will be dismayed in mind. Which dismay so far is it from contributing anything to the judgment for judging rightly, that it even hinders it most of all. For every perturbation of mind weakens the judgment, and pours darkness over it: certainly indeed when it is joined with distrust of God's mercy toward oneself: in what respect does it conduce less to salvation? Or rather, what hinders more?

But what a window has been opened from this to Satan for storming all the hope of the wretched man, and for casting into him many very pernicious thoughts? I confess indeed that it can also happen that a mind so dismayed raises itself to God, and implores, and obtains, help for attaining a certain knowledge of the truth. But although it so happens to some: who will deny that to some others also that can happen which we said before? But those who have obtained knowledge of the truth, since the matter (as we have supposed) was not necessary to be known, are not freed from a very great danger: but those who open a window to distrust of God's mercy toward themselves: their salvation is engaged in the very greatest danger. Whence it comes about that, as far as concerns those who are in doubt about some matter, it seems that it can do more harm if they are persuaded that it is necessary to be known, when it is not, than if they had not made more of the matter than it is worth. But he who is persuaded, and in the worse direction: unless he strives, as much as he can, to persuade all others of the same thing which he himself seems to himself to understand, he will think that he will not have God favourable to him; and so he will indeed strive openly, if it is allowed: but if not, secretly at least he will not be able not to endeavour it, whereas he would attempt nothing of that kind, at least certainly not with so great an effort, if he did not esteem the matter of so great account.

And in those in whom indeed error has already struck roots: what hinders equally, that they cannot be called back from error, as if they think that they cannot be led away from their opinion without loss of salvation? For for that reason they do not dare to hear one who wishes to teach them better things. And when they refuse that: what other return into the way is there? Those who think rightly, I confess indeed, the more they think is placed in each matter, the less easily will they give ears to those who wish to call what they think into controversy. But if the matter is less than their opinion of it, their error in that will be exceedingly harmful: because those who err in that matter will separate themselves from the rest of the body of Christ, that is, the Church, will despise it, will execrate it: and they will be compelled to make a sect apart by themselves: than which nothing more destructive can happen. For we see how cruel enmities the sects practise among themselves. And hatred indeed brings it about that whatever is said by one sect is easily suspect to the hostile one: and that it contradicts it. And whatever has once slipped out through rashness, a wicked elation of mind, and zeal for contention, does not allow it to be retracted: but wishes to defend it obstinately. And when the custom has prevailed by which, on account of any dissension whatever, one man condemns another, you may see daily sects being born from sects: which multitude of sects, through despair of attaining certain truth, takes away from very many all thought of religion: so much so that, if anyone observes rather more carefully how greatly the multitude of sects, and the dissensions and hatreds that are among them, hinder the course of the Gospel, he can easily understand that nothing can be done more fitted to Satan's designs than rash judgments of this kind, from which sects arise.

It is therefore much better to make so much of each matter as it is worth, and neither to increase it nor to lessen it. Which opinion is to be loved the more for this, that since Christ himself is the sole preserver of pure doctrine, he has no need of any false persuasion for that end — for what protection would there be in a lie? Since this is so, it follows that some method must be sought which, by weighing the weight of things, will not allow us to stray from what is right. But first this must stand firm and fixed: if you have departed from the divine oracles, which are recorded in the sacred writings, there is nothing to which you may safely give your belief: there is nothing which is not liable either to fraud or to error; the divine testimonies alone are certain: alone worthy for the conscience to acquiesce in. Certainly also, if there is a controversy about some point of religion, so that some say that it is so necessary to salvation that unless someone understands and embraces it he can in no way be saved: others, although they otherwise agree about the matter, nevertheless deny that it is of so great weight, but rather think that those who think badly about it can attain salvation, and are nevertheless to be held as members of the Church.

If (I say) the controversy is of that kind, since both sides are not to be said to think rightly, it will be necessary that one of the two is in error. Wherefore to have it ascertained and thoroughly known which of the two thinks more rightly will be to know something: just as to stick in it, and to doubt, will be to be ignorant of something: it is therefore consequent that not even this can be known [Marginal note: Teachings are to be weighed rightly and with judgment.] unless divine testimonies about it are extant. But here this must altogether be observed, [Marginal note: And use here as your balance God's word alone.] that a teaching is one far different thing, and the weight and judgment of the same teaching is another. As it is one thing to say: A Christian man is not to be circumcised: it is another thing to say: If you are circumcised, Christ will profit you nothing. For one who had heard that — A Christian is not to be circumcised — could still have thought that, although a Christian ought not to be circumcised, yet if someone thought that he ought to be circumcised, that did not matter very much at all; but when that word has been heard — If you are circumcised, Christ will profit you nothing — let him now understand the matter to be of much greater moment.

And since a teaching is one thing, and the judgment and estimation of a teaching is another: if indeed it comes into question whether some teaching is necessary to be known, as that it is lawful to swear in a trial: he will have done nothing to the purpose if someone brings many testimonies, by which he has proved that an oath in a trial is not to be refused; for someone will say that he indeed grants that, and that he is not asking about it, but about the weight of the matter — whether or not the matter is of so great moment: as, if someone is persuaded that, whatever the cause may be, it is never lawful to swear, and perseveres in such an error, there is no hope to be had of his salvation. And so testimonies will be needed here, not which teach that there is an oath in a trial (that which is not called into doubt), but which teach that he who has been persuaded that it is not lawful for him to swear will be condemned — such a testimony as is extant about not retaining circumcision. Now it will be understood that something is taught, and proved, by a divine testimony, if the testimony itself cannot be true unless that also is true which was to be proved; which will happen whether the testimony expresses that very thing which was to be proved, or whether it states those things which, being laid down, that necessarily follows.

For example, it is to be proved that a man is reckoned righteous by faith, not by the works of the law: and those words of Paul are cited, "We reckon therefore that a man is justified by faith, without the works of the law." This testimony expresses that very thing which was to be taught. But let it be proposed to teach that a man is justified by faith alone, and let someone reason in this way. Unless there were one sole manner of righteousness, and that through faith, the Apostle would have inferred ineptly, from its being written that the righteous lives through faith, and that works are not faith, that the righteous does not live on account of the works of the law, that is, that righteousness is not in the works of the law. But an inept piece of reasoning cannot be attributed to the Apostle without crime. There is therefore righteousness in faith alone; therefore a man is justified by faith alone. Now the testimony that had been brought forward will not indeed be said to express that which was to be taught: but nevertheless to state those things which, being laid down, that necessarily follows. But if what is brought forward as a testimony can indeed be true, and yet not be that which was to be proved, it will not be said that it has been proved by such a testimony: wherefore if, in order to teach that the Roman Pontiff has authority to make new laws by which he binds men's consciences, someone cites that place, "Whatever you bind on earth shall be bound also in heaven," for that reason it will be denied that that place proves what is in controversy, because the power of binding could have been given to Peter, and yet the power of making new laws not have been given to the Roman Pontiff.

Moreover, something can be expressed both in general words, and by name. For since the general word "law" embraces both the law which is about conduct, and that which is about ceremonies, and divine laws, and human ones, that Christ will profit nothing one who has placed his righteousness in the observance of the moral law — by the testimony of the Apostle, denying that those who have their righteousness placed in the law have anything to do with Christ — will be said to have been expressed in general words. But that a man is justified by faith, in those words of the same Apostle, "We reckon therefore that a man is justified by faith without the works of the law," we shall understand to have been expressed by name. Further, I see that one thing can follow from another, in a way that is to the purpose, in two ways. And the first is this, if some matter is laid down as necessary to be known, and it cannot be understood and believed unless some other thing has been understood, or believed, before: for that other too will assuredly have to be held among the necessary things; so, since it is among the necessary things that we believe that Christ was raised from the dead, and we cannot believe that unless we also believe that he died: assuredly it will also be necessary to believe that he died. The other way is: if it has been expressed that that is necessary to be known which ought to have been the more doubtful whether it was necessary, or at least not less doubtful, and that will indeed be necessary which was either less doubtful, or not more.

But let someone ask: if it is established that something ought either to have been done, or to be, does it straightway follow from that that the same thing is necessary for us to know? I would make what I mean plain by a likeness. Those who labour under a hot disease, physicians cure it with cold medicines: nor indeed does it matter at all that he who is in the disease is ignorant of what quality those medicines are, if only he uses them. Since therefore we too were in a certain disease — liable, namely, to condemnation on account of sin — many things can be enumerated which, in order that we might be saved, ought both to be, and to have been done. For example, that our sins be abolished, and through a man free of all sin, and of immense power, and therefore God's Son, and likewise God, and the like. But let us suppose, among other things, that he ought altogether to have been accused, and, condemned in a trial, to have suffered death. Let someone ask (I say): whether, as it was necessary that the Lord, condemned in a trial, should die, it is also so necessary to have it ascertained that he died not cut down in any way whatever, but accused, and condemned in a trial, or whether we may be allowed to be ignorant of this, just as much as it is not necessary for one labouring under a hot disease to know of what quality are, or where, or in what way were prepared, the medicines which are handed to him.

Certainly, however, in order that it may be established that these things also are necessary to be believed, one must either have a testimony from the divine writings which declares that whatever ought to have been done must also necessarily be believed (in order that a man may be saved): or it must be said that, from its being declared that something ought to have been done, it follows that it ought to be had as known. But a testimony which declares that whatever ought to have been done must also be believed to have been done — none, so far as I know, is extant. But whether, from its being declared that something ought to have been done, it also follows that it must necessarily be believed, may be known in this way: suppose it written thus: "It was altogether necessary, in order to abolish men's sins, that Christ, condemned in a trial, should suffer death. If he had met death otherwise than as condemned in a trial, his death could not have been profitable to man." But someone wishes by this testimony to prove that unless a man has believed that Christ died condemned in a trial, he cannot be saved. It is asked therefore: whether or not that has been proved by a testimony of this kind. And from this it indeed appears that it has not been proved: because that which has been brought forward as a testimony can be true, and that which was to be proved not be. For the statement which contradicts the one that was to be proved does not contradict the one that has been brought forward as a testimony.

For, since it is one thing that Christ ought to have died condemned in a trial, and another to believe that it ought so to have happened, he who says that one who was ignorant that Christ died condemned in a trial can still be saved does not contradict him who says that Christ ought to have suffered death condemned in a trial; and nothing prevents both statements from being true. Since this is so, it cannot therefore be understood to have been proved by that testimony that he who has not believed that Christ died condemned in a trial cannot be saved. From which it is brought about that, for proving that something is necessary to be believed, he does nothing who has proved that it is, or ought to have been done. [Marginal note: And about those things which follow from principles.] Let someone also ask: if something is established by God's word to be necessary to be known, ought whatever can be gathered from that thing by necessary consequence likewise to seem necessary to be known? In which matter indeed this must be observed: since (as we have said) a teaching is one thing, and the judgment of the teaching itself is another, so it is one thing for some teaching to follow, and another for that to follow which expresses, in a statement, the judgment and the weight belonging to a teaching; but this latter kind of consequences cannot be called into doubt.

For since it is written, "Christ will profit you nothing, you who have your righteousness placed in the law," and the ordinance of circumcision is a law: if someone said that it can happen that Christ nevertheless profits one who has placed his righteousness in circumcision, he would contradict Paul's words. But the question is about those things which follow the teaching itself that is necessary to be known. Since therefore to lay down the antecedent, and to take away the consequent, is to speak things that fight with each other, he who denies that which altogether follows a thing necessary to be known would not seem in truth to believe it, and therefore could not be saved. But I answer: when I grant that the antecedent is also necessary to be known, but deny that which you think necessarily follows from it, why am I not likewise said only to seem to take away what I do not in truth take away? Since, I say, these things cannot stand together — the laying down of the antecedent, and the denial of the consequent — why should the denial of the consequent seem rather also to deny the antecedent, than, the antecedent being granted, also to grant the consequent? For it is just as certainly true that, the antecedent being laid down, the consequent is laid down, as that, the consequent being taken away, the antecedent is taken away. But it is plain that one who understands that this follows from that cannot at the same time lay down that, and deny this.

But if someone, while he thinks the antecedent to be true, does not admit the consequent: that will happen for this reason, that he does not see that this follows that; for to see that this follows that, and to have that persuaded to oneself, and not to have this, would be nothing else than both to see and not to see the very same thing — which cannot happen. But that it can happen that someone does not understand that that which necessarily follows some other thing does follow it, is so certain that nothing is more so; for there are many things, both in mathematics and in other subjects, which, although they are most firmly demonstrated, yet many who do not hold their demonstrations both are ignorant of, and do not believe. As, for example, the body of the sun being many times greater than the whole globe of the earth. But if anyone could lay down things contrary to those which are demonstrated — for example, that the body of the sun is not greater than the earth — he would have laid down things which fight with statements so clear and certain that no one can fail to understand that they are true, namely with those from which those things are demonstrated. But they give belief to those clear and certain statements for this reason, and do not give it to those things which are gathered from them by necessary consequence: because they do not understand that these cohere with those by an indissoluble bond; for unless that were added — since no one does not have the truth of the principles, from which all demonstrations are made, as a thing known — whatever can be demonstrated, all men would necessarily know without distinction: and so all would be geometers, all arithmeticians, all musicians and astrologers.

And since it can happen that someone does not see that what follows from something else does follow from it, and accordingly is persuaded of that thing but is not persuaded of this, it will surely not follow at all, from the fact that someone denies a necessary consequent, that he seems not to believe that the antecedent is necessary to be known: especially if he has been so firmly persuaded of the antecedent that he could more easily also admit the consequent than deny the antecedent. But if he would rather deny the antecedent also than admit the consequent, then surely something else would have to be said. At least, that would certainly not be firmly believing the antecedent: it is therefore clear that the testimonies which have taught that something is necessary to be known do not also teach that whatever things follow upon that thing are likewise necessary to be known. This passage has a subtlety, and, in order to be understood, it demands an attentive reader; but it is very necessary to be known, for the sake of removing the rash judgments on account of which the Church is torn apart into sects.

But these things having been thus set out, it follows that we should inquire what things are set before us in the divine writings as so necessary to be known and to be believed that whoever has not known and thoroughly perceived them is not to be held to be among God's servants and the Church's members, whereas whoever has embraced all of them can be believed to be able to be a partaker of salvation, even if in all other things he strays far from the mark. On this matter I myself would indeed much rather have heard the judgment of others than have subjected my own to the hasty, and perhaps insufficiently scrupulous, censure of many; but the hope of tranquillity and peace for the Christian churches nevertheless weighed much more with me than any private consideration. It is not as if we were prescribing decrees to anyone, from which it would not be lawful to dissent; but what the Lord has deigned to open up to us, that we have thought should be brought forward in public in such a way that we are ready, with a most willing mind, to believe someone who brings things that are more certain. Our aim is certainly not to thrust our own opinion forward by any means whatever, and to wish it to prevail, but rather that the truth itself should be brought into the light. For just as it not rarely happens that a musician, whom you could not by any entreaties have persuaded to play upon the strings, is brought to do so if some unlearned person has taken up an excellent instrument and clashed upon it crudely in his ears, so we hoped that it would come about that, if we seemed to have said something in a matter which, beyond all controversy, is of the greatest moment, we should at least stir up someone endowed with a greater spirit and wisdom, who would himself more successfully accomplish what we had attempted with too little success.

Meanwhile, we ask again and again of all godly and good men that, whatever their judgment about our opinion may be, they nevertheless take our zeal for peace and concord in good part — and not zeal of just any kind, but a Christian zeal for the good — and that together with us they strive, with the most earnest prayers, from him who, in his care for every good thing, is also the sole author of true peace, that he may one day deign to bestow them on his Churches, for the glory of his name. Let us therefore weigh carefully, and thoroughly examine, those passages of the sacred writings in which salvation is either promised on account of faith, or denied on account of unbelief. The Lord says: Be most certainly persuaded of this, that he who hears my word and believes him who sent me will enjoy eternal life and will not come into judgment, but has passed from death to life. [Marginal note: Io. 5.] Again: This is the will of him who sent me, that whoever sees the Son and has believed in him shall obtain everlasting life. [Marginal note: Io. 6.] And again: Go through the whole world, announcing the Gospel to all; whoever has believed and has been baptized will be saved, but whoever has not believed will be condemned. [Marginal note: Mar. vlt.] How these passages and the like are to be understood can be doubted. For someone may ask: what, then, is it to believe God's Son and his Apostles? For indeed that man also certainly believes who is persuaded that the one who speaks is truthful, even if he does not understand what he says. In which way there is no one among all Christians who does not give faith to the divine writings to this extent — that he does not doubt that whatever is contained in them is altogether true — even though the true sense of all of them has not been thoroughly perceived by him; perhaps there is no such man, and there never has been. That man is said to believe much more, who, while he is persuaded that the speaker is truthful, also understands what he says. But that whatever each man understands God's Son to have taught, whether by himself or through his Apostles, ought to be held as most certainly true — so much so that it cannot be called into doubt without ruin — I judge to be beyond all controversy. For these things could not stand together: that someone believed Jesus to be the Son of God, and meanwhile was not persuaded that he is truthful and adorned with every most excellent virtue. But whether it is also necessary, in order that we be saved, that we should have a right knowledge and understanding of all the things which the Lord taught both by himself and through his Apostles, so that we embrace all those things not only in a confused way — that is, by a general faith — but also distinctly, is the question.

In any case, it must necessarily be either that all the things which have been divinely handed down are all so necessary to be known that none of them may, without ruin, be either unknown or wrongly interpreted, or that there is some distinction among them, so that some are altogether necessary to be known, while others, although they are indeed useful to be known, and it is not lawful to make little of them, are nevertheless not necessary to such a degree — indeed, even if some error occurs concerning them, the man who errs can still be saved. And indeed the Lord testifies [Marginal note: Ioan. 17.] that he teaches nothing except what he had received from the Father, and that what he had received from the Father he had handed down to his disciples, and that they had embraced those things, and had believed that he had been sent by the Father; and so he prays for their salvation, and also for the salvation of all those who were going to believe through their word; and to the Apostles it was said: Go through the whole world, announcing the Gospel to all, and teaching them to keep whatever I have commanded you; whoever has believed and has been baptized will be saved, but whoever has not believed will be condemned. [Marginal note: Mar. 16. Matth. 28.] From these words it is hinted that, unless someone has attained all things and has given faith to them, he will not obtain salvation — just as Paul, when the keeper of the prison asked him what he had to do, [Marginal note: Act. 16.] had commanded him to have faith in the Lord Jesus, and that in that way he himself and his household would be saved; afterwards, it is related, he instructed him in the Lord's teaching, so that the sense of those words, "Have faith in the Lord Jesus, and you will be saved," is this: "Embrace the Christian faith and teaching, and you will be saved."

But this seems to be confirmed most of all by this, that the same Apostle writes: If anyone teaches us differently from what we have taught, let him be detestable. For whoever wrongly interprets words anywhere teaches differently. But, on the contrary, just as Paul — in order to teach that righteousness is not in the works of the law [Marginal note: Rom. 4.] — uses, among other things, an argument of this kind: that if the inheritance belonged to those who had kept the law, since no one could keep it, no one could have been saved; therefore, since the promise was not empty, another righteousness had to be given than the one that was placed in the law — so, in exactly the same way, it may be reasoned here: since no one among all men rightly understands all the things which have been divinely handed down, but it is necessary, in order that someone be saved, that he rightly understand all things and err in nothing, no one, therefore, will be saved.

But it is certain that the greatest, and indeed an innumerable, multitude will be saved; [Marginal note: Apoc. 7.] men must therefore be able to be saved even if they understand something less than rightly and are held by some error. And Paul testifies that in this life we do not know all things fully, but know in part, and prophesy in part. [Marginal note: 1. Cor. 13.] But what clearer thing could even be wished for? Before the Lord underwent death, he testified that his disciples had embraced the things which he himself, having received them from the Father, had handed down, and he prays for them; and yet they had not yet attained either that Christ's kingdom was spiritual, not worldly, or that Christ's benefit belonged to foreign nations as well. Yet those were things which he had already taught them, and they were of the greatest moment. It therefore appears that what the Lord said — that the disciples had received the things which he had handed down to them — he understood of the things that were necessary for salvation, not that they had altogether attained all the things which he had taught. And since they had not yet attained these things of which we have spoken, those things were indeed not necessary for their salvation. From this it follows that not all the things which Christ taught are so necessary to be known that, if someone is ignorant of something, he cannot be saved. And it is very worthy of note that, although Christ's disciples had not yet understood that the kingdom was spiritual, and that foreign nations also would be saved, or rather would be saved above all, nevertheless he distributed to them the symbols of his body and blood.

Since, therefore, it is more than certain that all the things which have been divinely handed down are not in the same place — but that some must be altogether known and ascertained, while others can be unknown without certain ruin — surely either there must be some general mark or distinction by which these are separated from those, or only those heads of Christian teaching will be ascertained to be necessary to be known about which there have been, specifically and one by one, certain testimonies of God's word that they are necessary. But as for any general mark or distinction, although I have diligently searched, I have never been able to find one. For if someone says that each of the chief heads of teaching is to be counted among the necessary things, although he will have spoken most truly, he will nevertheless have said nothing. For I shall ask at once: what, then, are those chief things? And if there is a controversy about some point — whether it ought or ought not to be counted among the chief things — I shall ask by what reasoning or mark I can know whether it is to be held among the chief things or is not to be held. It therefore appears that we are still turning in the same mud. For it is the same thing whether I ask what the heads of religion are that are necessary to be known, or I ask what the chief heads of religion are; and each of the two is equally obscure, so that one cannot be made clear from the other.

It must therefore be investigated of what things, in particular, the understanding and faith are required by name. The Lord says: Eternal life consists in this, that they know you, the only true God, and him whom you have sent, Jesus Christ. [Marginal note: Ioan. 17.] And the Prophet: Whoever has called upon the name of the Lord will be saved. [Marginal note: Ioel. 2, & Rom. 10.] And the Apostle: This is the declaration which we publicly have concerning faith: that if you have confessed the Lord Jesus with your mouth also, and have believed in your heart that he was raised by God from the dead, you will be saved. But that passage of John's is notable: "And Jesus," he says, "also did many other wonderful things in the sight of his disciples, which are not included in this book. But these have been committed to writing so that you may believe that Jesus is the Christ, the Son of God, and so that, believing, you may obtain life through his name." [Marginal note: Io. 20.] At which passage one may note: first, that whatever things have been written about Jesus tend to this, to make faith that Jesus is the Christ, the Son of God; then, that if someone has believed that through him he will obtain salvation, he will not be frustrated in his hope. Therefore, when Peter had confessed that Jesus was the Christ, the Son of the living God, [Marginal note: Mar. 16.] he is called blessed, since knowledge of that kind would have been from no man, but from God.

So Martha, after the Lord had said to her that he was the resurrection and the life, and that if anyone believed in him, even if he had died, he would nevertheless live, and that all who lived and believed in him would never die, [Marginal note: Io. 11.] answers: I believe that you are the Christ, the Son of God, who was to come into the world. And to the thief, who had asked the Lord that, when he came into his kingdom, he would remember him, he answers in this way: I assure you of this: today you will be with me in paradise. [Marginal note: Luc. 23.] And the eunuch, after he had confessed that Jesus Christ was the Son of God, is baptized. [Marginal note: Act. 8.] And when four men carrying a paralytic in a bed were also trying to bring him in to be cured, and when, because of the crowd, they could not, they had let him down through the tiles, with his bed, into the midst, before Jesus, Jesus — their faith having been recognised — said such a thing to the man: Son, your sins are forgiven you. From this passage one may gather what that paralytic's faith was. For he believed (as is fitting) that the man who was called Jesus was from God and was in favour with him, and so he hoped that through him he could obtain health. But how probable it is that he also had knowledge of all the things which the Church has long held as necessary articles of faith, I leave to each person to judge: There are also many other passages which tend entirely to the same point. For to the unchaste woman who washed the Lord's feet with tears it is also said: Your trust has saved you. [Marginal note: Luc. 7.] The same is said to that other woman, suffering from a flow of blood, who had hoped that, if she had only touched the hem of his robe, she would be made whole. [Marginal note: Luc. 8.] The same is said to that one of the ten lepers who had been cured, who had returned to give thanks to God. [Marginal note: Luc. 17.] And John says: By this you will know God's Spirit: every spirit that confesses that Jesus Christ has come and has been made man is from God; and again: Whoever has confessed that Jesus is God's Son, God remains in him, and he in God. [Marginal note: 1. Ioh. 4.] Paul also denies that anyone can call Jesus Lord except through the Holy Spirit. [Marginal note: 1. Cor. 12.] And since Abraham is called the father of those who believe, and we are taught chiefly by his example that righteousness is placed in faith, it is indeed worthwhile to see what that thing is which, when he had believed it, was reckoned to him as righteousness. [Marginal note: Rom. 4. Gal. 3.] There was promised to him offspring to be propagated into a most numerous people; there was promised that it would come about that all the nations of the world would fare well and happily because of his seed; finally, there was also promised to him the land of Canaan and the greatest prosperity. [Marginal note: Gen. 12. & 15.] But about those points of religion which they think it is not lawful to be ignorant of, the silence is wonderful.

Indeed, even the very mystery of salvation is promised through his seed in a very veiled and obscure way. Nor indeed do I find more things which the sacred writings express as necessarily to be believed in order that a man be saved. Let us now see, on account of unbelief, to whom salvation is denied. In general, indeed, it was said to the Apostles: Go through the whole world, announcing the Gospel to all; whoever has believed and has been baptized will be saved, but whoever has not believed will be condemned. From what was said a little before, it appears that these words are not to be understood as if it had been said: Whoever has not rightly understood whatever you teach, but has wrongly interpreted your words on some point, will be condemned; but by the name of Gospel there is to be understood the happy message of redemption through Christ's name, so that the sense is: Whoever has believed that he will be saved through Christ's name will be saved; whoever has not believed this will be condemned. In the same way are to be understood those words of Paul: If anyone teaches you differently from what you have received, let him be detestable [Marginal note: Gal. 1.] — namely, just as if he had said: If anyone teaches that we shall obtain salvation by another means than faith through Christ's name, let him be detestable. Although indeed also whatever someone teaches otherwise than has been handed down by the Apostles, even if it does not belong to the chief heads of religion, can be held accursed to this extent: insofar as every false teaching is from nowhere else than from the Devil, the father of lies.

But it does not immediately follow that that man is altogether outside the way of salvation; for the Lord also, rebuking Peter — Peter, who had dissuaded him from undergoing death — calls him Satan. But in particular the Lord says: Unless you eat the flesh of the Son of Man and drink his blood, you will not live. [Marginal note: Ioan. 6.] That is worth the same as if it had been said: Unless you believe that I am God's Son, and that I have been sent by God the Father, and that you will obtain salvation through my name, you will not live. And again: Unless you believe that I am he, you will die in your sins. And John: Who is the liar, he says, except the man who denies that Jesus is the Son? [Marginal note: 2. Io. 2.] And: By this you will know God's Spirit: every spirit that confesses that Jesus Christ has come in the flesh is from God; but every spirit that does not confess that Jesus Christ has come in the flesh is not from God. [Marginal note: 1. Io. 4.] Paul adds: If you are circumcised, Christ will profit you nothing. [Marginal note: Gal. 5.] And a little afterwards: You have no share with Christ, you who hold that your righteousness is placed in the law; you have fallen away from the benefit. And Peter: There is no other name under heaven among men through which we are to be saved. [Marginal note: Act. 4.] By these testimonies we are taught that those passages which testify that whoever has believed that Jesus Christ is God's Son will be saved are not to be understood in this way: as if, just as someone believes that Julius Caesar was the first man who, the Republic having been reduced to servitude, made himself sole master of things at Rome, or believes any other thing whatever which has nothing to do with himself — if someone, in the same way, has believed that Jesus Christ is God's Son, that man will obtain salvation.

But a man rightly believes Jesus to be the Son of God in this way: that he believes it as a thing which concerns himself: that is, that he believes that he whom men name Jesus is the Son of God, and his own Jesus, and his own Christ, that is, his own Saviour, and his own King, in whom alone he has placed all the hope of his salvation. If this explanation is not admitted, neither Paul's words nor Peter's will stand. These are the things which we have found, in those few passages indeed, which the divine writings declare to be necessary for every man to believe, in order that he may be saved: namely, that he acknowledge one only true God, and believe that Jesus Christ, whom he sent, is his Son, made man, and that he was raised by God from the dead: and that through his name he will obtain salvation, and not have his righteousness placed in the works of the law: indeed rather, that he be persuaded that there is no other name under heaven through which we are to be saved. Let us now examine what else there may be which follows from the things that have already been laid down, and is also believed to be necessary.

And since the divine writings teach that every man is liable to God's severe judgment — inasmuch as, conceived in sins because of the fall of our first parents, and endowed with a nature by which he never fails to offend God, he is guilty of eternal punishment — and since they also teach that there will at last be an end of this world, and that all will come to life again on the last day, the righteous indeed to everlasting blessedness, but the unrighteous and ungodly to everlasting torments: no one can understand what it is to be saved, and therefore neither what he ought to believe, unless he has also, to some extent, come to know his own condition, and the divine law, and God's purpose concerning the judging of the living and the dead. For the Apostle testifies that knowledge of sin arises from the law, and that he would not have known sin except through the law. [Marginal note: Rom. 3.] But it does not follow from the things that have been laid down that so great a knowledge, either of the divine law or of the human condition, is necessary: that if anyone has erred a little in the interpretation of some precept, or in estimating human powers, he cannot be saved. But it follows only that so much knowledge is necessary, that a man understand that he is liable to eternal damnation, from which he may then trust that he has been set free through Christ, and by his benefit destined to everlasting blessedness.

Since Paul also openly testifies that those who have placed their righteousness in the law — in God's law, I say — have nothing to do with Christ: what shall be said of those who have placed their righteousness in men's laws, in men's commandments and inventions? They will certainly have to be said to have fallen away from Christ's benefit much more. Since the divine writings likewise teach that salvation is to be hoped for through the name of Christ alone: it will follow that whoever has hoped that he will obtain salvation through the name either of the blessed virgin, or of Peter, or of Paul, or of any holy man whatever, has nothing to do with Christ. Since likewise it is placed among the things necessary to be believed that Christ was raised from the dead, and it cannot be understood that a man has come to life again who has not first died: it must certainly also be necessarily believed that he died. Since, finally, it is the Lord's command that all be taught the Gospel and baptized in the name of the Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy Spirit [Marginal note: Mat. vlt.], whose work this faith is — inasmuch as he opens our hearts, and makes this kindness of God toward us plain to us — both this command must be known, and that there is a Holy Spirit, through whom we believe, and through whom God dwells in us, who, finally, is to be sought by us from God with constant prayers.

What more I could show, by any testimony of the divine writings, to be altogether necessary to be known for salvation, I for my part confess that I do not find. If anyone can teach more, nothing would be more welcome to me than to understand what things are not those, and to hear what those testimonies are, by whose authority those things ought to be acknowledged as necessary. But that we should now enumerate, one by one, which things we think are not to be held among the necessary things [Marginal note: In other matters, cultivate brotherly concord.] is neither necessary nor expedient: and it would be an endless labour. For what is straight is the rule both of itself and of what is crooked. Only, so that it may be better understood by what method anything that comes into controversy can be proved either to be, or not to be, necessary to be known for salvation, we shall examine two doctrines: so that, as these are examined, all the others may be examined in the same way.

Sabellius (as Vigilius the martyr, bishop of Trent, reports) so acknowledged the Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit, that he would not have one understood as the Father and another as the Son. But he said that he who was the Father himself filled the womb of the virgin Mary, and in that very man whom he had taken to himself was called the Son, since not one and another, but the same Father was understood. And in this way one God was worshipped. Someone may therefore ask whether or not this matter is of such a kind that it is necessary for a man who wishes to be saved not to err in it: that is, whether or not Sabellius could have been saved with such an error, or whether he must altogether be held a heretic, just as he has always been held.

Certainly it has already been shown that it is necessary to believe that Jesus Christ is the Son of God. But to believe that he is the Son of God is not either to pronounce these words, or even in thought to ascribe to Christ the title of Son of God: but rather to ascribe to him, with sincere faith, that which the words signify. But the word "son" does not signify anything of that kind: the notion of this word, I say, is not such that anyone can be understood to be a son of himself. Just as neither can a father be understood to be a father of himself: but he is called a son who has a father, not himself, but another: and a father, who has a son, not himself, but likewise another. Since, therefore, Sabellius confused the Father with the Son, and denied that they are one and another: it is sufficiently proved that Christ was not believed by him to be the Son of God, which it was altogether necessary should be believed. By this example we are taught that, in those things which are divinely set forth as necessary to be believed, we must diligently consider what the words signify, so that we may not, while retaining an empty shadow of the words, deny the things which the words signify, but may sincerely embrace the things themselves.

This doctrine having been thus examined, let us take up another, which, as times now are, is perhaps not a little more worthy of consideration. There has long been a great, and truly tragic, controversy about the interpretation of those words, "Take, this is my body." Let us therefore see whether this matter is of so great weight that on account of it some ought to reject others, to harass them, to afflict them with every kind of insult, and to hold them in the place of enemies. It is not necessary, however, for me to disclose in this place to which of the two parties I belong: only thus far, indeed, do I profess myself to be of both: that I do not at all doubt that both parties belong to the true Church of God. But I shall deal separately with each party: and although I grant that both parties otherwise speak rightly, and interpret the Lord's words rightly, I shall nevertheless show that neither ought to condemn the other party, or to charge it with any heresy. Come then, so that we may make a beginning of speaking from this side: you who admit no trope in the Lord's words, but judge that they are to be understood just as the words sound — those who maintain that there is a trope in them you not only say interpret the Lord's words wrongly, but you also seem to hold them in the same place as those who think most ill of religion.

No word seems sufficiently atrocious to be at hand with which you may curse them according to their deserts. What if, even with so great and unspeakable an error, they may nevertheless obtain salvation, and belong to God's Church? How can they (you will say), for whom the Son of God himself is either a liar or unskilful, uttering absurd things, and things most utterly at odds with the judgment of reason? Ah, wretched men, to what do the enmities born of contentions drive us? Is it then, because the interpretation of words is called into controversy, immediately necessary to make Christ a liar? Who of those whom you have as adversaries in this matter ever denied that faith must be given to those words of Christ, or to any others? Indeed, to whom did it ever enter his mind to suspect anything of that kind? Certainly they would fear lest he be a liar, for whose name they could despise even the fiercest tortures of tyrants. Let that peevish, and altogether unfair, interpretation begone: the dispute is about the meaning of the words, not about their truth, than which they most gladly confess that nothing among all things is more certain. But (you will say) the words are so clear, so plain, and so open, that it cannot fail to be seen what the meaning is. To you who understand them so, they are open and clear: to him who thinks that their sense is another, they are not in the same way open, or if they are, they are open in another sense.

Therefore this must altogether be granted: that the dispute is about the interpretation of the words alone, not about their truth. Therefore you who say that those who deny the presence of Christ's body in the Lord's Supper are heretics, and have no share in salvation: by what testimonies from the sacred writings do you teach that? The Lord himself promises life to all who have called upon his name, who have believed that he is the Son of God, who have placed all the hope of their salvation in him alone, not in the works of the law, or in any other thing. This faith and hope those whom you make heretics profess most steadfastly: led by what testimonies of God's word do you, by your own judgment, take salvation away from them? Bring forward one word from the divine writings by which it has been declared that such men will not be partakers of eternal life. Certainly it is not lawful for you to declare that from your own head: or do you perhaps think that it cannot happen that a man calls upon the Lord's name, and has all his hope placed in him, even if he does not understand, and therefore does not believe, that his body is in very deed present and distributed in the Supper? Why not? But if you contend that it cannot happen that a man believes those things unless he has believed this, I would very gladly hear in what way you prove what you say. But if that faith, to which the Lord promises salvation, can stand without this confession of the presence of the Lord's body, and nevertheless you do not cease to hold those who cherish that faith as strangers to the true Church of God: see again and again lest you yourself truly defile yourself with the very crime which you are accustomed falsely to cast upon others, that they make Christ a liar.

I come now to those who maintain that there is a trope in the words, who, from the fact that Christ's body is said to be present in the bread, and at the same time in very many places, gather many very great absurdities: but especially this, that with such an assertion a denial of Christ's humanity is joined. Therefore, since this matter concerning God's Son made man, and raised from the dead, belongs to those heads of Christian teaching which it is not lawful to be ignorant of, they will deny that those who assert such a presence can be saved, unless they come to their senses. The absurdities which they themselves gather from the assertion of that presence, so that they judge those things to follow from it (whether they err in this or not), experience nevertheless is witness cannot in any way come to pass. Let us therefore grant altogether that all those absurdities follow from it. What then? Will those who assert that presence, for all that, be without Christ? It must be so (you will say): for that condition which is attributed to his body, that it can be at the same time in several places, clashes with Christ's true humanity: so that he who says that Christ's body is at the same time in several places does exactly as if he said that Christ's body is not a body, and that if Christ is not endowed with a true body, he is therefore not truly man, and if he is not truly man, he either was never made man or did not rise again, each of which we have shown to be necessary to be believed, and since no one can believe things that clash with one another, and it would be true that those men do not believe that Christ was truly made man and rose again, it cannot therefore be true that they believe it; and since they do not believe that which is necessary to be known for salvation, it follows that they cannot be saved.

These men do indeed press the argument sharply: but let us nevertheless try whether we can show that even with such absurd errors hope of salvation is still left. Therefore what they say, that it cannot happen that anyone believes things which clash with one another, is true only on this condition (as we have taught): if he understands that they clash. For I confess that no one can at the same time believe both that Christ is endowed with a true human body, and that he is not. But things which in truth clash with one another can nevertheless seem to many not to clash. And if they seem not to clash, nothing prevents men from believing them at the same time; wherefore, even if it were true that these things clash with one another, and that it cannot stand together that something should be a true body and yet at the same moment be in several places, they can nevertheless seem not to clash, so that there would be no doubt that they do so seem: and therefore it can happen that men believe that the Lord is endowed with a true human body, and yet that that body is at the same moment in several places.

Since, therefore, they believe, and most firmly believe, that Christ is a true man, and the Son of God, and that he was raised by God from the dead, and all the other things which the sacred writings set forth as necessary to be believed: why is it that you deny that they can be saved even in their own error? It appears, therefore, that both those who assert the presence of the Lord's body in the bread, and those who deny it — although it is necessary that one side or the other is in error — are nevertheless both (if otherwise they sincerely obey the Lord's precepts) in the way of salvation. From this it comes about that they owe one another charity and reverence, as to brothers, servants of God, and members of Christ. If they harass one another with reproaches, insults, and curses, if they go on practising hatreds among themselves, they will not escape God's severe judgment. Therefore I exhort both parties, and beseech them through Christ Jesus, that, laying aside enmities and rash judgments, they should strive with one another in the duties of charity, and treat controversies in a friendly and humane way.

Let curses, jeers, and contempt be absent: so it will most certainly come about that those who err will at last be drawn to the knowledge of the truth. If this union of minds has once grown together, the Lord will not deny us any favour or success whatever: nor will he allow the labour of those who, with true charity, have striven to call their brothers back from error to be spent without fruit. Now, in the way in which we have examined these two doctrines — namely, Sabellius's error, and the controversy about the presence of Christ's body in that bread which is distributed to those who trust — anyone will easily be able to judge whatever other matters may come into controversy. For whatever error is in question, it must be looked into whether he who errs in this way can nevertheless believe all those things with which the promise of salvation is joined. So that, if he can, he may understand that the error is not in a matter necessary to be known for salvation. If he cannot, let it be understood that it is. Moreover, whoever wishes anyone to be condemned for some error, however thoroughly he has ascertained that salvation cannot stand together with such an error, must look to it again and again.

For why? Since the Lord says, "Come to me, all you who labour, and are weighed down by a burden, and I will raise you up": will anyone be of such rashness that he dares to drag back one who is coming, and to say to him from his own head, "Hey, you! You come in vain, since you do not believe this and that; who are you, that you take this upon yourself? Who are you, that you should be a hindrance to another man's servant? With what confidence do you take this upon yourself, that you should divine the divine will, which has not been very clearly expressed to you? Will you indeed, by your own mind, set any bounds to the divine goodness, mercy, and kindness? He himself, whose it is to bestow life, calls all men to himself, and does not limit, by your — I know not what — acceptances, the grace which he promises: will you limit it?" But what are you doing, when you do this? Certainly, as far as in you lies, you take life away from a man: and not indeed this life, which is more truly death than life, but the everlasting life, and the kingdom of heaven, and you doom him to everlasting torments; and you do this wrong not to one man only, but perhaps to many thousands. You maim Christ of as many members; you tear God's Church apart into sects; finally, you pave the way for countless evils. And if the Lord threatens with such severity him who has been a stumbling-block to any, even the least, what do you think will happen to those who, by their rash judgments, have driven men to make sects, with no necessary cause?

But if the method handed down by us for distinguishing the necessary heads of religion from the non-necessary does not satisfy you: bring forward yourself, if you can, a better one, but one that is certain, firm, and constant. If meanwhile you wonder that among the things we have enumerated as necessary to be known some points of religion, although held in the highest place, are not counted: turn over diligently the whole Old and New Testament, and search out by what testimonies you could prove that those points are so very necessary to be known that no one can be saved unless he has attained them. Weigh how far they could have been known to the Israelite people from the books of the Old Testament, whom nevertheless it was necessary should be saved by the same faith as we. Weigh also what that thief could have had known, when it was said to him: "Today you shall be with me in paradise": or the eunuch, when he was baptized by Philip: or the unchaste woman, to whom, when she had washed the Lord's feet with tears, it was said: "Your faith has saved you": or that other woman, wasted by a flow of blood, or the paralytic, or so many others, to whom, at the sole confession of the Lord's name, sins were forgiven. It must also be observed what the reason is why, although the divine letters so often repeat the things we have taught to be necessary, and so often demand them as necessary, they do not likewise demand the things which they indeed hold of such great account.

If those books which we acknowledge as sacred (as indeed they are) had been written by human talent, we would say that good Homer had somewhere nodded. But they were written with the Spirit of God as author, who can neither have lapsed in memory nor have nodded. That was therefore not done by chance, but by a certain counsel of God: who, if he had wished those things to be in the same place, would at least certainly have left his will openly attested somewhere once. And since he has not done so, who is the man who could divine God's will? These things must be measured not by our own judgment, or by our own talent, but by the divine wisdom revealed to us from heaven, whatever reason may dictate: this alone is to be heard. [Marginal note: Care must be taken, however, not to condemn rashly either a doctrine or a man.] But if anyone errs in a matter which ought to be certain and thoroughly explored, and cannot by any reasoning be called back to soundness: no one ought to doubt that he is justly condemned, and cast out from the rest of the company of the godly. And not only if, having been warned not to do so, he goes on also to seduce others: but even if he wishes to keep silence; for he to whose faith eternal life has not been promised has nothing in common with God's Church. But if the point about which someone thinks wrongly is not of the number of those which are altogether necessary to be known for salvation: now I think a distinction is needed.

For if he who thinks wrongly, and cannot be led away from error, also tries to seduce others, although he has often been warned to desist: I do not see why he could not, and ought not, be cast out from the company. For he grievously offends the brothers; for it is not because a thing is not among the chief things that the error is therefore not pernicious. For just as from one branch of knowledge another branch of knowledge is drawn, so errors arise from errors: and what can there be which (since it in some way pertains to God's glory) is not of great weight? But if he does not seduce others, provided he holds the things that are necessary for salvation, although weak, he is nevertheless still in the number of the brothers, and so, if they cut him off from the Church, they cut off (as far as in them lies) a still living member from Christ's body — which would be an impious act, nor indeed does any command of God exist that it ought to be done. And let this much have been said about just and unjust judgment: now we must see how one may rightly and properly come to it, what is to be followed, and what things are to be guarded against. As far as doctrines are concerned, if a very pernicious error arises, there is no reason why we should delay, but it must be met at the earliest possible time, and the people must be taught (so that they may beware of it) what sort of doctrine it is. But the man is not to be cast out before all means of winning him, according to the Gospel's prescription, have been tried in vain.

And if it must altogether come to casting out, care must be taken that nothing be done from which any disadvantage could return to the Church. But that is not obscure: that the worse disposed anyone has been toward the Church, the more trouble is to be expected from him for it: namely, that he plot against it the more wickedly, and with the greater striving of mind; that he secretly seduce whomever he can; that he stir up hatred by slanders, and contrive other things of that kind. But the more bitterly anyone has been received, the worse disposed he will be. Wherefore, on my advice at least, a heretic will be condemned in such a way that, the case being simply set out, all reviling and reproach be absent, and it be done with an expression more of grief than of any anger or hatred, so that the judgment may appear to have gravity, but no bitterness. But above, when we were treating of the duty of a private man, those things were said which can no less be fitted to this place: and we showed that more disadvantage can always be received from bitterness of words than advantage. Finally, care must be taken that those do not arrogate to themselves judgment, whether of a doctrine or of a man, to whom it does not belong. For it is certain that this belongs to no private person: nor even to two or three.

Indeed, it belongs to the Church. "Let two or three prophets speak" (says Paul) "and let the rest judge." [Marginal note: 1 Cor. 14.] And in this way indeed the Church, by its authority, meets errors as they spring up; now we must speak about the Magistrate. [Marginal note: Secondly, the Magistrate.] The topic is a disputed and perplexing one, if any is, and one in which there is very much at stake. For those who shrink from the received doctrines, whether they themselves think better or worse, it is grievously hard not to be able with impunity to bring forward publicly what they think. On the other hand, those whose doctrine both the Magistrate and the people have now approved, and who have thereby attained authority, since they cannot without great trouble, and often also with some risk to their own reputation, hear controversies raised daily, cannot but think the use of the sword most necessary for repressing those who refuse to acquiesce in the received doctrine; and generally, as each thing is of use to each man, so too he most easily persuades himself that it is rightly done. Wherefore, since regard had to one's own advantage does not allow a man to judge rightly enough, his thinking must (as far as it can be done) be drawn away from reasonings of that kind; or, if that is hard to do, the next best thing is that each man at least in thought exchange his own position for the opposite one, and so enter into a reckoning of what seems most both expedient, and agreeable to the divine law.

So then, if either liberty or licence is going to be convenient for you, imagine yourself in the place of the man who approves the received doctrine and wishes it preserved, and that there are daily men who make trouble for you — men of no learning, no judgment, no spirit, without shame, daily clamouring, and being extremely troublesome; that about the highest heads of religion tailors, fishermen, butchers, cooks, little women, and boys argue and contend most confidently; that nothing is left untouched, everything is called into doubt, a deception is practised on the simple, and all things are filled with stumbling-blocks. If you have at least (as I said) been in that state in thought, and understand that these things happen, it could hardly fail to seem to you most necessary that such great licence be bridled. [Marginal note: Whether controversies of religion are to be settled by the sword, or an executioner is to be summoned against those who err.] But if you then also look at those passages of the divine letters which are believed to arm the Magistrate against the corrupters of religion, you will perhaps be much more inclined than before to that side, so that you think they bear on the matter. The man who judges that the doctrine commonly received is true and sincere, and thinks it advisable, in order to preserve it, to punish heretics with death, thinks that it can nevertheless happen that, while the pastors slumber, the doctrine be gradually corrupted, and the Church degenerate from its former godliness.

For as soon as any peace and tranquillity comes to it, discipline is gradually loosened, godliness grows cold, and a strange forgetfulness of God, and of his law, creeps in. And since God cannot leave that ingratitude unpunished, he withdraws his Spirit, and allows freer rein to the seducers, by whose arts and craftiness it comes about that, although the doctrine seems to remain the same, it is nevertheless gradually changed, and corrupted; but when the practice of settling controversies about religion by the sword has prevailed, whatever the doctrine in the people afterwards may be, whoever has dared to oppose that doctrine, whether rightly or wrongly, is to be held altogether a heretic: and to whatever testimonies from the divine letters, or arguments, he may bring forward, to all of them one man will answer — the executioner. And so it will come about that what had once been a just severity, with Satan at last ruling far and wide, turns into a monstrous and enormous cruelty: imagine, therefore, that you have fallen on times in which those who think rightly are not allowed with impunity to censure the commonly received errors, but if they have dared to utter anything, the sword, crosses, and fires are at hand, and there is no place for pleading a case; and let it also come into your mind that such a tyranny took its origin from the punishments inflicted on true heretics: surely no evil was ever inflicted on a heretic worse than such a tyranny introduced.

It must also be considered how often outcomes do not answer to wishes in the killing of heretics. For if anyone understands that he cannot safely bring his opinion forward publicly, he will not disclose it at any time, in any place, or to anyone, but will first sound out minds, will gradually dig underground passages, and insinuate himself, and if he sees that he is digging in a place not well suited, he will turn his plans elsewhere. But if he notices that ears are open to him, he will gradually go further, until he occupies his friend's whole mind, and he will do this now with one man, now with another; and those whom he makes sharers in his error, he will make almost the same eager also to corrupt others and yet others in secret, and to propagate the sect, so that before the Magistrate understands that one man has been seduced, a great part of the people has sometimes been seduced, and a wound has been received that is almost incurable. And since these things are done in secret, not even those who are set over the Church apply their remedies for uprooting errors — namely, the force of the word; for although they hear some rumours, they cannot clearly understand what is being done, and there is no one who reports by what arts the seducers proceed, or by what arguments they persuade men of their views.

For those who could, neither dare nor wish to; those who would wish to, do not know; and for the most part they report only their own suspicions, or inventions; and even if the pastors have something to bring forward for that purpose, since they are intensely hated, like tyrants of a sort, by those whom they ought to cure, all their labour is spent without fruit — especially if those things also are added which generally tend to be present, bitterness of words, and reproaches. Moreover, when pastors have placed their hope of preserving the doctrine, and of resisting heretics, in the sword, and do not see that they need great learning to defend it, they give themselves to idleness; and so gradually an ignorance of all things follows, so that afterwards Satan can thrust upon them any superstitions and errors whatsoever with no trouble. Nor indeed is that a thing to be made little of, that since it generally belongs to the godly to endure persecutions, disgraces, and afflictions for the sake of religion, by this, as by a most certain mark — especially if they bear them with a brave, cheerful, and constant mind, and their outward life is blameless — they strongly commend their cause to the simple; and, on the other hand, those at whose hands such things are endured can hardly escape the reputation of great injustice and tyrannical cruelty. From which things it comes about that minds daily become more and more embittered, and hardened, in error, and become altogether incurable.

Of which evils there is hardly any need to seek examples from the old histories. For there are too many before our eyes, which, if anyone considered diligently, he would scarcely have any reason to desire greatly the use of the sword for rooting out heresies. Finally, so that your judgment may be pure and sincere, you must so prepare yourself that you do not wish this side more than that, but wish that alone, to whichever side in the end it falls, which is more agreeable to God's word, and joined with his glory. And that indeed is certain: [Marginal note: Deut. 13.] that by the old law the penalty of death was appointed against seducers; indeed, if any city, seduced, had fallen away from the lawful worship of God to idols, the law ordered that city to be destroyed, the town to be burned, and never afterwards to be rebuilt. But there are some who think that law was in force only until Christ, and has no place in the New Covenant, taking their conjecture from this: that the Israelite people was a type of the Christian people, and had also promises of things falling under the senses, whose use would be in this life, and had such sacrifices — in short, all such things — which nevertheless signified invisible things, pertaining to eternal life.

Since this is so, they gather both that that bodily penalty was a type of eternal damnation, and that therefore this law, together with all the rest that were given for the signification of future things, ceased at the Lord's coming. Which conjecture has seemed to me so far from inept that I could not find why it could be rejected, unless the reason placed in the law opposed it. For there is in the law: "that all Israel, hearing, may fear, and may henceforth commit nothing of that kind." Which reason indeed is perpetually in force, so that, although the law itself has expired, the Magistrate nevertheless has just as much right to make a similar law as he has a right to make laws against murderers, adulterers, and other criminal men. [Marginal note: The parable of the tares is examined. Matth. 13.] But the Lord's words, at the place where he sets forth the parable of the tares, seem to persuade the contrary. For when the servants asked whether they should go and gather the tares, the master of the house is reported to have answered that they should not go, lest in gathering the tares they should also uproot the wheat. Since others interpret that place in other ways, it can be understood how much it matters to seek out testimonies — not so that you may establish a judgment already fixed in your mind, but so that, by examining them considerately, the truth may become clear. But there would be a way to the discovery of the truth, if, when an exposition had been found which seemed to favour your opinion, you took the part of an opponent, and diligently searched out what could be said against it, and did not applaud yourself at the first opportunity.

In that way it would come about that those who disagree among themselves would not disagree forever, but those in error would at last believe those who think rightly. But we are generally more anxious about victory than about the truth; and since the loosening of the knot proposed seems to lie altogether in the true exposition of this place, care must be taken again and again that no partiality dazzle our eyes, and that we spend no too little diligence, or time, in weighing it. Indeed, that interpretation by which some say that Christ does not here take away every kind of severity, but warns that evils which cannot be corrected without ruin must be tolerated, does not seem agreeable to the words of the master of the house: for he does not say, "Come, go"; but, "See again and again that you do not uproot the wheat also together with the tares"; but he forbids going at all. And he wishes both the tares and the wheat to grow until the harvest. From which it appears that great force is done to the words by such an exposition; nor indeed do those seem to expound it more suitably who wish that what is treated here is the purging of the whole Church, which the Lord commands to be deferred to the time of the last day: but that particular purgings of churches are not on that account forbidden.

Nor indeed does it seem that we need to examine at length how truly angels are understood by the servants of the master of the house; rather, let us look with intent minds at what is forbidden. For from this it can also easily be clear to whom it is forbidden. And it is indeed agreed that the wheat is the godly, the tares the ungodly: but whether every kind of ungodly men, or only a certain kind, we shall see afterwards. And the field indeed is the world. To gather the tares out of the field, therefore, is to kill the ungodly, and to remove them from this number of those living in the world. But the Lord forbids this to be done, and wishes them to grow together with the wheat, and not to be separated from it before the time of harvest. But (you say) he forbids a universal purging of the field to be made, not particular purgings of individual churches. What then, if men are so diligent in making those particular purgings that no church is left unpurged? Will a universal purging not have been made in that way? But you deny that this can be done: yet imagine that it can: you must admit that, if no particular church is left unpurged, a universal purging will have been made. But if that were to happen, or the time of harvest, it would happen contrary to what the Lord had commanded, since he did not will it to happen before the time of harvest: he certainly did not will it to be done by men any more than by angels.

But if any magistrate undertakes, not indeed the universal purging, which he cannot carry out, but that which he can: what else will this be than to act, as far as he can, against the Lord's will and command? And certainly, however small the purging may be, it will be a part of the universal one: so that he who has done as much as he could must be said not to have been the reason why he did not carry out the universal purging. From which things indeed it follows that this exposition cannot be approved. Others will have it that it is forbidden only that the tares be removed by war: but since the words can equally be fitted both to that way of pulling up the tares in which they are pulled up by punishing individuals, and to that in which they are pulled up by war, the man who will have it that the householder meant only the second, without bringing forward a certain reason why he should so interpret, must be said not to interpret, but to divine. For unless a certain reason persuades otherwise, the words are to be taken as they sound: so that if they are general, we are to understand the sense also to be general: if the law does not distinguish, neither are we to distinguish. In what way, then, shall we have expounded this similitude? "Those words, *Let both grow together until the harvest*, could not" (you say) "be understood, as they sound, so that the impious are to be allowed to grow just as the godly are: up to this point I do not oppose it."

For I see what absurdities follow: since all authority of the magistrate, and all discipline, would be taken away. But it is certain nevertheless that a certain kind of impious men is set down here, who, although they could have seemed fit to be removed from the midst, yet those whose business it would have been to do it are forbidden to do it. Now this cannot be understood to have been said of adulterers, robbers, and other criminal men, since it is established that the magistrates have been appointed by God, and are his ministers, and avengers of evildoers, and do not bear the sword in vain: [Marginal note: Rom. 13. 1 Pet. 2.] indeed not even if the man who has committed adultery, murder, or some other crime should seem to be wheat, that is, of the number of the elect (since indeed a notable repentance has followed), ought the magistrate on that account to spare him, nor indeed would anyone ever be so senseless as not to understand that certain monsters of crime ought altogether to be restrained by the avenging sword. But if what is said in the similitude cannot be fitted to all the impious, then those to whom it does belong must be distinguished from all the others by some certain difference. But from what could that difference be better taken than from the thing in which the godly and the impious differ most from one another? And what, pray, is that? Is it not the doctrine of religion?

It is chiefly in respect of this that these men are called godly, those impious. But there are two kinds of impious men: for some are strangers to the true religion both in fact and in name, as the worshippers of idols, as the Turks — but most of all the atheists; others are Christians in name indeed and in profession, but in fact they are not: since their doctrine, defiled and corrupted by great errors, is that of those whom we call heretics. The first kind can again be distributed into two parts. For some have never professed the Christian religion, and some have fallen away from it. Since, therefore, the difference must be taken from religion, it must be said: here either both kinds of impious men are in question, or one of the two: the words of the similitude do not greatly persuade us to understand that both are in question: for the matter in question is the seed which the enemy had sown among the wheat: so that it appears that the impious in question are those who live among the godly, that is, among true Christians, and who adorn themselves with the name of Christians: which kind are the heretics. And this agrees remarkably with the nature of a tare: for it is said to be nothing other than wheat, or barley, corrupted by the excessive dampness of the place, and washed out by frequent rains: so that heresy could not have been signified by any more similar thing. It is therefore just as if the servants had asked the Lord whether they ought or ought not to kill the heretics and remove them from the midst.

That this exposition of this place is neither absurd nor disadvantageous, but rather necessary, will be understood, I think, by whoever weighs the words with a mind devoted to no party except the truth. The enemy is said *to have sown his own among the good seed*: among the seed (I say) which the Lord had sown. Now the word "seed" is taken now for the thing from which something is born — and that properly — now, but less properly, for the thing which is born from seed. Properly, the seed which the Lord sowed was the Gospel, which he taught: as it is used in the similitude immediately preceding; but improperly, they are those who believed the Gospel. So the Devil's seed, properly, are the false doctrines scattered among Christians: improperly, however, they are those who embrace those false doctrines. Now that both the godly are called the Lord's seed in this way, and the impious the Devil's seed — so that regard is had to the seed from which both their godliness and their impiety derived their origin — is plain from this: that after the sowing was made, good plants alike and bad are said to have grown up, and the servants to have wondered whence the tares were, since nothing but good seed had been sown, and bad plants could not seem to have been able to be born except from bad seed.

From which things it follows that by the name of tares no others can be understood than heretics. "The old law, then" (you will say) "and its principle, will have been wholly abrogated by this one." But what if nevertheless both can stand in force together, and this one takes nothing away from that? It may seem wonderful, I think. But what if it can? What ground will then remain why this exposition should not be admitted? Come, therefore, let us see whether this one can stand with that one or not, even if it forbids heretics to be killed. Against whom, then, was the old law laid down? Certainly against those who had either tried to lead others away from the God of Israel to the worship of foreign gods, or, themselves seduced, had fallen away. But those who do such a thing we do not call heretics, but deserters, runaways, and, in a word that is generally Greek, apostates. There is no mention of heretics there. "But God equally abominates" (you will say) "that worship which is at variance with his commands: just as if you worshipped foreign gods." Let it be so indeed: but what then? Is it therefore plain that the law which was laid down against apostates is to be drawn over to heretics? A heretic is not contained under the name or the notion of an apostate, nor indeed is he touched by any other word of the law. But if you wish to draw the law over to him, that will be done not indeed by the proper force of the words, but — as the lawyers speak — by extension and by a broad interpretation.

But it would first have had to be ascertained whether every law admits extensions of this kind: and if not every one, which ones admit them: and for what reason: and whether in this law those things are present on account of which the extension ought to be made. Certainly, what the words do not express — if you fit the law to that — is done only by a certain conjecture of the lawgiver's mind. But what lawgiver is in question here? The one, namely, whose mind it is as hard to divine as the hardest thing of all: so that every conjecture of that kind cannot fail to carry with it great doubt. But since there stands a general law which forbids killing, it would seem that one must come to the shedding of blood not on the authority of a doubtful law, but of a most certain one. The matter would indeed have all this difficulty, even if we saw no difference between the case of an apostate and that of a heretic. For we could rightly suspect, even if we ourselves did not see it, that there is nevertheless perhaps a great difference. Nor would we rush headlong to make an extension of the law against anyone's life. But what if there are also differences, and by no means slight ones, which we do perceive? For an apostate not only falls away from the true worship of God, but also abrogates all faith in God's word: with a heretic, its authority is still unimpaired.

So that you have arms, besides stones or iron, with which you may confound a heretic and resist him — namely, the word of God — whereas you have none with which you may confound an apostate. Wherefore God perhaps willed, by appointing a most heavy penalty, to deter man from the thought of wholly deserting his worship, and of abrogating all faith in his word: and yet he did not will that a heretic should be subject to the same penalty. It is added that from apostates no fruit returns to the church, but from heretics much does. But chiefly for this reason: that by them the godly are stirred up to search the Scriptures much more zealously and diligently, who would otherwise give themselves over to sloth and gradually fall into ignorance of all things. But this makes the greatest difference: that it cannot seem to a magistrate that a man is wholly falling away from religion, or is soliciting others to fall away, when he does not do so: whereas that it should seem to him that a man errs in doctrine who does not err, or errs most gravely who does not err so gravely, can easily happen — as the many controversies, even among the most learned men, by which the church is never not exercised, are a most certain proof. I say, moreover, that a magistrate can err: not because God's word is not open and certain, but by his own fault: because for judging he brings neither the learning nor the spirit that is needed: so that this ought not at once to seem to belong to the disgrace of God's word.

For how many of those who hold the magistracy, even if he has given his name to the Gospel, bestows enough labour on the reading of the divine letters? How many are there of any singular godliness and prudence, so that from him neither an unjust nor a headlong judgment in this kind is to be feared? You will say: "The question is not whether every magistrate has the right to punish those whom he holds to be heretics, but the matter in question is a truly godly and prudent magistrate, and true heretics." I hear you indeed: but who, when he bears the sword, would not wish to be held godly, and be ready to hold as a heretic whoever opposes him in the doctrine of religion? And so this will altogether follow: that whatever right shall have been permitted to a godly magistrate against true heretics, every man will usurp that same right against those who have dissented from him, whatever in the end they think. Since, therefore, we have shown that not even then could the extension of the law have been made safely — if we could have perceived no difference between the case of an apostate and that of a heretic — what happens when it appears that there is so great a difference? It follows, therefore, that the old law remains in its force, or at least that a like law can be renewed: and yet that the punishments of heretics are not permitted: because, namely, the case of an apostate is one thing, that of a heretic another.

But someone may ask: is there even then a right to punish a heretic, if, when silence has been commanded him, he does not cease to infect with his poison whomever he can? The matter is of great moment, and error on either side is dangerous. For if it is indeed God's will that magistrates punish heretical seducers, he wills it, doubtless, because it is expedient for his Church that it be done, and belongs to his glory: so that if the magistrate is negligent in it, he will have great penalties to pay to God: but if God does not will them to be punished, it is certain also that he has a sufficiently great reason why he does not will it, so that a magistrate will not act against his will with impunity. Care must therefore diligently be taken that the magistrate should not wish to divine what God's will is by his own judgment or talent, but should perceive it from God's word diligently examined and sifted. And since it is established that heretics are now signified under the covering of the tares — if nothing else had been added except that heretics were forbidden to be killed — a true way of interpreting, fitted to the common use of speech, would not obscurely require that the law — if it may so be allowed to call the householder's prohibition — should be understood to forbid heretics to be killed, insofar as they were heretics, or because they were heretics.

Just as, if a law forbade a thief to be killed, it would be understood to forbid him to be killed for the theft he had committed. But if someone, besides being a heretic, wished to teach against the magistrate's prohibition, the words of the law would not seem to protect him: just as, if someone joined murder to theft, the law which forbade a thief to be killed would not free him from punishment. But a reason and cause has been added why the householder does not wish the tares to be pulled up: namely, lest with the tares the wheat also be pulled up — that is, lest with the heretics those also be killed who were not heretics, but sons of God. From this reason, therefore, a certain interpretation of the law must be hunted out. Most men, however, think that the danger of error lies in this: that a man who is a heretic today may, if he lives, one day come to his senses: and that for that reason it is forbidden that they be killed — an opinion which those men seem to me, very prudently, to meet who say that if regard had had to be had to a coming to one's senses of that kind, it had as much to be had in the old law, laid down against seducers and deserters of the divine worship, as in this new law. And since it was not had in that law, it must be believed that it was not had in this one either.

It is added that a man who is a heretic today — if he is of the number of God's elect — cannot be put to death before everything that is necessary to be known for salvation has been disclosed to him: if he is not, hope of repentance ceases. So that this indeed seems by no means a fit reason why a heretic should be spared. And if no regard was had to repentance, there is no other danger of error than that a magistrate will sometimes hold as a heretic a man who is not one: either because it is not that man who thinks wrongly, but the magistrate: or because the error is not of such weight that a man should on that account be held a heretic. But if the householder — that is, the Lord — looked to this, he plainly declared, in definite terms, that magistrates are not fit judges of doctrines, and forbade them every use of such jurisdiction. Yet if someone still contends that regard was had to repentance, he will not for all that have proved that regard was not had to that other error. The law forbids heretics to be killed, lest with the heretics those be killed who are not heretics. In whatever way, therefore, it can happen that one man is killed in place of another, that way must be said to belong to the law; the law speaks generally and without distinction. Whoever, therefore, takes it upon himself to wish to distinguish, unless a certain reason why he does so is established for him, will seem rather to divine than to expound.

A thing which cannot fail to carry the fault of rashness: since, unless a certain reason stands in the way, the law — I say — speaks generally and without distinction, and it is to be understood generally and without distinction. Whoever rashly indulges himself, at his own discretion, in devising distinctions, must be said to wish not to interpret, but by a certain subtle kind of quibbling to elude the words of the law. From which things it follows that, whether regard was had to repentance or not, regard does altogether seem to have been had to that error, at least, by which a magistrate will sometimes kill as a heretic a man who is not a heretic: and that in such a way that these kinds of judgments belong not to the magistrate, nor to any men, but to the Son of God alone, who *only on the last day, through his Angels, will separate the tares from the wheat, and cast them into the fire*. But if the magistrate is not a fit judge to distinguish between a man who is a heretic, so that he could kill him, and a man who is not, so that he would spare him, how will he be fit to distinguish between a cause which makes a heretic — that is, a heretical doctrine — so that he may forbid it to be taught by appointing a penalty, and a cause which is not fit? For what is it to recognise a heretic, except to recognise the cause which makes him a heretic?

What is it to err in recognising a heretic, except to err in recognising the causes on account of which men are to be held heretics? But let us look into the matter a little more deeply. For it certainly appears from the householder's words that he forbade the servants to gather the tares: that he did that not for the sake of the tares, but for the sake of that grain which might by error be taken for a tare. For he willed — to speak plainly — by these words that heretics should be spared: and, in order to provide for the safety of God's true servants, he denied to godly and prudent magistrates the power of punishing true heretics, lest, by their example, impious and imprudent men should abuse God's godly servants. Since, therefore, this law was laid down for the sake of God's godly servants, lest they should sometimes, by a perverse judgment, be punished with the penalty of death as if they were heretics, care must be taken that no such interpretation be admitted that, while the magistrate seems to himself to obey the law, the lawgiver nevertheless does not attain the end for whose sake he made the law. But if the magistrate so interprets the law that he thinks heretics indeed who have kept their heresies to themselves, and have not tried to persuade others of them, ought to be spared, but thinks that he can appoint the penalty of death if they wish to teach others, will the safety of godly men have been provided for in that way?

Not at all: for edicts of magistrates of that kind, whenever the truth has acquired the name of heresy, will remove God's servants from the midst: for he to whom the truth has been disclosed by God is not permitted to bury in the earth the talent he has received: that is, he is not permitted to conceal it by silence. But God wills it to be acknowledged before men: he wills it to be made open. It cannot, therefore, happen that the magistrate should hold any good and faithful servant of God for a heretic, but that the same man, whatever punishment has been appointed for what seems to the magistrate to be heresy, will strive to persuade anyone whatever of it. And so it will come about that, when a law has been passed for the sake of such men, it will profit no one less than them. It follows, therefore, that that interpretation can in no way agree with the lawgiver's mind: indeed, that it is utterly opposed to it. Nor, indeed, do I wish on that account that the godly magistrate should have no office in Christ's kingdom; there are many other things which he both can and ought to do: as, to punish irreverent utterances against God; to take action against those who have dared to desert the whole Christian religion, and who have dared to be authors to anyone of his deserting it; if any foreign forms of worship have been set up, or images erected, to remove them; to turn away from the necks of the godly the violence and injuries of the ungodly; to guard the public tranquillity; and if there is anything else of that kind.

But as for their setting themselves up as judges of controversies among those who subject themselves to the authority of God's word and use its testimonies, and, force being interposed, ordering these doctrines to be taught and forbidding those, let them see again and again what they are taking upon themselves, and how much they are allowing themselves. There are men to whom it seems that, if the sword rests, it will at once be all over with the whole of religion: but a great injury would be done to the Lord if we suspected that he sleeps, and that no care for his people touches him, or that he cannot preserve his Gospel without the sword, as if the word had no force, but all the Christians' hope seemed to be placed in iron. For if, after Satan's tricks had occupied all things, and Satan himself had gained possession of affairs, the force of God's word was nevertheless so great that it could drive Satan down from his strongest and most strongly fortified kingdom, will there, pray, be no force and no strength for defending Christ's kingdom against Satan's darts, now blunted and broken? Let us be of good heart: the Lord does not sleep, but watches. If all our hope is placed in him, if we fight with the word — but breathed upon by his Spirit (who is to be obtained by assiduous prayers), diligently and prudently, those tricks of Satan of which we have spoken so far being avoided — truly there will be nothing for us to fear from the heretics; let the truth engage cautiously with the impostures, and it will necessarily at last come out on top.

Accordingly, if there are daily men who raise new questions, sow new lawsuits, bark against the pastors, and make trouble, let this at some time come into your mind to consider: What does this Satan want for himself? He has come, namely, to attack the evangelical truth: but is this, however, all? What if he is both doing this, and perhaps something else still greater besides? Namely, that he may lay the foundations of a new tyranny (and indeed by your work), which he may one day exercise against God's godly servants, and may utterly destroy the Gospel itself, having at last become Lord of affairs? Come, then, what will you do here, you who boast a zeal for God's honour? You who profess that you value the evangelical truth so highly? You who wish to seem wholly given over to the interests of the Church? What (I say) will you do here? Will you go, namely, and join your forces with Satan? Will you hire out your work to him? Will you supply tools, stones, and mortar? Will your sweat be shared with Satan's slaves? Will you really, in order to avoid the troubles of a single moment, be a helper in laying the foundations of so great an evil? What if he is also doing this: that the arms which he most fears for himself, he may wrest those out of your hands, and provoke you to those things which he does not fear at all — indeed, which he plainly despises?

But certainly he does not value even a hair the sword and bugbears of that kind; and there is nothing which he fears more, and which he can resist less, than God's word. But if the pastors have once obtained this, that whoever has dared to mutter anything must at once have the executioner summoned, who with the sword alone may loose all knots, what great zeal for the divine letters will there be thereafter? Certainly they will understand that they have no great need of it. For they will be able to thrust upon the wretched little people whatever they have dreamed, and nevertheless keep their own place of dignity. Woe to us, woe to our posterity: if we cast away this weapon, the one with which alone we are both permitted to fight and can always conquer, it is all over. [Marginal note: Certainly the one and only weapon for piercing heresies through is God's word.] But (you will say) some men's impudence, and likewise their lust for brawling, is so importunate and so intolerable that they must be restrained by other arms than those of the word alone. Do you want me to say what that is? It is certainly nothing else than if some soldier should say — and indeed that soldier who seems to himself to surpass all in military virtue — that his enemy fights him so busily, so strenuously, that he must throw away his spear and obey him. O fine soldier! For what else would Satan do, than send in men eminently lost, who would provoke God's servants, and indeed provoke them most wickedly?

Shall we, then, because those men vigorously do Satan's business, do what Satan most wanted? This, I say, is what Satan is chiefly about: to wrest this weapon of the word from us, and that we, furnished with another kind of arms which he himself holds in contempt, should go down into the contest. Shall he, then, be obeyed, because he strives for this with the greatest effort, because he fights most stubbornly in this quarter, because he sends in soldiers most fit for carrying out his design: so that, having cast away those arms with which alone we can conquer, we should seize those with which we must at once fall, and which must at once be turned against our own throat? Rather let us at once hold this weapon of God's word fast with our teeth: let us fight with this alone: with this alone let us strive to cut the throat of both Satan himself and all his emissaries: and let us take pains to learn all the most convenient methods both of striking with this weapon and of warding off blows: about which matter we seem to have treated at sufficient length above. But although it is foreign to the magistrate's office to make himself a judge of doctrines and to apply force, it is then most foreign of all if he applies force at another's discretion. [Marginal note: He is not to be a master: a servant of another's desire, or fury.] As, for example, if, when he has entrusted the care of religion to certain men, he is ready to draw the sword against anyone whatever they advise, or has even armed those same men themselves for that purpose.

But how he will approve his own deed to the King of kings on that day, full for the ungodly indeed of the utmost terror, but for the chosen of the utmost delight, is weighed from this: if you yourself, a king, when you were about to travel abroad, had entrusted the care of your kingdom, household, and all your affairs to the faithfulness of some servant, and he had handed over those things in which the royal honour, dignity, and all fortunes were placed to other servants — of whatever kind indeed, but especially of such a kind that he had not ascertained of what wisdom and faithfulness they were — while he himself meanwhile had taken for himself the care of all the most trifling things; and when those highest matters had come into danger, he had nevertheless persisted in his own course; when you had returned home and learned these things, could that servant's deed be approved by you? For even if you had found all things safe, would you not hold him in almost the same place as if all things had turned out adversely? But what if some unworthy and most unbecoming disaster had been suffered? With what punishment would you judge that servant worthy? But is not God's Church God's kingdom, patrimony, and all his delights? Is not all its beauty and dignity placed in the purity of its doctrine, and in worship instituted according to its prescription?

What, moreover, does the whole administration of the kingdom have which, if it is compared with these things, would not be a most trifling thing, and of no value? Come, then, how could you recognise that anyone thinks rightly about religion, so as safely to entrust and commit your sword to him, unless you yourself have first learned what is to be thought? But if you entrust matters so great to men, both in faithfulness and in judgment, whom you do not know (as the saying is) inside and in the skin — indeed, if you entrust them to men of whatever kind — and you yourself attend to other things, what account of your administration will you render? Especially if, while you sleep and care nothing for matters so great, both godly doctrine is oppressed in place of ungodly, and ungodly doctrine established in place of godly, and there has followed on that account both the slaughter of many godly men and the ruin of an innumerable people? That people whom the Son of God valued so much that he did not hesitate to spend his life for its preservation? What severity of judgment do you think will be against you? For my part I think this: that whatever touches God's glory in any way — whether it is the restraining of the heretics' licence in spreading pernicious errors, or anything else whatever (so far, namely, as the character of your office seems either to demand or to permit it) — cannot be passed over without the greatest fault; but nevertheless that the blame will be greater by infinite parts if you prefer to use another man's knowledge in it, rather than to do everything in your power so that you yourself understand what is being done; and that if you cannot attain that, there will be much less danger if you instead interpose no authority of yours, either through yourself or through others.

By what more illustrious example, moreover, could magistrates have been warned not to trust anyone's judgment, than that God's Son himself, through sloth of this kind, was handed over to his most unjust enemies, not so much to their judgment as to their will? "Take him yourselves" (says Pilate), "and judge him as it is provided by your law." But beware of thinking in this way: Now we live in the clearest light of the Gospel; now the pastors are most holy; now all things are safe. What? Are we also to think that Satan is snoring? That he is plotting nothing at all? That he is laying the foundations of some new kingdom? That he is erecting new tyrannies? That he is bringing forth new monsters? But that saying is well known: a small error in the beginning is the greatest in the end. And since all these things are of the greatest weight, whatever may occur, I for my part would exhort the magistrate to seek counsel, with most ardent prayers, from him who can never deceive, and to determine nothing, however, nor yet in that matter, unless he had ascertained that he is acting rightly.

But if he at last settles in that opinion, that he can prescribe by edicts which doctrines are to be taught and which are not, at least care must be taken again and again that Satan gain nothing here; while, however, the magistrate places his own opinion too far from the heretic's opinion, and falls into Scylla, wishing to avoid Charybdis: either he permits him something which he ought not, or he uses words or forms of speaking which, although he wants nothing less, nevertheless bring in some new doctrine, or he wishes what he has gathered from the divine letters as if by a necessary consequence — perhaps not correctly enough — to stand as an opinion from which it is not permitted to dissent. But so that he may fall into no error of that kind, let him examine how ascertained he holds each thing, by those methods which have been set out by us above. And besides, that precaution seems to us the best: that whatever is laid down as agreeing with the divine letters be expressed also in the very same words and forms of speaking which God's Spirit has used in those letters. Otherwise it could hardly fail to happen that the human mind thrusts in something of its own, and if that happens, the error is almost incurable. Care must also be taken that a heretic is not said to hold, and forbidden to teach, something other than what he holds and teaches, in order to avoid those disadvantages which have been mentioned by us above.

This, indeed, I have never been able to grasp — how there can be any reason at all in it: that men think that one who seems to have taught something false and ungodly is to be driven to sing a recantation, impunity being offered if he does it. To what end is that, I ask? What use will there be if, to avoid punishment, a heretic, his conscience resisting, retracts his opinion? This one thing can be alleged as a pretext: that those who are held by the same error, and perhaps lie hidden, will seem in their own minds to be about to do the same by his example, and to cast away the error, and indeed that one will become an author to another that they do the same. But that persuasion must have been most lightly impressed which can be so easily torn out. What? Does no suspicion arise that the retraction was made not from the heart, in order to avoid punishment? Will not very much be added to ill-will from this quarter, rather, if the magistrate is seen not only to kill the body, but also to contrive the destruction of the soul? Are we, moreover, so unarmed for war against errors that we must place our protection in lying, in retractions feigned for fear? But let someone say: We are not doing this at all, so as to extort a recantation of whatever kind, but so that the heretic may acknowledge his error not only with his mouth, but also with his heart: That is splendid, indeed — if only you could obtain it. But what do threats or flatteries mean here? For these can indeed avail something toward storming the will. But the matter is with the intellect: that is changed neither by threats nor by flatteries. These things cannot bring it about that what before seemed true should afterwards seem false, not even if he earnestly desires to change his opinion. But if this is perhaps new and strange to your ears, I shall not deal with you by many arguments. You think, namely, that a man, when he wills, can change his opinion, no new reason having been offered which might make the matter seem to him to stand otherwise than he had been persuaded: I deny it. Try it yourself, therefore, with yourself, whether you can or cannot, even for the least time, be drawn away from your own opinion in this very matter, so that you may believe with me that a man cannot change his opinion as he wills: for without doubt you will understand that you cannot do it. But take care not to take imagination here for persuasion, for nothing prevents you from fashioning in imagination whatever you will. Weigh also this, I ask: if you happen to fear something — for example, lest something turn out not well, lest what you would least wish should happen — and for that reason it keeps you so anxious that you cannot even take sleep: change your opinion in that matter for yourself, so that you hope well, and in that way the distress will have been removed.

O easy and ready remedy for relieving a man of the greatest part of his distresses in life! O short philosophy, too! If whatever evil a man has feared will happen, he could believe (if only he wills) that it will not happen; whatever troubles him, because it seems evil (although very often there is not really any evil in it at all), he could persuade himself, when he wills, that it is not evil. But experience denies that any of these things can be done. But if he cannot, can a heretic, if he indeed wills, cast away his error, unless reason compels him? Why are you troublesome to the wretched man, pressing him that he should lie, and in that offend God more and more? It certainly cannot be denied that this was Satan's invention, by which, through fear of punishments, he compelled God's servants to renounce Christ. So that I wonder that certain men, otherwise not at all foolish, did not see this. How much better it would be, moreover, if it is ascertained that someone is otherwise to be punished with death for the sake of religion, whether he recants or not, to proceed by law, and not to invite him by even one little word to recant, unless it were so that he might be won.

Which, nevertheless, would itself have to be done not by threats, or by flatteries, or by any hope held out, but by the efficacy of testimonies and demonstrations. But the magistrate's authority is most especially necessary in this: that he not allow immodesty and a lust for reviling in anyone, whoever he may be, to go unpunished. If anyone must hear reproach, let him hear it where it is permitted to answer and to defend himself at a trial. For the rest, let another man's life and name be safe from anyone's tongue and pen alike. If there are controversies about religion, let the contest on both sides be with testimonies and arguments; but as for whatever pertains to provoking one another, let the magistrates see again and again that men abstain from it for fear of the punishment appointed for it. If they do this, they will both make the Church exceedingly tranquil, and will obtain the fullest praise among all sensible men. And let this much, indeed, have been said about that change of opinion which is observed to take place in the doctrine of religion. In which change, those who have embraced errors do indeed notice that they have changed their opinion, but they think that they have changed it for the better; only their adversaries understand that it has been made for the worse. Now we shall say in what way it comes about that doctrine is adulterated, without our noticing it, and by what method care is to be taken that this does not happen.
