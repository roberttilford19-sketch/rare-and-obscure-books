# Letter on the Egyptian Inscription of Rosetta

**from Johan David Åkerblad's *Lettre sur l'inscription égyptienne de
Rosette* (Paris, 1802)**
A fresh English rendering, plain and complete

---

**A fresh translation, not a reprint.** This is the whole of Johan
David Åkerblad's *Letter on the Egyptian Inscription of Rosetta* —
the 1802 pamphlet in which the decipherment of the Rosetta Stone
reached halfway and stopped: the Swedish diplomat-linguist's
letter-by-letter reading of the stone's middle script from Coptic,
his 29-sign alphabet laid out in Coptic order, his naming of the
Greek proper names (Ptolemy, Alexander, Arsinoë, Berenice, Aetos)
in Egyptian characters, his Coptic readings of the words for
Egypt, temples, king, and "in the Greek tongue," and his closing
restoration of the Greek text's final lacuna — translated sentence
by sentence from the French of the 1802 Paris edition, in order,
nothing left out and nothing added.

Original: French letter-pamphlet, Paris, Imprimerie de la
République, An X (= 1802 old style). Basis for this rendering: the
Google-digitized copy of the 1802 edition as served by the
Internet Archive, identifier `lettresurlinscr00kegoog` (OCR text
`lettresurlinscr00kegoog_djvu.txt`, 2,703 lines, read 2026-10-11) —
the letter proper (OCR lines ~223-2356; printed pages 1-63).

**How it is presented:** Åkerblad's French in clear modern English,
plain enough for an ordinary reader to follow. Where the
two-hundred-twenty-year-old print and the OCR defeat a certain
reading, the English says what can be said and marks the rest —
[obscured in the scan] — rather than inventing it. His technical

toward what came later; a short editor's note at the end says
briefly which half of his alphabet the histories say held. A

**Reader note:** This is a scholar's working letter, not a
finished treatise: dry by the author's own admission, repetitive
by necessity ("to pile up proofs of each letter's true value"),
and addressed to a rival — Citizen Silvestre de Sacy — whose own
conjectures Åkerblad sets out to overturn, politely. He got
halfway: the Greek names read, the consonants fixed, the vowels
left vague. He knew it, and said so: he offers the vowel part of
his alphabet "rather as a first attempt than as the final result
of my researches." Nothing in it is praised or modernized — it is
presented as Åkerblad wrote it, for the reader to judge.

## Dedication

**Johan David Åkerblad (1763–1819)** — who he was: Sweden's
diplomat-linguist, once secretary of the commands of the King of
Sweden, of the Royal Society of Sciences of Göttingen. The
verifiable record (the Ninth Encyclopaedia Britannica at
Wikisource; Wikipedia, "Decipherment of Egyptian scripts") gives
it plain: in 1802, working from Coptic, he got the Demotic script
of the Rosetta Stone half-right — an alphabet of 29 signs, half of
them later proven correct — then met skepticism and gave up, as de
Sacy had; he served out his days as a diplomat in exile from any
academy, and died at Rome on 7 February 1819, on a pension from
the Duchess of Devonshire, three years before Champollion read the
famous letter.

Why his work is vital — proved, not praised: two hard facts. First,
he found the way in that the decipherment actually took: through
the proper names. From the Greek of the decree he picked out the
groups for Ptolemy, Alexander, Arsinoë, and Berenice in the
Egyptian middle text and read their letters one by one against
Coptic, fixing the consonantal values — d, l, m, n, r, s, t —
that held. Second, he said plainly what he had not done: the
vowels, he admitted, were a first attempt, not a result. A man who
carried the reading halfway and marked the exact spot where he
stopped needs no praise; the alphabet is the monument.

Its relation to this rescue: the Boneyard rescues the working
knowledge of dead masters, and a decipherer's working papers are
among the most perishable of all — they die with the controversy.
Åkerblad's letter survives in modern histories as quoted
fragments; the series sets out to give the Swede back his own
voice, sentence by sentence, in plain English an ordinary reader
can read — the half that held and the half that didn't, exactly as
he laid them down.

## How to read this letter

Åkerblad writes for a fellow orientalist. His conventions are kept
as printed, in plain words:

- *Citizen* — the revolutionary form of address; *C.* before a
  name means the same (*C. Chaptal*, *C. Marcel*).
- *Coptic* — the late Egyptian language of the Christian
  Egyptians, Åkerblad's "torch" throughout; *Thebaic* — the
  southern (Sahidic) dialect of Coptic; *Bashmuric* — a Delta
  dialect he cites for its confusion of R and L.
- *JUL* — his printing of the Coptic prefix *m-* (mark of case,
  "of"); *ir* — the Coptic plural article *n-*.
- *hieratic*, *epistolographic* — keep his words: he takes the
  stone's middle script for the "hieratic" writing of Clement of
  Alexandria. [Editor's note: the script of the stone's middle
  text is what is now called Demotic; Åkerblad's identification of
  it with Clement's hieratic is his own, and stands uncorrected
  in the text.]
- Greek words are given in Greek letters as he prints them, with
  his Latin transliterations of Egyptian kept as read.
- Bracketed notes in the form [Editor's note] are the editor's,
  not Åkerblad's. Where the scan defeats the reading, the English
  marks it [obscured in the scan] rather than guessing.
- Light bracketed dividers (e.g., [The name Ptolemy]) are the
  editor's signposts through the letter's one long argument; the
  original has none — it runs straight through, page 1 to 63.

## Note on the French text

The source is the raw OCR of an 1802 printing, and it shows both
its age and its digitization: the long *s* prints as *f*, *u* and
*v* interchange, accents drop, Greek and Coptic passages come
through mangled, and whole page-number markers are lost. The
translation was made by reading through the noise to the French
beneath; uncertain readings are flagged, never smoothed over. Two
transmission scars are left visible: the two engraved plates —
Plate I (the word-groups) and Plate II (the 29 signs in Coptic
order) — cannot be reproduced from the OCR text, so where the
letter says "see plate," the English says so and carries on with
the prose; and de Sacy's appended *Reply* (printed pages 64-70),
being another author's letter, is not translated here — it is
described in the notes as a possible follow-on. A duplicate-scan
check of the OCR file found no repeated passage in the letter's
span, so the letter is translated once, straight through.

## The letter

*Letter on the Egyptian Inscription of Rosetta, addressed to
Citizen Silvestre de Sacy, professor of the Arabic language at the
special school of living Oriental languages, &c. By J. D.
Åkerblad, formerly secretary of the commands of His Majesty the
King of Sweden; of the Royal Society of Sciences of Göttingen,
&c. Paris, from the Printing-House of the Republic. Year X = 1802
old style.*

### [Opening: the stone, the copy, the debt]

CITIZEN,

The rarity of monuments of the Egyptians' alphabetic writing,
known down to these last years, had set an invincible obstacle in
the way of the efforts scholars made to discover the alphabet of
that ancient people; and they had almost lost all hope of
succeeding in it — as hope was long ago given up of discovering
the value of the hieroglyphs — when the Rosetta inscription came
to revive their zeal along with their hopes. From that moment it
was judged that the veil covering Egypt's ancient monuments was
at last going to be lifted.

The advantage I have had, of obtaining sight of a copy of this
inscription before the monument was made public — an advantage I
owe entirely to your friendship, Citizen — lays on me, in a
manner, the obligation to communicate the results of the many
researches I have made on this precious monument. And although
there is a total difference between the learned conjectures you
have just set out in your *Letter to Citizen Chaptal*, minister of
the interior, and the results of my work, which I am about to
have the honor of laying before you, I do not hesitate an instant
to address these observations to you, persuaded as I am of your
impartiality, even in a matter on which you have already declared
your opinion. Besides, the thorough examination you have made of
this inscription, as well as your knowledge of the Coptic
language — the language that has served me as a torch in all
these researches — already make you naturally one of my judges.
I invite you to use that right with scrupulous severity: your
objections will be precious to me for correcting whatever is
rash in my assertions, as your approval will flatter me, if I am
so happy as to obtain it.

I do not undertake to lay before you the analysis of the whole
Egyptian inscription; my work on it is far from finished. I will
not even go into the detail of all I have so far managed to
decipher of this inscription; such details would be
unintelligible to all who do not have the inscription before
their eyes. I propose only to pass in review before you a small
number of words that will make known to us all the letters that
compose the Egyptian alphabet. The first plate accompanying this
letter shows these words; the other contains the Egyptian letters
arranged in the order of the Coptic alphabet. This Egyptian
alphabet, though it is neither entirely complete in all its parts
nor perhaps free of all error, cannot fail to be useful to those
who wish to make further researches on this subject.

The groups of the first plate, as well as the isolated letters of
the second, were faithfully traced from the copy I have before my
eyes: this copy is the same one Citizen Marcel had put at your
disposal, and which you were good enough to entrust to me about
two months ago. People who know this monument only from the
sample you gave after your *Letter to the minister* will perhaps
judge that the outlines of the letters, as I show them, are too
clean, too finished: the fault lies with the artist you employed,
who greatly exaggerated the unevenness of the outlines. What I
assert here may be checked against the different copies of this
monument that exist in Paris — but above all against an excellent
sulphur impression belonging to Citizen Raffeneau-Delile, which
will no doubt serve as the model if the monument is to be
engraved, as we are led to hope.

### [The road in: the proper names]

The road I cut for myself to decipher this inscription is the
same one the illustrious Barthélemy followed to discover the
Palmyrene alphabet, the same that made known to you the writing
of the Persians of the Middle Ages. I first set myself to discover
the proper names that, on the Greek inscription's showing, had to
occur there in fairly large number. From the first moments I gave
to this research, I found the group of letters forming the name
of Ptolemy: it is indeed fairly recognizable, being most often
followed by a series of words nearly always the same, which I
supposed from the first to represent the Greek formula
*Aiōnobios ēgapēmenos hypo tou Phtha*, &c. — "living forever,
beloved of Phtha" — which accompanies the name of Ptolemy almost
every time. This first step taken, I discovered the group which in
the sequel turned out to be indeed the name of Alexander, though
at the time I had no other reason to assign it that meaning than
the circumstances that determined you to find the same name in
it — which come down to this: that one meets there a letter
resembling the Greeks' sigma, and that this word returns at the
tenth line, answering nearly to the seventeenth of the Greek
text, where mention is made of Alexandria. Finally the name of
Arsinoë was the third I believed I recognized; and from that
moment some time passed before I discovered the other proper
names — several were known to me only once I had already fixed
the value of some of the Egyptian words neighboring them. That
was still to have done very little: I knew, it is true, the
meaning of each group that occurred several times; but I did not
dare assign to each of the letters entering into their
composition its place in the alphabet. After very varied and
often repeated attempts, I believe I have at last succeeded, and
I will first lay before you the alphabetical analysis of almost
all the proper names, to propose to you afterward the application
of this analysis to a small number of purely Egyptian words. I
will insist less on this last part, until — when the inscription
is published — I can set out the whole series of my analysis;
which would be of no use so long as this monument is not in the
public's hands.

I ask your indulgence, Citizen, for the unavoidable dryness of
this exposition. It is a matter of assigning to each letter its
place in the alphabet; which obliges me to go often over my own
steps, to repeat frequently the same thing, solely to pile up
proofs of each letter's true value. I will try to pass as rapidly
as can be done over these minute and barren discussions to which
I am condemned by the nature of the object I have set myself.

### [The name Ptolemy, letter by letter]

I begin with the three proper names to which you and I attribute
the same meaning, though our way of dividing up the letters
differs essentially. The first of these names is Ptolemy's. You
see in this name an immense aleph swallowing three of the letters
I find in it; you neglect, on the contrary, the first stroke of
this group, which I believe essentially belongs to it. I have had
this group shown, with some of its varieties, under no. 1 [of
Plate I]. At first I had believed that the small curved line that
almost always precedes this name, as seen in the first group
under this number, entered into the formation of the word: it was
only later that I found it is an *m* which, in Coptic, is — as
you know — a prefix placed before almost all cases, singular and
plural alike; and I have the proof at the beginning of the fourth
line, where in fact this letter is not found before the name of
Ptolemy. The letter that looks almost like an *a* with its base
prolonged, and which follows this prefix, is therefore the first
letter of this name — that is, a P.

We will see in the sequel that besides this value, this letter
sometimes has that of a B, a PH, and even an OU or EU; which will
not seem at all strange to those who know that these letters are
confounded at every turn in the Coptic language.

The second letter is composed of two lines forming an acute angle
turned to the left. We will suppose it a T: soon we will see that
this is indeed its value. The oblique line that follows, and
which forms the main stroke of your aleph, is an L: I beg you to
observe that this letter has a small bar near its middle, to
distinguish it from another letter resembling it. This L is
followed by a perpendicular line broken toward its top: it is
sometimes joined to the preceding letter; often it is separate
from it. We will see in the sequel that this letter is an *o* or
an *ou*; for the present it must be supposed. The M that follows
is figured nearly like the one I pointed out to you as a prefix.
The other letters composing the name of Ptolemy vary greatly in
form, as may be seen in the different renderings I give of this
word. Since I have familiarized myself with the Egyptian
alphabet, I judge by analogy that there are three letters to be
distinguished in this heap of small lines, sometimes curved,
sometimes straight. The first is formed of three perpendicular
lines; it is a vowel we will meet again fairly often: here it has
the value of *ai*, pronounced as a simple vowel; elsewhere it
represents the *ei*, the *ē*, and the *i* of the Coptic alphabet.
After this vowel comes a stroke of curvilinear figure which, in
this place, has the value of an O. This letter may be compared to
the Hebrews' *vau*, since, like that element, it is susceptible
of several sounds. Lastly, the final letter is an S, consisting
of three and even four small strokes. The first two are sometimes
entirely omitted, especially at the beginning of words. As a
final, this letter has the form of the Greek sigma *C*, as seen
figured in some inscriptions; sometimes it is terminated by a
perpendicular stroke, nearly like a K.

Putting together the letters we have just found, according to
the value we suppose them to have, we form the name
*ptolemeos*; which does not depart much from the Greek spelling
*Ptolemaios* or *Ptolemeos* — for this name is written both ways.
The Copts, who ordinarily keep for the names they received from
the Greeks their primitive ending, nevertheless sometimes cut it
off. They say, for example, both *...sklopios* and *...sklopi*,
both *...sen...sios* and *...sen...s*, both *Ptolemeos* and
*Ptolemi*. It is in this last way that I find the name of Ptolemy
written in the *History of the Martyrdom of Saint Apater*
(manuscript of the National Library, formerly of the Vatican,
no. [obscured in the scan], fol. 69 verso). It is needless to
observe that the first two letters without a vowel represent the
syllable *Pto*, as is the usage in Coptic — above all in the
dialect of Upper Egypt — and that *t* and *d* are always
confounded: those who know a little Coptic will find not the
least difficulty in it. It was natural to suppose that the
pronunciation of this famous name was preserved among the Copts
as it was among their ancestors. Our inscription justifies this
supposition. Even the Arabs, who received this name from the
Copts, pronounce it nearly like them; and despite the foreign
form it has, written in Arabic, they do not even add an *elif* to
it, as they are accustomed to do in many other foreign names, as
you observe in your *Letter*, p. 16. What you take for a capital
aleph in this word has therefore led you to admit a spelling of
the name of Ptolemy little conformable to the genius of the
Coptic language — which does not at all avoid the meeting of
several consonants at the beginning of a word, but on the
contrary seems to love it, as every page of the dictionaries of
this language proves. But I perceive I am engaging in controversy
before I have established my data. Let us return to the
alphabetical analysis, however tedious it be.

### [The name Arsinoë]

The name of Arsinoë, which sometimes accompanies Ptolemy's in our
inscription, occurs there four times, not counting a fifth at the
beginning of the fourth line, where only its last letters remain,
the stone being damaged in that place. This name is preceded by
an *m* like Ptolemy's; here it is the mark of the genitive. The
first letter of this word must be an A: the figure representing
it held me long in suspense, because it seemed to me too
composite for this first element; I was nevertheless forced to
yield to the evidence, since all the names that in Greek begin
with an A have this letter for their initial in Egyptian. Next
comes an oblique stroke nearly like the one we took for an L in
the preceding name, except that it is not furnished with the
small bar in its middle that characterizes that other letter. We
suppose it to be an R; and this resemblance between these two
letters is met with in one of the ancient writings of Persia, the
Pehlvi, as you have observed after M. Anquetil [1]. It appears
from the Sassanid inscriptions of *Kirman-shah*, which you have
explained, that in pronunciation itself these two letters were
often put one for the other; and this is also observed among the
inhabitants of a part of Egypt, who have so thoroughly confounded
the pronunciation of R with that of L that they very often
substitute one for the other in their writing: this substitution
forms one of the characters of one of the three dialects of
Coptic, the one called Bashmuric [2].

[1] *Memoirs on various antiquities of Persia*, by M. Silvestre
de Sacy, page 27.
[2] See *Fragmentum Evangelii S. Joannis* by Father Georgi,
Pref. p. LV and following.

The following letter must be the same one that ends the name
*Ptolemeos*; and so it is indeed: here it is composed of three
small strokes, two of them slightly curved — the form this
letter ordinarily has in the middle of words, as we observed a
moment ago. The three small perpendicular lines we remarked in
the preceding name, where they represented an *ai* or *e*, have
the same value here.

The Greeks write, it is true, *Arsinoë*; but this difference is
too slight to stop us. Besides, the Copts, who know this name
very well — since one of their cities bore it — write it
*arsinoi*, a name still met with in the Coptic dictionaries as a
synonym of *Feyyoum*. (See Coptic MS of the N.L. no. [obscured
in the scan], fol. 77.)

The form of the letter I recognized as an N varies a little in
the two examples I had shown under no. 2 of the first plate. For
the rest, it is only in the middle of words that it is figured
in this way: when initial, it has a quite different form, as we
will see later. The O is the same as in the name of Ptolemy. As
for the last two strokes ending this name, they appear to form an
S, as we saw this letter figured in the name of Ptolemy; but I
confess I would not like to admit that the Egyptians pronounced
this word *Arsinoës* — especially since this name is found again,
as we have just seen, among the Copts, who probably did not
change its spelling. I prefer therefore to see in the half-circle
followed by a perpendicular line an *e*; and such is indeed the
value of this figure in several other places in the Rosetta
inscription. Admitting what I have just said, we have the form
*Arsinoe* exactly as this word is written in Coptic.

In your Pl. II, under no. [obscured in the scan], this name is
shown; but the first stroke of the first letter has been cut off.
Despite this small mutilation, I confess I do not find much
resemblance between the figure you believe to be a capital aleph
in this word and the one to which you attribute the same value
in the name of Alexander.

### [The name Alexander]

This last name, Citizen, will occupy us for a moment. I do not
recognize in it at all the four capital letters you see there;
and certainly I would have been quite astonished to meet this
invention of fairly modern times in a monument of such
antiquity. You yourself were struck by this singularity, and it
was the subject of one of your observations. The name of
Alexander, which is in the genitive, is preceded by the *m* we
have already twice found as a prefix. The following letter is an
A, the same one beginning the name of *Arsinoe*; the second is an
L, likewise known to us from the name of Ptolemy; the third,
placed above another letter, is a K — we will meet it again in
the name of *Berenice*. The one below is *shai*; and it is by
these two letters that the Egyptian interpreter expressed the
Greeks' *x*, which was lacking in his own alphabet. The Copts,
who adopted this letter for the Greek words where it occurs,
employ it sometimes, but rarely, to render the two letters *ks*
in words of their own language. I find, for example, *olas*
instead of *oklaks*, "angle," in one of the Coptic manuscripts of
the National Library, and the dictionaries offer other examples.
I confess, however, that I never found the Greek *x* expressed in
Coptic by *ksh*, as one would judge from this example that the
Egyptians sometimes did. I say sometimes; for commonly they
substitute for this Greek letter — just like the Copts — a K and
a *ch*, as we will see below.

The fifth letter of the name of Alexander is the same as the
first; also, after the two letters just spoken of, we find one
exactly like the one beginning this name in Egyptian. This letter
is placed above another which must be an N, though it is too
badly rendered in the copy I have before my eyes for the strokes
composing it to be distinguished. We have just seen twice in
succession in this name two letters placed one above the other.
The three following letters form a single group. The one above
the others is a T exactly of the same form as the second letter
of the name Ptolemy. This T here replaces the Greek *d*: as the
Copts have no *d* in the words of their language, they employ
this letter only in words taken from Greek; and in these very
words they often substitute a T for a *d*. One finds, for
example, *...* instead of *...*, *...* instead of *...*
[obscured in the scan]. The two letters below the T are fairly
difficult to recognize: one must be an R lying horizontally; the
figure representing an O is sometimes met with in our
inscription; and the last letter, which we already know as an S,
leaves no doubt about the value of the two preceding letters.

We have therefore *ALEXANDROS*, letter for letter, expressed in
Egyptian characters. But why are some of these characters placed
one above another? I do not know — unless space was to be spared,
which this name at full length would have taken up. Perhaps too
there enters into this arrangement of the letters some
calligraphic pretension we cannot appreciate. So it is that, in
many Arabic manuscripts, one of God's epithets — *the Most High*
— is often written so that the last two letters are placed above
the first three; and in the lapidary writing of the Arabs,
Persians, and Turks, the letters of one word are at every moment
placed one above another, which often makes reading their
inscriptions extremely difficult. Besides, we find letters so
disposed in some other places of our inscription, and we will
soon produce another example.

You will allow me to observe, on this occasion, that the form of
the name Alexander which you had shown, Pl. II, no. [obscured in
the scan], and of which you give the analysis on pp. 10 and 11
of your *Letter*, is entirely inadmissible; the place this name
must occupy being perfectly fixed by the preceding and following
words — above all at the second line, where this name occurs for
the first time. The word preceding this name in that place is the
one meaning *priest*, *ouēb*, and which returns often enough for
its meaning to be beyond doubt. The word immediately following
the name of Alexander, and which you make enter into its
composition, is the conjunction *and*, met with more than forty
times in our inscription, whose value is indubitable even if you
did not admit the pronunciation I attribute to it. Lastly, the
stroke ending this proper name in your plate is the article *ni*
or *n* belonging to the following word *gods*, as you may verify
by collating the Greek inscription, which reads *ho hiereus
Aetou tou Ae...tou Alexandrou kai theōn*, &c. — this proper name
being sent to the end of the phrase in Egyptian, as I will
observe elsewhere — *tou de tou Alexandrou kai theōn*, &c.

The group containing the name of Alexander is shown twice under
no. 3 of the first plate.

### [The name Berenice]

To these three names we will add a fourth, equally illustrious
in the annals of Egypt: I mean Berenice, read at the third line
of the Egyptian inscription; it is engraved under no. 4. The
prefix *m* fixes the case of this name, which is here in the
genitive. The B beginning it is of a particular form; and I am
not entirely sure whether this figure is a variation of the P, as
we saw it in the name of Ptolemy, or whether the Egyptians
actually had the two letters B and P in two different forms. Be
that as it may, its value here will, I hope, meet no
contradiction. The R is the same we saw in the name of Arsinoë.
So is the N, though with a slight variation that cannot embarrass
us. The three perpendicular lines return here for the third time;
in this place they have the value of an I. As for the K, we saw
it in the name of Alexander. The following stroke, joined to the
letter K, is a vowel that returns very often, and which we
already found in the name of Ptolemy, where we assigned it the
value of an *ai*. In the alphabet I give it that of *ē*, and I
would not be far from taking it for a kind of fulcrum, nearly
like the Hebrews' *vau*. We will suppose here that it is an E —
unless one prefers to pronounce this name *Berenikes*; for the
last letter is incontestably an S.

The letters we have just analyzed, whether pronounced
*berenikeës* or *berenikes*, express nearly the form this name
would have in Coptic, though I do not recall having met it in
the books of that language. The S is too much in this name, and
it may be that this letter was added by the ignorance of the
Egyptian translator who, seeing the name in the genitive written
*BERENIKĒS* in the Greek decree, will have believed the sigma
radical: for I do not doubt an instant that this decree was
conceived in Greek, and that the Egyptian is only its
translation, as we will have occasion to remark elsewhere.

### [Aetos the high priest]

The third line of our inscription offers several other Greek
names, seen under nos. 5, 6, 7, and 8 of the first plate. The
first we will examine is that of the high priest appointed to the
cult of Alexander and the Ptolemies. One reads in the Greek
inscription: *hou hiereus Aetou tou Aetou Alexandrou kai theōn
Sōtērōn*, &c.; which the Egyptian interpreter renders by
"... being priest of Alexander," &c. *Aetos* — whose construction
would be a little forced in Coptic; and it appears the translator
departed, in this phrase, from the natural construction, to avoid
placing the high priest's name before those of Egypt's
sovereigns. In the following periods, where mention is made of
the priestesses set over the cult of Egypt's queens, he did not
think this inversion necessary, and we will soon see the natural
order of the phrase observed there.

Let us see now how the letters composing this name fit our
alphabet. The first is the one beginning the names Alexander and
Arsinoë. The three perpendicular lines are equally familiar to
us; they form *te*: but here they are followed by that stroke of
which we spoke on the occasion of the names Ptolemy and
Berenice, and which appears entirely idle — which confirms my
supposition that this sign is only a fulcrum fitting several
vowels. Next comes a T already known to us, as well as the
perpendicular line broken at its top, which we said was an O when
speaking of the name Ptolemy. The last letter S is preceded by
two small strokes as in Arsinoë. There in full letters is
*Aetos*, exactly as this name is written in Greek. But another
particularity must be observed here. This word is repeated twice
in succession, and the two words are separated only by a stroke
I do not distinguish well in my copy, but which I would be led
to believe is an *m*. *Aetos m-Aetos* would be "Aetos son of
Aetos," just as *Ptolemeos m-Ptolemeos m-Arsinoës* — a phrase
read at the second line, where the Greek text omits it — means
"Ptolemy son of Ptolemy and Arsinoë." In the Greek inscription
no mention is made of the high priest's father; but such
differences between the two inscriptions occur in other places,
as we just saw regarding the name of Ptolemy. It might however be
that this name is repeated here by a fault of the sculptor.
Besides, this turn of phrase is not analogous to the genius of
the Coptic language: so I believe it borrowed from the Greek
*...the Ptolemies*, without having remained in the language; for
the Copts always insert the word meaning *son* — they would say,
for example, *Aetos pshēri m-Aetos*, "Aetos son of Aetos," as we
say in our modern languages. It is also in this manner that our
inscription expresses itself in speaking of the three priestesses
whose names will occupy us in a moment. The high priest Aetos, if
he was of Egyptian origin, probably bore among his compatriots
the Egyptian name *Pakhom*, meaning in Egyptian the same thing
as *Aetos* in Greek — that is, eagle. This Egyptian name, made
famous in the annals of Christian Egypt by St. Pachom, afterward
passed into the Greek language, as that of Aetos had been
introduced into the Egyptian idiom.

### [The three priestesses: Pyrrha, Areia, Irene]

We come to the names of the three priestesses consecrated to the
cult of Egypt's queens, read at the third and fourth lines. The
Greek has: *Athlophorou Berenikēs Euergetidos Pyrrhas tēs
Philenou*. The Egyptian construction of this phrase differs much
from the Greek. In Egyptian it is the same as it would be in
Coptic or in French: "Pyrrha daughter of Philenus being
athlophore of Berenice Euergetes." In the name Pyrrha there is
only the last letter unknown to us; it is a vowel whose value,
relative to the Coptic letters, is as vague as that of the other
Egyptian vowels: it appears to be here a final A; elsewhere it
represents an E, perhaps even an I. This latitude I allow myself
for the vowels will perhaps seem suspect to those not familiar
with the ancient languages: but certainly I have no need of
apology for the liberties I take regarding the vowels, toward
you, Citizen, who know perfectly how much they vary —
particularly in the Coptic language, as you observed with much
justice on page 51 of your *Letter to Citizen Chaptal*.

The word meaning *daughter* is traced like a kind of siglum or
monogram: it returns twice more; so its value is beyond doubt. I
pronounce this word *sheri*, and I find in it indeed the *sh*
we remarked in the name of Alexander; an R of the form known to
us; and a small circle followed by a very thin stroke — nearly
the form of the final *i*, as we will see in another word. The
article *t-* appears to be indicated by the horizontal stroke
lacking the other bar.

If you have taken the trouble, Citizen, to follow my alphabetical
analysis thus far, you will pronounce without hesitating the
father of this priestess's name — Philenus, or something
approaching; for I abandon the vowels to you, and I do not at
all pretend to teach the pronunciation of the Egyptians. The
first letter, which we have till now recognized as a P,
represents here the aspirate *ph*; which, I hope, will suffer no
difficulty. The Egyptians have however an aspirated letter
answering more particularly to the Greeks' *ph*; but it appears
they were not very scrupulous about changing letters of the same
organ. Their descendants the Copts take the same liberty at every
turn; they write, for example, *...* instead of *...*, &c. I will
not entertain you with the Egyptian word expressing the title of
athlophore: I could not do so without proposing some conjectures,
and we are not there yet. I do my best to persuade you, Citizen,
of the justness of my alphabet; conjectures are postponed to
another time — for it would be clumsy of me to present first the
weak side of my doctrine. Let us return to the examination of the
proper names.

The Greek text continues: *Kanēphorou Arsinoës Philadelphou
Areias tēs Diogenous*; which the Egyptian translates just as one
would say it in French: "Areia daughter of Diogenes being
canephore of Arsinoë Philadelphus." The word *Areia* presents no
difficulty, except in the last vowel, known to us, it is true,
but whose value is very vague. The three lines answer here to
*ex* pronounced as a simple vowel; which proves how ancient is
this pronunciation of the diphthongs we find again in modern
Greek. The monogram meaning *daughter* is already known to us.

The name Diogenes is written in Egyptian *Tiokenes*. We have
already observed that the letter *d* is foreign to the Egyptian
alphabet, as it is to the Copts'. So is the Greeks' *g*, which
the Copts hardly employ except in Greek words adopted into their
language, and always confounding it with K. One finds, for
example, *...* and *...*, *...* and *...* [obscured in the
scan]. Sometimes they substitute an F for a K in words of their
own language; the inhabitants of the Thebaid do so very
frequently in the second person singular of their verbs: they
write, for example, *...* instead of *...*, "thou shalt have no
trouble" — which Father Georgi did not understand [1]. The form
of this K is the same as in the names Alexander, Berenice. The N
is equally known to us, as is the final sigma. As for the vowels,
*ti* and *to* are known to us: the last two syllables are
destitute of vowels.

[1] *Acta S. Coluthi*, pag. 28.

It is a pity that the word meaning *canephore* fell in the part
of the stone that is mutilated. Only the beginning remains:
*pet-*, "the word meaning he or she who carries something." I
find in the manuscripts *petshōb*, "bundle-carrier,"
*petkeol*, "axe-carrier" or archer, and the dictionaries offer
other words beginning with this syllable. But let that be said in
passing, since this letter is solely consecrated to the search
for the alphabet. I return to the priestesses.

The third is named Irene; she was set over the cult of Arsinoë,
wife of Ptolemy Philopator, to whom the inscription attributes
the same surname her husband bore. The Greek has: *hiereias
Arsinoës Philopatoros Eirēnēs tēs Ptolemaiou*. The Egyptian
translator renders this: "Irene daughter of Ptolemy being
priestess of Arsinoë Philopator." Observe that the epithets
*Philadelphus* and *Philopator* are translated by the
corresponding words in Egyptian. The first letter of Irene has
the form of a half-circle: we saw it in the monogram meaning
*daughter*, rather badly rendered, it is true; but we will meet
it elsewhere in a more recognizable form. The two consonants R
and N are familiar to us. The last vowel is the same one ending
the word Pyrrha; one must therefore suppose either that this
letter was pronounced in different ways, or that the name
*EIRĒNĒ* was vulgarly pronounced in the Doric manner.

The monogram returns here for the third time.

The name Ptolemy is not preceded by an *m* in this place, where
however the genitive meaning seems to call for this letter. The
same construction holds in the two other phrases we reported.
The Copts ordinarily put an *m-* or an *n-* before the father's
name; and I do not recall having met this construction in their
books — at least when they employ the word *sheri*, "daughter,"
between the two proper names: for I do find *...* "son of
Menas," in a subscription — but then the word meaning *son* is
Greek. For the rest, the thing is quite indifferent in itself,
and must not delay us an instant.

You see, Citizen, from this faithful analysis, that the ministers
of the cult kept their Greek names — which seemed doubtful to
you. (*Letter of Citizen de Sacy*, page [obscured in the scan].)

### [One more Greek word: the syntaxeis]

I could end here the analysis of the Greek words expressed in
Egyptian characters occurring in our inscription, since we have a
large enough number to dare form the alphabet of the letters the
Egyptians have in common with the Greeks; but I cannot too much
prop up the alphabet I present to you: so you will allow me,
Citizen, to dwell on this object an instant longer.

The word *SYNTAXEIS* is read in the Greek inscription at the end
of the fourteenth line, where mention is made of the annual
impositions in grain and silver, destined for the temples' use
under Ptolemy Philopator, and which Ptolemy Epiphanes confirmed.
I was quite surprised to find this word preserved in the Egyptian
inscription, where you will find it at the end of the eighth
line. It is shown at Plate I, no. 9. I need not insist much on
the spelling of this word, whose letters are all already known to
us. The first is an N; it is the form it constantly has at the
beginning of words — as here, where this letter represents the
plural article *n-*, or *m-*. The next is an S already known to
us, found again at the end of this same word; it replaces, again
jointly with the K, the Greek letter *x* at the beginning of the
last syllable — but there its form is a little different, though
not unknown to us, since we already saw it so traced in the names
Arsinoë and Aetos. After the S, one sees a very small stroke
appearing here to replace the Greek vowel *a*. The N is figured a
little differently from what we found elsewhere. I believe the
sculptor of the inscription is in large part the cause of these
variations in the letters' form; and the typographic process used
to take the copies that came to France will also have helped
increase the uncertainty regarding many of these letters. Below
this N is a horizontal line with a small bar in its middle: this
letter has no correspondent in the Greek word; it resembles a
little the one we found in the last syllable of the name of
Alexander. The T has its ordinary form.

You will not object to me, I hope, that the *x* which in the name
of Alexander is rendered by a *k* and a *sh* is here expressed by
*ks*. Such anomalies are met with on every page of the Coptic
books, and notably *ch* often changes to *sh* in the Thebaic
dialect.

This Greek word is not the only one the Egyptian inscription
retained from the Greek — which was no doubt the language this
decree was conceived in. I believe I meet there those of
*Aiōnobios*, *Epiphanēs*, *Eucharistos*, *Euergetēs*, and
others; but I have not always been able to assure myself of the
true structure of each of these words, and I can hardly do so
until I have a plaster cast of this inscription, or at least a
more exact copy than the one that has served me till now — which,
as you know, is entirely botched in several places. I will
remark in passing that the group where I find *Epiphanes* is not
exactly the same one where you believe you read this title.

### [The purely Egyptian letters: the word for Egypt]

Pardon me, Citizen, this long and tedious analysis; it was
indispensable to justify the alphabet I have the honor of
communicating to you. To complete it, the letters peculiar to the
Egyptian language must be added; but I will pass rapidly over
this part, since the inscription would have to be known to the
public for me to go into detail on all the objects it embraces.
It is not enough to produce an Egyptian word and compare it with
a corresponding word of the Coptic language; one must further
prove, by comparison with the Greek inscription, that this
Egyptian word must necessarily have such a meaning in the place
indicated. I will depart from the order the letters peculiar to
the Egyptians have in the Coptic alphabet, to have occasion to
speak to you first of the name Egypt bears in our inscription.
The conjecture you had first formed regarding this word, page
[obscured in the scan] — besides the objection you yourself make
to it, which made you abandon it, namely that Egypt was never
named *Amor* by its own inhabitants — meets another equally
grave one: the group you propose to read *Amor* returns only
four or five times in our inscription, while the name of Egypt is
read at least twelve times in the Greek inscription. The word
meaning Egypt, which I had shown with some of its variations
under no. 10, is one of the first I guessed — because it occurs
more than twenty times in the Egyptian inscription. Today, now
that I know a fairly large number of Egyptian words, its
combinations with several of these words, perfectly known to me,
leave me no further doubt of its value. One may perhaps object
to me the very circumstance that this word is found oftener in
Egyptian than in Greek; here is my answer. The Egyptian
interpreter inserts the word Egypt where the Greek text has only
*basileia*, "kingdom," understanding *tēs Aigyptou*, "of Egypt,"
as happens at the very beginning of the first line. He employs
it again where the Greek designates Egypt by the word *chōra*,
"country" — for example, line 5 of the Greek text, *hoi alloi
hiereis pantes ek tōn kata tēn chōran hierōn*, is translated thus
in Egyptian: "the other priests of the temples of Egypt."
Lastly he sometimes inserts it where the Greek thought it
superfluous: line 9, *anetheken eis ta hiera argtikas kai
sitikas prosodous*, &c. — the Egyptian adds after the word
*temples* the name of Egypt. So this objection disappears
entirely. The name of Egypt in the Rosetta inscription is, as you
must well expect, *chēmi*. The first letter has not yet passed
before our eyes. It is the Egyptians' aspirated K, answering
both to the *ch* and the *h* and even the *kh* of the Coptic
alphabet; at least I have not discovered, till now, any other
aspiration [1]. This letter fairly resembles the capital *h* of
some Coptic manuscripts of the tenth century; and it is this
circumstance that not a little helped me recognize it. The second
letter of this word is an *m*; we know it from a crowd of
examples. The last is composed of a half-circle preceded by one
or two small, slightly oblique lines — for the writing of this
word varies singularly, as may be seen from the examples I had
engraved: it is the same letter we saw in the name Irene, and
which, though less distinct, enters again into the monogram we
recognized as meaning *daughter*. It is an I, well to be
distinguished from another letter equally in half-circle form,
but a little larger, of which we will speak soon. *Chēmi* — or,
in the Thebaic dialect, *kēme* — is the only name of Egypt found
in general in the Coptic books; and it is precisely this name we
have just found in the group occupying us. It is the *Cham* of
the Hebrews [1], the *chēmia* of Plutarch [2], and the *Ham*
St. Jerome says was the name the inhabitants of Egypt, in his
time, gave their country [3].

[1] The soft aspiration, indicated in Coptic by the letter
*hori*, is sometimes entirely suppressed in our inscription. For
example, the name Horus, written in Coptic *hōr* — as we may
judge from the proper names *...*, *...* [the Greeks write these
names *...* (cf. Palladius, Nicephorus, Suidas)] *...*, &c. — is
written *ōr* or *ēr* in the Rosetta inscription. May I be
permitted, on this occasion, to propose a conjecture which,
however plausible it seems to me, I made it a scruple to insert
in the text of this Letter, where I carefully avoided all that
was mere conjecture. Among the long and pompous titles
decorating Ptolemy Epiphanes in the Greek inscription is that of
*eikōn zōsa tou Dios*, "living image of Jupiter." The name which
in Greek means Jupiter is rendered in Egyptian by a word
consisting of a single letter — the one we recognized as an *en*
or an *ou* in the names Ptolemy, Aetos, &c. This name embarrassed
me a little; Coptic offered no appellative of this form and of a
suitable meaning, and I knew no Egyptian divinity of this name.
I believe I have at last found the solution of this difficulty;
but I propose it as a simple conjecture. The city of Jupiter, or
Diospolis (parva) in the Thebaid, bears in the Coptic
dictionaries the name of *Hō* or *Hoou*. In manuscript no. 69,
from the Vatican, now at the National Library, mention is made
of the nome *Ho*: *...* — it is the ancients' *Diospolites*
nome, and Makrizi's *...*. Most Arab authors and several modern
travelers know this name; and d'Anville inserted it in his map,
writing it *How* after the English spelling. Now it seems to me
fairly probable that this name was that of a divinity worshiped
in Upper Egypt, the same as the *ou* of the inscription; that
the city we just spoke of was called *tpolis m-Ho*, "the city of
Ho" — or, after our inscription's spelling, *...*; lastly, that
the Greeks, who loved to refer everything to their mythology,
translated the god's name by *Zeus* or Jupiter, and the city's by
Diospolis. Those fond of etymological rapprochements will
perhaps find this word again in *No-Amon*, a name met with in
the prophets, doubtless designating some great city of Egypt —
though the interpreters are hardly agreed which one is to be
understood. The Septuagint and the Coptic version do indeed
render this word by Diospolis (Ezek. XXX, 14); but it must be
confessed the description of No-Amon, read in Nahum III, 8,
suits ill with Diospolis of the Thebaid. So the Septuagint — and
the Coptic, which slavishly translates it — found something
quite other in that passage than in Ezekiel's. A passage of
Jeremiah, XLVI, 25, finishes making the thing at least very
doubtful. As for the etymology of this name, it seems to agree
fairly well with our *Ho*: No-Amon would be *...*, "Jupiter
Ammon" — especially since the prefix *p-* has become adherent to
this name, which is even written *...* instead of *...* in one
of the Thebaic vocabularies of the National Library, no. 44. I
know very well that Herodotus, Plutarch, and other ancient
authors tell us Jupiter was called Ammon by the Egyptians; but it
could be that this last name was only an epithet of this
divinity, which in the sequel caused the very name preceding it
to be suppressed. So Venus was worshiped, in a part of Asia,
under the name of Mylitta, which is only an attribute of this
goddess; and in the abraxas, *Sabaoth* is sometimes met with as
a divinity's name, while in its origin this word had not at all
a like meaning.

[1] *De Iside et Osiride.*
[2] *Quaestion. in Genesin*, Op. t. III, ed. Maffei. "*Ham*, ...
and Egypt is called HAM in the Egyptian tongue to this day."

A group of letters sometimes preceding the word *chēmi* in our
inscription will now occupy us. It is figured at no. 11 of the
first plate. It is the same one you had shown, Pl. II, no. 8,
and which you believe to mean Osiris. Allow me first to observe
that the name Osiris occurs only three times in the Greek
inscription — twice at the tenth, once at the twenty-sixth line,
and that jointly with that of Isis. On the contrary, the group
in question returns more than twenty times, and most often
without being joined to the one you believe to contain the name
of Isis — which in its turn is met with fairly often. That would
suppose a very great difference between the two inscriptions —
as indeed you believe, Citizen, you must admit. As for me, I
find, from all I have deciphered till now, that with a few
slight variants the Egyptian inscription faithfully renders the
Greek text.

### [The word for temples]

The word we are going to examine is preceded by an *n*, the
plural article we saw elsewhere. A perpendicular line, sometimes
a little swelled in its middle, constitutes the first letter,
which I suppose to be an R. We saw this letter traced obliquely
in the names Arsinoë, Berenice; we saw it lying horizontally in
the name Alexander; here is the third form in which it presents
itself. The next is a P, but here surmounted by a small oblique
line, which most often merges with the letter. A fairly large
number of examples convinced me that the letter in this form
answers to the *ph* of the Coptic alphabet — which, in truth,
must have had a pronunciation very close to that of P and even
B, since these letters are constantly confounded with each other
in the Copts' language. The third letter, a vowel, is very well
known to us; we saw it successively represent *ai*, *ei*, *ē*,
*i* — sounds which, at the time the Egyptians adapted the Greek
alphabet to their writing, must have much resembled each other,
as they still do in modern Greek. The fourth letter is met with
fairly often in our inscription; but I confess I have not well
been able, till now, to fix its value: it is found, for example,
in the preposition meaning *in*, which I pronounce *hen* as in
Coptic. Elsewhere it appears to be an E. In the group occupying
us, Coptic analogy calls for an *ou*. Be that as it may, the
uncertainty of this vowel's pronunciation must not stop us. The
last letter varies a little in form, as may be seen in the
different renderings of this group the plate offers. Sometimes it
resembles the O in the word Aetos; often it has the form of the
letter ending the name Irene; and it is nearly the letter needed
to complete the word *n-rpeei*, which this group's analysis made
us find. This word, meaning *temples* in Coptic, is the plural of
*rphei*, likewise met with in our inscription. In the Thebaic
dialect this word is written *erpe* and *rpe*; and it is one or
the other of these various spellings the Arabs express, joining
to it the Coptic article, by *berbi* — a name consecrated to
designate the magnificent ruins of these ancient temples in
Egypt. The construction *n-rpeei n-chēmi*, "the temples of
Egypt," found several times, is very admissible even in Coptic —
though in that language one would rather say *n-rpeei
n-te-chēmi*. In general, it appears the Egyptians, in times later
than the Rosetta monument's epoch, made more frequent use of the
prefixes *m-* and *n-* than was done in more remote times.

I do not know, Citizen, whether I may flatter myself I have
persuaded you in what I just said about this last word. Allow me
to add that, despite the persuasion I am under that the Coptic
language contains the debris of ancient Egyptian, and must
consequently serve to interpret our inscription, I always
proceeded with the greatest circumspection in applying Coptic
words to Egyptian groups. In the one we just analyzed, I had
recognized, by comparing all the places where temples are
mentioned in the Greek inscription, that this group must
necessarily mean *temples*; and it was only after that I tried
applying to it the Coptic word meaning the same thing. Now what
is the great difficulty of recognizing the Coptic word's
identity with the Egyptian! Four letters are already known to me
by an almost evident analogy: 1. the article *n-*, which in this
form returns any number of times; 2. the *ph*, which does not
differ essentially from *p*, whose value is incontestably fixed;
3. the K, recognized from several examples; 4. the last letter,
which in other words does indeed express the *s*, but which
might very well replace a *ch*, as the letter *ch* in the Coptic
alphabet renders these two sounds alike. Now, if I suppose that
the letter *r* — which we already saw in the form of a line
sometimes oblique, sometimes horizontal, according to the place
it occupies — might still be figured as a perpendicular line; if
I then pronounce *ou* the penultimate letter whose value is
still doubtful to me; and if, by means of these two suppositions,
I find exactly the word which in Coptic means *the temples* —
have I not some reason to believe this word is indeed the one
the Egyptian inscription offers me! And indeed, Citizen, does
not the Arabic writing present anomalies at least as great as
those we find in the Egyptian inscription! — and yet it is not
embarrassed by the vowels causing here so much confusion. Remark
further that Coptic, far from helping us disentangle this
confusion, increases it on the contrary by that reigning in its
own vowels, which vary infinitely. We will say a word about it
soon; for the present, let us pass to the group you believe
designates Isis.

### [The word for "much"]

I already took the liberty of observing that, unless one supposes
a very great disparity between the two inscriptions, Greek and
Egyptian, this word could not be the name of that divinity, since
it returns very often in the Egyptian, while mention of Isis is
made only twice in the Greek inscription. Indeed, this word is
read twelve times in the Egyptian, and always in passages
corresponding to those where one reads in the Greek *PLĒTHOS*,
*POLLA*, *KATA POLLA*, or something approaching. That is exactly
the meaning of the Coptic word *mēesh*, a substantive meaning in
its origin *quantity*, *plēthos*, but most often employed to
express the adverb *much*. You will find here the A placed a
little obliquely, and joined to the *sh*: this last circumstance
is very ordinary in our inscription, where often two letters are
linked together — which adds not a little to the difficulty of
deciphering this monument. The third letter is familiar to us;
it answers here to the diphthong *ei*, as in the name
*PTOLEMAIOS*. For the rest, it is good to observe that the
Coptic word's spelling varies; I find it written *mēsh* in one
of the Thebaic fragments I copied at Rome at M. Cardinal
Borgia's.

The word occupying us is found twice beside the one meaning
temples — which you took for Osiris: the first time, line 6, in
the part answering to the end of the ninth line of the Greek,
where one reads *KATA POLLA EUERGETĒKEN TA TE HIERA KAI*, &c.
— that is, that Ptolemy Epiphanes had done much good to the
temples of Egypt. In the Egyptian phrase as in Coptic, the verb
precedes the adverb; and so the word *much*, *mēesh*, is found
beside *n-rpeei*, the temples. The second passage where this
occurs is met with at the end of the same line, answering to
part of the eleventh of the Greek text, which reads *ANETHEKEN
EIS TA HIERA ARGURIKAS TE KAI SITIKAS PROSODOUS KAI DAPANAS
POLLAS PEPOIĒKEN*, &c. — that is, that the same king had
assigned to the temples revenues in grain and silver, and had
made considerable expenses to assure Egypt's prosperity. The
Egyptian interpreter abridged this phrase in recasting it; whence
resulted a construction nearly like that of the preceding
example. And that is how words having otherwise no relation in
the Greek inscription came to be joined in the Egyptian
inscription.

I beg your pardon, Citizen, for having taken from you these two
divinities — which however procured you the occasion to
communicate to us very ingenious etymological ideas, perhaps more
learned than those of the true names of Isis and Osiris would
really be.

I will not speak to you of these names for the present, since
one would have to begin by proving at what place they must be
found in our text, and establish for that the meaning of all the
words surrounding them — which I will not undertake to do before
the monument is published.

### [The remaining Egyptian letters]

The letter *shai*, which we saw in this word, is of a slightly
different form in the word *...*, "...." I first believed it a
monogram where the *p* was joined to this letter; but it could be
that it was only a *sh* alone, and that the Egyptians sometimes
said *osh* or *es* instead of *shai*. What makes me suspect it is
a proper name *Harsiesis* I found in one of the Coptic
martyrologies of the National Library [C. 65 Vatic., f. 15, v.],
a name appearing to mean "Horus son of Isis."

The *fel* has precisely the same form as in the Coptic alphabet,
and is found turned the same way. This letter marks the third
person masculine singular in verbs, and forms the suffix of the
same person and gender, as in the Coptic language. This letter
must be well distinguished from a figure much resembling it,
which is, in my opinion, an *A* and an *I* joined together.
These two letters so joined are seen in the word *m-philadelphos*,
"Philadelphus," and in many others. We already spoke of *shai*
and *hori*; as for *ganga*, I am not equally sure of its form. I
believe I find this letter in a word I read *choeis*, "lord,"
after the Thebaic manner — seen at the first line, where the
corresponding Greek phrase is *KYRIOS BASILEUS*; this letter
returns again in other words I have not yet entirely deciphered.
(See Pl. I, no. 13.)

The *schima* is figured as in Coptic, with some variations, as
may be seen in my alphabet. I had the word *sōtp*, "remnant" —
in Coptic this word is written with a *ch* — shown, and I met
several others where this letter is very recognizable.

The last letter of the Coptic alphabet, *ti*, is — as is known —
improperly placed among the letters, since it represents a whole
syllable consisting of two letters. The Egyptians have a
particular way of joining the T and the I; but with them it does
not form the same figure we remark in the Copts' alphabet, taken
from the Greek.

There, Citizen, are nearly all the Egyptian letters answering to
those of the Coptic alphabet. Some other figures are indeed met
with in our inscription, which I have not inserted in the
alphabet; but I do not believe they are new letters — the less
so since the number of those we recovered squares fairly well
with the one Plutarch indicates. These figures are either letters
whose form varies much according as they are found at the
beginning, middle, or end of words, and which I recognized only
in one of these places; or else they are two, perhaps even three
letters grouped together, whose strokes, by this joining, have
sometimes become unrecognizable. If I did not insert them in my
alphabet, it is because I preferred to make it incomplete rather
than assign an arbitrary value to letters not yet well known to
me.

### [The word for king; the word for "Greek"]

Among a fairly large number of Egyptian words whose analogues I
find in the Coptic language, I will cite you only two more,
which will end this long and tedious alphabetical analysis. The
first will be the one meaning *king*, met with very often in our
inscription. This word is always found there with the article and
the prefix; and it is in this form it is shown, no. 15. We find
there first an M, then a figure fairly resembling the B of the
name Berenice, but surmounted by that oblique stroke we remarked
in the *ph*. Then come an *ou* known to us, an R formed by a
small oblique line, and an O in half-circle. This last letter
fairly resembles the one we supposed to be an I in the names
*Eirenē*, *...*, &c. Perhaps it is only one same letter as that
one, comparable to the *waw* of the Oriental languages — as
indeed its figure much resembles that of this letter in the
Phoenician and Ethiopic alphabets. If this supposition is
admitted, the small lines sometimes accompanying it might be
regarded as the sign fixing the value of the *i*, of which it is
deprived when it represents the *ou*. A third letter, the M,
sometimes again resembles these two others; which no doubt much
increases the difficulty of deciphering this inscription — but
this resemblance of several letters will seem neither strange nor
unexpected to those who know how frequent it is in the Oriental
languages. I beg you, Citizen, to remark that the letter I
believe to be here an R is sometimes inclined, sometimes upright
in this same word, as you may see in the plate, at no. 15, where
I give several variations of it. This confirms what I said of
this letter when speaking of the word meaning temples.

The letters we just recognized form exactly the word *m-p-ouro*
or *m-p-ēro* — which, stripped of the prefix and the article, is
the substantive *ouro*, "king." The result of this analysis,
which moreover seems to me constant, is still confirmed by the
place this word occupies in our inscription, where most often it
immediately precedes the name of Ptolemy, when in the Greek text
there is *BASILEUS PTOLEMAIOS*, "the king Ptolemy."

Let us end with a word met with at the end of the inscription,
in the place where the Greek text says the decree was to be
engraved in three kinds of characters — sacred, common, and
Greek. The Egyptian word designating this last character is shown
at no. 16. We already know all its elements, forming fairly
exactly the Coptic word *ouēinin*, an adjective meaning *Greek*,
belonging to a root which, in all the Oriental languages, has
the same meaning. The last N in this word is joined to a
perpendicular line crossed by a bar: I believe it is the letter
I, seen elsewhere, though the bar here is a little more
lengthened than usual. It could be that this vowel was pronounced
before the consonant it accompanies — which would bring this
word still nearer the Coptic spelling. I believe this supposition
fairly founded, from other examples it would be too long to
allege here; and perhaps this same inversion must be admitted in
the name of Ptolemy, where we saw an O after the L, and the
first consonants entirely destitute of vowels — which would
assimilate the Egyptian pronunciation of this proper name to that
of the same name in the Greek inscription. The word *ouēinin* is
preceded by a syllable I pronounce *met-*, which, joined to this
word, forms the adverb *met-ouēinin*, *hellēnisti*, "in the
Greek tongue" — a form often met with in the Coptic books.

### [On the limits of Coptic]

Independently of the Egyptian words I recover in one or the
other Coptic dialect, there are a fairly large number whose
reading seems assured to me, but for which I find no analogues in
that language. This must not surprise those who know how bounded
are the matters the Coptic books we possess turn on: they consist
only of translations of the Bible, liturgies, homilies,
martyrologies, church canticles, &c., whose humble and vulgar
style must necessarily differ from the more elevated language of
a decree composed in the name of all Egypt's hierarchy, destined
to preserve the memory of a king's exploits and benefactions.
Besides, an infinity of Greek terms adopted into the Coptic
language — above all since the introduction of Christianity —
have insensibly made men forget the Egyptian words they
supplanted. The Copts employ, for example, Greek words to say
*law*, *image*, &c., and the equivalent Coptic terms have
entirely disappeared from that language. These terms, and others
equally unknown in the modern idiom, are found again in our
inscription — which, in its turn, admits expressions taken from
the language of the Ptolemies' court, which the Copts do not
appear to have kept in theirs, but whose value they render very
well by words of their own language. If to these considerations
one adds further the circumstance that several centuries lie
between our inscription and the oldest compositions in the
Coptic language — the version of the Psalms, for example — and
that in this interval the language must necessarily have
varied, one will no longer be astonished at the difference met
with between the inscription's idiom and the Copts'.

From these reflections, justified by our inscription, one may be
surprised that scholars like La Croze, Jablonski, and others —
who knew only a part of the Coptic works we possess today —
imagined that with these feeble means they could recover, in
modern Egyptian, the etymologies of all the Egyptian divinities'
names: so not a single one of the etymologies they proposed,
regarding the names of the divinities mentioned in the Rosetta
monument, is found confirmed by this monument. The National
Library possesses a great number of Coptic manuscripts — some
from the old collection, others brought from Rome. The latter
lay buried in the Vatican library, whose access was extremely
difficult: so most of these manuscripts had remained unknown to
scholars. At Paris, literary treasures are open to everyone; and
the truly admirable kindness of the scholars charged with
keeping the National Library's manuscripts invites, so to speak,
men of letters to visit them. I made, last winter, extracts of
all regarding Egypt's geography and civil history in the Coptic
authors; I enriched my copy of La Croze's Dictionary with a
great number of words lacking in it; I formed the framework of a
Thebaic Dictionary already holding more than two thousand
articles: despite this, I meet very often — as I just said —
Egyptian words whose meaning is fixed by the Greek inscription's
analogy, as well as by the construction, and which are not found
in my collections. Yet the Rosetta inscription is anterior by
only a few centuries to the oldest pieces written in Coptic —
the version of the Psalms, for example: how then dare one, with
still more reason, flatter oneself of recovering in our
ecclesiastical Coptic the etymologies of those Egyptian
divinities' names whose antiquity is lost in fable's remotest
times — while the origin of the denominations of Greece's and
Italy's gods is barely known to us, despite the great number of
authors, very ancient in comparison with the Copts', remaining
to us in the two languages!

### [A point in the middle of words; the god Ra]

I would wish, Citizen, to entertain you with some particulars I
remarked in this inscription; but I fear tiring your patience,
and overstepping a letter's bounds. I will however choose one at
random, though I have very little to tell you on the subject. One
sometimes meets in the middle of words a point I first took for
a sign of abbreviation, nearly like that line placed above the
words *...* in Coptic; but it could be that this sign has here
another employment. This point is seen in the name of *Ra*, in
that of Osiris, in the word meaning *priest*, and in some
others. For example, the name *Ra* — which returns often enough
to be perfectly determined both in form and in value — is
composed of a *rē* and the letter we recognized as an *ou* or an
*o*. Between these two letters is a point placed on the line:
now I do not know whether this point here replaces a letter, or
whether it only indicates that the last is not an O but an A
surmounted by a T — which I am much inclined to believe. On this
supposition we would have the word *rēt*, much approaching the
Greek pronunciation. I will remark on this occasion that this
Egyptian term answers to the two synonymous denominations the
Greek inscription employs — that of *HĒLIOS* and that of *THEOS*
— and this, to say it in passing, destroys the observation you
thought you could oppose to the system adopted by the scholars
who considered *Hēlios* and *theos* — or *thou* — as one same
divinity. The same word further reminds me of a passage of your
*Letter to the minister* (page [obscured in the scan]), where
you say that "measuring the space the Egyptian inscription
presents in certain places between the name of Ptolemy Epiphanes
and that of his father and mother, one would be led to believe
the style of this inscription less emphatic than the Greek
inscription's." Permit me, Citizen, to observe to you that
Egyptian words are often shorter than Greek words, and that, for
example, in the titles of Ptolemy Epiphanes which you cite to prop
your conjecture, the whole Greek phrase *ĒGAPĒMENOS HYPO TOU
PHTHA* is expressed by the single word *meri-Ptah* or
*mei-Ptah*, meaning the same thing. This composition is not
exactly conformable to the usage of the Coptic language, in which
one would indeed say *mai-Ptah* to designate someone loving
Phtha — or *Vu...* — but not so grammatically *mei-Ptah*, "he
who is beloved of Phtha"; yet I do not believe it contrary to the
language's genius; and these small discords between the ancient
idiom and the modern tongue must not astonish us. To return to
Ptolemy Epiphanes' titles: far from finding them less emphatic
in Egyptian than in Greek, I read exactly, word for word, the
same titles in the one and in the other of the two inscriptions.

### [The alphabet, its vowels, and the ancient witnesses]

I will add one more word to what I already had the honor of
telling you of the alphabet I present to you. In general, it
resembles no other known to me — though I do not deny that
isolated letters are found in it having some relation to certain
letters of the Phoenician and Syriac alphabets, and even, if you
will, to Zend. I will not engage in these rapprochements, which
everyone will make in his own way anyway, as everyone will find
in them the affinities he wants to find. In arranging the
letters, I followed the order of the Coptic alphabet, because we
know almost nothing of the order in which the letters followed
each other among the Egyptians. As for the vowels the ancients
tell us were seven in number in Egypt's alphabet [1], I had
believed for a moment they might be recovered in the seven
classes of the Ethiopians' vowels; and indeed, the Ethiopians'
two A's are found again among the Egyptians and their
descendants the Copts — who, independently of the ordinary A,
attribute this pronunciation in certain cases to the *e* and the
*i*: but I met difficulties that made me abandon this idea, and
I contented myself with assimilating the vowels I find in the
inscription to those of the Coptic alphabet — partly from the
value indicated to me by the Greek proper names expressed in
Egyptian letters, partly from the analogy of the Coptic words
that seemed to me indubitably the same as the Egyptian words in
which these vowels were found. I am far from pretending I always
hit the truth; and I deliver this alphabet — above all for the
vowels' part — rather as a first attempt than as the final result
of my researches, which I am still far from having finished.

[1] Demetrius, *On Style*, § 71.

I need not recall the little the ancients transmitted to us
regarding the Egyptians' alphabetic writing: M. Zoëga entirely
exhausted this matter in his work *De origine et usu
obeliscorum*; and you yourself, Citizen, discussed in a very
satisfying manner the principal passages of the ancient authors,
so far as it entered into the plan of your *Letter to Citizen
Chaptal*. You will however permit me some observations on this
subject. I quite agree with you regarding the application of
Herodotus's *dēmotic grammata* to the writing of our
inscription — which itself designates it by *enchōria grammata*,
meaning nearly the same thing: but we differ essentially in the
interpretation of Clement of Alexandria's famous passage
(*Strom.* v, 4), which already gave rise to so many different
opinions. It seems to me extremely probable that his hieratic
writing was nothing other than the one our inscription is
composed in — since Clement says expressly it was the writing
used by the hierogrammates or sacred scribes, and these same
sacred scribes are clearly designated by our inscription as
forming part of Egypt's hierarchy, from whom this decree
emanated. What then is more natural than to believe they composed
it in the character the ancients assign to them, and in which at
least a part of their sacred books were written! This appears
from another passage of the same Clement, where he names first,
among the hieratic books, the one containing hymns to the gods'
praise. These hymns must necessarily have been written in
alphabetic characters, since the hieroglyphs could but very
imperfectly express poetic language, where the tongue's shades
play so great a role. It appears then very probable that the
hierogrammates employed the character Clement of Alexandria
designates by the name of hieratic — both to transcribe their
sacred books and to compose the acts emanating from the college
of priests of which these scribes formed part. As for the
epistolographic writing the same author speaks of, it must have
been only a kind of cursive writing, derived — with some
alteration — from the hieratic or the books' writing. That is the
course the art of writing followed among all peoples. If this
explanation of Clement's passage is admitted — he being the only
one, with Porphyry, to speak of a triple Egyptian writing —
these authors reconcile perfectly with Herodotus and Diodorus,
who admit only two kinds of writing: the hieroglyphic, and the
common or alphabetic. It is this last that Clement calls
hieratic, because it served the hierogrammates' uses; and the
cursive derived from it is what he names the epistolographic
writing. This cursive writing might very well be the one we find
on mummies' bandlets and on papyrus rolls — evidently a
degradation of our inscription's writing. I recognize in it
several letters met with in our inscription; and if I have not
discovered some word that could put this conformity beyond doubt,
it is because I have not had occasion to make rather sustained
researches on it. Besides, the fragments we have had till now are
too mutilated, and of too inconsiderable an extent, for one to
promise oneself much success from such a work. The expedition to
Egypt much enriched our collections in this kind. Besides
several pieces of epistolographic writing found in the work — as
interesting as it is magnificently executed — of Citizen de Non
[Editor's note: Vivant Denon], the largest and best preserved, at
least in some parts, of all the papyri I know, has just been
given by the First Consul to the Cabinet of Antiquities; and I
do not doubt that the learned and indefatigable Citizen Millin —
to whom antiquities already owe so much — will soon make known
to us this precious monument, which will no doubt contribute
much to fixing scholars' opinion on this subject.

### [Closing: restoring the Greek lacuna]

Before ending this Letter, you will permit me, Citizen, to show
how much a fuller knowledge than I have yet been able to acquire
of the Egyptian inscription may become important for restoring
the mutilated passages of the Greek inscription. Several of
these gaps are, it is true, fairly easy to supply; but there are
some where one could not succeed without the Egyptian's help:
such is, for example, that of the forty-sixth line, where a date
is lacking, probably to be found nowhere else. I could not
decipher the passage corresponding to it in the Egyptian — all
that part of the copy you entrusted to me being entirely
effaced; but I do not despair of succeeding, if I obtain a
plaster impression — which should not be so difficult, since
there exists at Paris a mold of the Rosetta monument. The last
line is in the same case as the one I just spoke of — that is,
it can hardly be restored without the Egyptian inscription's
aid; and it is this restoration I am about to have the honor of
laying before you. One reads, at the last line of the Greek
inscription, that the decree was to be engraved on a hard stone
in sacred, common, and Greek characters, to be placed in each of
the first and second ... *STELEON LITHOU TOIS TE HIEROIS KAI
ENCHŌRIOIS KAI HELLĒNIKOIS GRAMMASIN KAI STĒSAI EN HEKASTŌI
TŌN TE PRŌTŌN KAI DEUTER...* Here begins the lacuna which, supposing
the line was written to the end, occupies the space of about
fifty letters. Here is how I fill this lacuna from the Egyptian
inscription's analogy: *... kai tritōn hierōn en hois hidrystai
hē eikōn tou theou basileōs aiōnobiou* — that is, this stone was
to be placed in each of the temples of the first, second, and
third order, in which the statue of the king would be erected.
But a difficulty presents itself here: this phrase is a few
letters too long for the place the line affords. Perhaps there
was *ESTIN* instead of *HIDRYSTAI* — though the Egyptian word
answering to this last verb is found there very expressly;
perhaps too the Greek decree employed an equivalent but shorter
expression — for example, *TOU THEOU EPIPHANOUS* instead of the
last three words I find in Egyptian. Be that as it may, the sense
seems to me to have been the one the restoration I propose
offers.

It is fairly remarkable that this restoration is confirmed, as
regards the three orders of temples, by the hieroglyphic part —
which till now I have had time to examine only very lightly. One
sees there, at the end of the last line, three hieroglyphic
figures lying horizontally, marked underneath I, II, III, from
right to left. For the rest, I leave to the scholars occupying
themselves with commenting the Greek part of the Rosetta
monument to tell us what these three orders of temples were; and
I end this long Letter begging you, Citizen, to accept the
assurance of my high consideration and my inviolable friendship.

Åkerblad.

## Editor's note: the alphabet and the half that held

Åkerblad's second plate lays out 29 Egyptian signs in the order
of the Coptic alphabet; the plate itself is an engraving and
cannot be reproduced from the OCR text, so the sign-forms must
be sought in the printed pamphlet. What the letter's prose
establishes, sign by sign, is the value-set won from the Greek
proper names: P, T, L, M, the vowels *ai/ei/ē/i*, O, S, A, R, K,
*shai*, N, E, B, PH, F (*fel*), H (*hori*), *ganga*, *schima*,
and the syllable *ti* — with the vowels confessedly uncertain.

The modern histories' verdict, briefly, as the series was
charged to give it: about half of his 29 held — the consonantal
values read out of the proper names (the Ptolemy-Alexander
series: the *p/t/l/m*, the *r*, the *s*, the *n*, the *k*),
which later decipherers confirmed from the same names; the vowel
scheme — the *ai*, the *ē*, the fulcrum-vowel, the stacked
inversions — did not. He said as much himself: the vowels were
"a first attempt," not a result. The text above stands exactly
as he wrote it, uncorrected; this note only marks where he
himself marked the stopping-point.
