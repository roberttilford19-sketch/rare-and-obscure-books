DEDICATION

To Hermann Hankel of Halle and Tübingen (14 February 1839 – 29 August 1873) — Riemann's student, the man who heard what Grassmann had been saying to an empty room, and who at thirty-four left the history of mathematics itself unfinished on his desk — this English dress of his last book is dedicated, under his own name, as everything in this series is published under the dead scholar's name and no one else's. [Dates and posts: MacTutor biography, University of St Andrews; M. J. Crowe, "Hankel, Hermann," Dictionary of Scientific Biography; see References.]

He was born at Halle, where his father Wilhelm Gottlieb Hankel was a physicist, and raised in Leipzig, where he read the ancient mathematicians in the original Greek at the Nicolai Gymnasium. He entered the University of Leipzig in 1857, studying with Möbius and with his own father; in 1860 he went to Göttingen and became a student of Riemann; in 1861 he worked with Weierstrass and Kronecker in Berlin. His doctorate came from Leipzig in 1862, his teaching qualification in 1863. In the spring of 1867 he was made extraordinary professor at Leipzig; by the autumn of that same year he was ordinary professor at Erlangen, where he married Marie Dippe; in 1869 he accepted the chair at Tübingen, and there he spent the last four years of his life. [MacTutor.]

In 1867, at twenty-eight, he published the work on which his name still rides: the Theorie der complexen Zahlensysteme ("Theory of Complex Number Systems"). Its engine is his Prinzip der Permanenz der formalen Gesetze — the principle of the permanence of formal laws: extend a number system if you like, but the old rules of reckoning must be allowed to keep ruling in the new country, and Hankel proved, not merely asserted, what that costs and what it buys. From it he showed that the familiar rule of signs is not a convention but a forced conclusion, the one multiplication on the line that extends the multiplication of positive numbers while keeping the distributive law; and a few pages later he proved the boundary of the whole enterprise, that no system of hypercomplex numbers can satisfy all the laws of ordinary arithmetic at once. In the same book he did an act of scholarly justice his century had neglected: he recognized the significance of Hermann Grassmann's long-ignored writings, as MacTutor puts it the first to do so, and said so in print. His later work — the Untersuchungen über die unendlich oft oscillirenden und unstetigen Functionen (1870), taking up Riemann's theory of integration, and a series of papers in the Mathematische Annalen — still stands in our textbooks, anonymously: the Hankel functions (Bessel functions of the third kind), the Hankel transform, and the Hankel matrix bear his name. [MacTutor; Crowe, DSB.]

He died at Schramberg, near Tübingen, on 29 August 1873, aged thirty-four. The treatise on the functions of a complex variable, for which the Theorie of 1867 was to have been the porch, was never written. Neither was the great history of mathematics he had planned in two stages — first one volume for a wider circle of readers, then a larger, more specialized account for his fellow specialists. Death stopped even the first of these. What survived was a sheaf of fully worked-out sections in his papers, and it was his own father — the physicist Wilhelm Gottlieb Hankel, under whom he had once studied — who carried them into print unchanged, writing the preface at Leipzig on 3 October 1874 with the help of Wilhelm von Zahn, his son's closest friend. The preface is translated in full below. A father published this book. [MacTutor; W. G. Hankel's preface, translated in this volume.]

Why is the book vital? Because of what stands in its first five pages. Hankel does not begin his history with Thales or with pyramids; he begins by asking what shape the whole story has — and answers with a scheme of three great periods (the Greek geometric age; the arithmetic-algebraic age of Indians and Arabs; and the modern age that begins when Descartes fuses the two) that historians have been using, refining, and arguing with ever since. It is the work of a working mathematician of the first rank writing history with a theorem-prover's conscience: distrust inherited anecdote, demand the sources, and say plainly where the evidence runs out. Moritz Cantor pointed out many errors in it, and the errors are real; but Cantor himself, and Allman, Cajori, Heath, and Merz with him, recognized the brilliance of Hankel's historical insight. [Crowe, DSB.] A history of mathematics that a dying thirty-four-year-old wrote better, in its design, than the healthy men who outlived him — that is the sort of bones this series exists to raise.

This volume stands in Robert Tilford's Rare & Obscure Books series beside the other Boneyard rescues: works by dead scholars, translated or finished at last, and put back into the world under their own names. No English translation of Hankel's history, in whole or in part, was found after research (searches run October 2026, recorded in NOTES.md); if one exists, the editor will gladly stand corrected.

INTRODUCTION: THE BOOK, THE FATHER, AND THE UNFINISHED DESIGN



The book

Zur Geschichte der Mathematik in Alterthum und Mittelalter ("On the History of Mathematics in Antiquity and the Middle Ages"), Leipzig: Druck und Verlag von B. G. Teubner, 1874. (iv) + 410 pages, with two appendices: a fragment on Euclid (p. 381) and a sketch of the mathematics of the Chinese (p. 405). Published after the author's death and edited by his father, Dr. W. G. Hankel, from sections found complete among his papers. [Title page and table of contents verified from the Internet Archive scan, identifier `zurgeschichtede00hankgoog` (Google Books scan, University of Michigan copy); collation (iv), 410 pp. per the Boston Public Library catalogue record.]

The book is not the whole history Hankel meant to write, and its preface says so. It treats antiquity and the Middle Ages, but deliberately leaves out the geometry of the classical Greek period — the omission is the editor-father's own note, not a loss in transmission: Hankel's worked-out sections simply did not include it, except for the Euclid fragment printed as an appendix. What the volume does contain is listed in the table of contents translated in this edition: the periodization essay; four chapters on the pre-scientific age (number-words, numerals, practical reckoning, practical geometry); the Greeks from Thales to the founding of the Alexandrian school; Greek arithmetic, algebra, and indeterminate analysis; the Indians; the Arabs (the longest single chapter, pp. 223–293); the Romans; the two medieval periods; and the algebra of the Renaissance, breaking off at p. 380 — where his manuscript broke off too.

Hankel's Arabs chapter had a life before the book: it appeared, still in his lifetime, in Italian dress in the fifth volume of Baldassarre Boncompagni's Bullettino di bibliografia e di storia delle scienze matematiche e fisiche — the father records this in the preface, and no other translation of any part of the history was found after the research recorded in NOTES.md. No English translation of the book, whole or part, was found after research (October 2026): not in the Internet Archive, not in HathiTrust or Open Library records, not in the Mathematical Association of America's Convergence "Mathematical Treasures," which exhibits the German title page and calls the book by its German name. A few of Hankel's words

Eintheilung — "division," the carving of the history into its periods. Grundzahl — the "ground-number," what we call the base of a number system. Stufenzahlen — "step-numbers": the successive powers of the base (10, 100, 1000 …), thought of as steps up a stair. Einer — the "ones," the digits below the base. vorwissenschaftlich — "pre-scientific": Hankel's name for the long childhood of mathematics before the Greeks. Alterthum is the 1874 spelling of Altertum, antiquity.

THE EDITOR'S PREFACE (W. G. HANKEL)

Book: Zur Geschichte der Mathematik in Alterthum und Mittelalter, 1874. Printed pp. i–ii.

When the undersigned's son, the ordinary professor of mathematics at Tübingen, departed this life in the past year, he left behind, besides weighty works belonging to pure mathematics, a number of writings on the history of mathematics. From the very beginning of his academic studies he had followed the historical development of mathematics with special fondness, and had pursued profound researches into the sources of its history. On the basis of these studies he intended first to compose a presentation of the history of mathematics in one volume, addressed to a wider circle of readers, and afterwards a larger, more detailed representation of the same for specialists. His early death prevented him from completing even the first-named work. Among his papers there was found a number of completely worked-out sections, which are here published unchanged. They present the development of mathematics in antiquity and the Middle Ages (with the exception of the geometry of the classical period), to which is further joined, by way of appendix, a fragment on Euclid. One of these sections, that relating to the history of mathematics among the Arabs, has already appeared, during the author's lifetime, in Italian translation in the fifth volume of Boncompagni's Bullettino di bibliografia e di storia delle scienze matematiche e fisiche.

It only remains for me to express heartfelt thanks to the numerous scholars who supported my son in his historical researches, in part by sending him their writings, in part by personal information; and to Dr. v. Zahn, the youthful friend of the departed, who has aided me most effectively in the care of this edition.

Leipzig, 3 October 1874.

Dr. W. G. Hankel

THE DIVISION OF THE HISTORY OF MATHEMATICS

Book: Zur Geschichte der Mathematik in Alterthum und Mittelalter, 1874. Printed pp. 1–5.

The history of a science can itself become science only when, rising above the boundless fullness of detail, it succeeds in discerning and presenting the determining principle of development even in the most general features. Think of a waterfall: the form of the falls, in broad outline, remains ever the same, although the path that each single drop takes through the air is determined by chance and remains eternally capricious. Whoever undertakes to know the present state of a science thoroughly must — far more in its history than in consideration of its momentary state — attend to the fixed form and set the fortuitous aside.

If, however, the history of a science is not to remain strictly within the bounds of necessary development, but is also to mirror the life of nations and of individuals, then its task will be to compose both elements of historical development, the necessary and the fortuitous, in due measure into a true picture of the whole development. There lies hidden in the first, general principle a great difficulty for the presentation: it must make the ideas that govern the actual development stand out clearly and sharply, without becoming abstractly one-sided and thereby perhaps false. In this sense the merit of a contemplation of history will consist more in the presentation of what is significant, in the sharp characterization of the turning-points of development, than in general pragmatic reflections or in philosophical constructions ventured from speculative points of view.

In this general sense every history of mathematics will have to move. Mathematics, more than any other science, knows no national barriers: nowhere, not even among the sciences of nature, do we see all peoples following, with such single-minded unanimity, the same great laws of development — at one time retarded (as indeed happens even in mathematics), at another advancing again. In no science does the individual, creative talent unfold itself in such self-contained harmony — or, where this harmony is lacking, sink so soon into deep oblivion. Nowhere, finally, does so noble and significant a period of development stand at the very entrance, in such a way that it can still serve as a model for all succeeding generations.

The beginning of the history of mathematics coincides with the beginning of science in general: in the sixth century before Christ — as far as our knowledge now reaches — with the Greek sages and their schools. An ever-rising development runs through the next three centuries to the classical epoch of geometry in the third and second centuries; however excellent the acquisitions this period brought to science, the striving of the Greek mind after a noble form stifled its fresh forward urge. A period of standstill and gradual decline follows.

But now, from the end of the first century after Christ, there dawns a wholly new, purely arithmetical and algebraic development, which arose out of an entirely new mode of intuition and must therefore not be regarded as a continuation of the classical period, but rather as its direct opposite. Whence this new direction came, how far it served the general spiritual development of mankind, are questions that have often been asked, but hitherto never definitively answered.

It is very remarkable that the second period in the history of philosophy, the medieval period, also begins with that same century; and it is not to be expected that this can rest on mere accident. Historical research alone will be able to answer the questions raised, if it enters deeply enough into the history of the general spiritual development of mankind; but it can already be said with certainty that it could enter into life at all only through the cooperation of quite different, and in part hitherto insufficiently appreciated, factors. Substantially, all peoples of the ancient civilized world gradually came into contact with one another through the world-embracing Roman Empire and inherited one another's spiritual treasures. Among all that worked together in this way, one in particular exercised a decisive creative influence, whose great significance has often been misunderstood: the people of the Indians. They were called to solve the task set by the spirit of the age, and they have brilliantly succeeded in solving it. The Brahmins erected a pure arithmetic and, as its support, a number system out of which much that is beautiful and useful has since grown. Because of this wholly new spiritual creation, the influence of the Indians upon modern Europe is all the mightier, for being quieter and less noisy than that of classical antiquity; for every European schoolchild does pieces of Indian wisdom daily in his arithmetic lessons without knowing it, and even our learned men of today still stand, more than they themselves believe, under the influence of Indian modes of thought.

The Indians have rendered to mathematics a service similar to that of the Semitic peoples in the development of religion: they were the teachers of the other peoples, and their influence will last for ever. But mathematics did not travel from India straight to us; it came by the road of a long-continued and variegated contact of many peoples. The Arab conquerors entered into relations with the Indians and bestowed on their spiritual character a mighty stimulus; since the eighth century, at the court of the caliphs of Baghdad, a lively scientific commerce between Arabs and Indians has been documented. The spirit of Greek mathematics, too, was gradually assimilated by the Arabs, insofar as they came into contact with the Greeks in Syria, Egypt, and Spain. Although it might almost seem as if the two opposed powers of human knowledge, Greek geometry and Indian algebra, would cancel each other out, a mighty mathematical development awoke among the Arabs. Insofar as we can know the mathematical achievements of the Semitic peoples in the Middle Ages, they lie decidedly on the side of Indian influence, not Greek; the Arab treatment of mathematics is far more algebraic-Indian than geometric-Greek. Indeed, the interaction of the Greek and the Indian spirit, bodied forth in separate peoples, the confrontation and conflict of their modes of thought, is in general the most remarkable and consequential event in the development of our science.

Thus enriched from all sides, medieval mathematics pressed forward slowly and laboriously; but while the Arab world at one end of Europe already enjoyed the most splendid flowering, the poor, weak West waited long for the fruits of the other peoples' labors. Westward the transmission went slowly, as the circles of culture widened; but the influence of the Indian number system, in its communication to the West from the end of the tenth century onward, at first in the imperfect form of the abacus, made itself felt in all parts of Western mathematics. If the West now accelerated its steps, it was only because it appropriated Indian arithmetic, as it appeared improved and enlarged among the Arabs. At the same time — as the surge of Muslim intellectual life gradually slackened, its manifold activity silenced, and its youthful power aged — the new Europe begins its scientific career at the beginning of the thirteenth century, with the reception in Italy of the genuine Indian number system together with the whole mathematical learning of the Arabs.

But brilliant as the names of the Italian mathematicians from the beginning of the thirteenth to that of the seventeenth century are, their treatment of mathematics remained faithful to that Indian-Arab character which had been imparted to it at its reception. Their works stand in the most decisive contrast to the classical productions of Greek geometry; yet the reverence for the Greek geometers stands so high in Europe that no Italian of that time could follow their paths. Translating and commenting on Greek masterpieces and solving algebraic problems were the sole occupations of the mathematical West up to the end of the sixteenth century; the new science of algebra did not desist from these tasks until it had fulfilled them. For more than three hundred years a one-sided activity in arithmetic and algebra persisted in the West. One-sidedness gave it immense power, but at the same time prevented many a worthwhile harvest of geometric knowledge.

It is Descartes who first united the two elements of mathematics, geometric magnitude and discrete number, which had not known each other through long centuries, and points out to modern mathematics its path. The concept of number is extended to the continuous, variable magnitude. Everything in Greek mathematics was framed to exclude all variability from the magnitudes treated. The moderns have discovered in variability the most fruitful principle of all mathematics. Insofar as the mind was gradually brought to understand the paradoxes of change without contradiction, mechanics came to their aid, and the methods of the infinitely small emerged, methods that have been ennobled and improved by the subtlest minds in an unceasing development down to our own day. The new science of "Analysis" — a name owed more to chance than to any fitness — still determines the character of all mathematics today. Even geometry and algebra rose again to new life in our century, not least because they took up again that fundamental idea of variation into their foundations and methods.

To the further development of these ideas, and to their demonstration in detail, this work is dedicated.

NUMBERS AND NUMBER-WORDS IN THE PRE-SCIENTIFIC PERIOD

Book: Zur Geschichte der Mathematik in Alterthum und Mittelalter, 1874. Printed pp. 6–22.

The period whose facts we now come to view precedes the truly scientific treatment of mathematics. It has its significance, however, in that it allows the first beginnings of mathematics to be recognized, beginnings which only later attained full clarity established in concepts. Every man must, in his own development, run through this period, for the beginnings of the Indo-European peoples are recapitulated in every child. Thus the observed facts, when the individual has grown accustomed to abstracting, will no longer seem strange to him.

The child's first mathematical activity is counting. Indeed, even certain animals succeed in distinguishing small groups of things; a duck that counts her young knows when one is missing. But this is not counting; it is a mere sensual perception of a familiar group. Counting, by contrast, is genuinely the first mathematical activity, for by it an abstraction is for the first time brought forth that can lead further. This abstraction, the concept of number, requires that one not merely grasp a multiplicity of distinct things just as they occur, but abstract from their qualities and attend only to their multitude, uttering this abstractly in the word of the sign that we call number.

Counting clearly begins, under normal circumstances, on the fingers and toes; this has been established by all observers of savage peoples. The counting itself gives rise to the symbols for the numbers, which we must consider next in their two natural forms: as number-words in the spoken language, and as ciphers in the written.



The origin of the number-words

The number-words belong to the oldest stratum of language, incised in the earliest times of a people, and they stay as they are and are carried along through all the changes of language, almost unaltered. The evidence for this is above all the agreement of certain number-words among peoples who are quite separated from one another (Sanskrit, Zend, Persian, Greek, Roman, Celtic, Germanic, Slavic). Such an agreement unequivocally signals the unity of the Indo-Germanic (Aryan) primitive language, just as the same fact at the same time shows that the number-words, alongside certain kinship terms, form the oldest foundation of language, because it was they above all that could survive such a process of gradual change.

But further inquiry here leads to the difficult question of the origin of these number-words. In particular, insofar as the number-words belong especially to the oldest layer of every language formation with us, it is also impossible to demonstrate shorter verbal roots in them; but it is also not to be given up, for it has been shown that no verbal roots appear in the number-words. The attempt to trace the number-words for 1, 2, 3 back to the personal pronouns I, you, he is indeed ingenious; but whether it is phonetically tenable for the thousands of languages in question has not been tested by any of them.

The view has often been advanced that the number-words are in origin nothing other than concrete designations of sensible objects in use as symbols for a number; for example, 5 with the meaning "hand" among certain Malay peoples, 11 and 12 with the meanings "foot one," "foot two" among an American people, 20 with the meaning "man, whole man," and among some African peoples "heap, mountain." But such designations are in fact to be found only in certain languages; we must restrict such observations to fresh and vigorous peoples. Nations with higher intellectual capacities seem early to have felt the essentially abstract character of numbers, and to have pushed back as far as possible the concrete picture to which the number-word might possibly allude. With that, however, the number-words fell victim to that process of purely phonetic decay which in the course of time gradually let their origin be forgotten. [Hankel's note: Pott, Die quinäre und vigesimale Zählmethode bei Völkern aller Welttheile, Halle 1847, with its supplement, Die Sprachverschiedenheit in Europa aus den Zahlwörtern nachgewiesen, sowie die quinäre und vigesimale Zählmethode, Halle 1868, supplied me with the material for my treatment of the number-words.]



The principle of the number system

It would be an impossibility to designate all numbers, up to any considerable height, by particular self-subsistent words. In the undifferentiated series of numbers there must be given certain resting-points from which one can orient oneself, and fixed rules according to which every single point in the number series is to be reached; the numbers must be brought into a system that has to fulfil the ideal demand: to represent every number according to a fixed principle, with the clearest intuitiveness, out of as few fixed numbers as possible.

This problem, difficult in such generality, the peoples — when they began to designate numbers beyond the lowest region — would not have been able to solve by abstract speculation; happily, however, their accustomed manner of counting off on the fingers put the solution of the task into their hands before they had even thought of the task itself. When one had counted through the fingers of one or both hands, or, where it was the custom, the toes as well, one made oneself a mark as a reminder — either a stroke in the sand, or one laid aside, as the Negroes do, a pebble or a kernel of maize — and began again afresh to count on the fingers. If one came to the end once more, one made a second mark, and so on.

When it now came to setting up a system of number-words, it was only necessary to reproduce this procedure in words: first to designate, by particular names, the quantities that could be counted off on the fingers themselves — the ones. The last of these number-words then gave at the same time the ground-number represented by that mark, which we will in general call X. The following numbers were now represented, corresponding to that manner of counting, according to the scheme X + 1, X + 2 …; and this aggregation was expressed by a determinate grammatical form; so it went on up to X + X = 2X; and it lay in the whole manner of conception that one rendered this number grammatically, as also 3X, 4X, by a certain combination of the number-words for the ones 2, 3, 4 with that for the ground-number X. From 2X one proceeded again, by aggregation of 1, 2, to 2X + 1, 2X + 2, and so could designate all numbers up to X·X unambiguously according to a fixed principle. The number greater by X, X·X + X, could however be represented either according to this scheme (in the decimal system: "ten-ten and ten") or according to the scheme (X + 1)X (in the decimal system "eleven-ten") — on the choice that was made, the whole systematic structure of the numbers depended. With a happy instinct one took the first scheme, regarding the ground-number X, like 1, as a unit, only of another order or step, which must not be multiplied beyond the X-fold. This thought, even if it did not then come to clear consciousness, now guided the further steps: the number X·X counted in a similar way again as a unit, which was grammatically combined with the ones according to the scheme 2[X·X], 3[X·X], to designate its multiples. With that wonderful instinct which so often reveals itself in the formation of language and in other advances of culture in the earliest period of the human race, one obtained, proceeding consistently in this way, a number system fully answering to the ideal demand above, whose idea we can represent in pure, abstract form as follows:

A number system with the ground-number X is that arrangement of the numbers according to which they are expressed by an aggregate proceeding according to powers of X, with coefficients all smaller than X.

The numbers from 1 to X − 1 may be called ones; the powers X, X², X³ … units or step-numbers of the 1st, 2nd, 3rd step; and every number additively composed out of their multiples, an aggregated number.

With the exception of the crudest peoples, inaccessible to all culture — notably African peoples, who move only among the lowest numbers and can hardly count to 20, sometimes in fact not to 5 — all peoples have arrived at a number system, and all have in the main applied the principle just developed. This seems to me a fact well worthy of attention. For here there is an agreement which was by no means necessary from the nature of the matter: in all sorts of ways one could have satisfied the need to order the numbers systematically; nor has any interchange taken place here among the peoples, as the age and the national character of the number-words, but above all the diversity of the ground-number X, sufficiently proves. We stand here before one of those points where the unity of the human race overcomes all individual diversity.

The designations of the ones and of the ground-number, regulated by no principle, were chosen in the most diverse ways everywhere, and their agreement between two peoples proves their kinship of stock. But as concerns the composition of these absolute number-words, it is evident that it had everywhere to turn out essentially the same. Everywhere, both the aggregation and the multiplication had to be expressed by a determinate grammatical combination of the number-words of the two parts, and in such a way that, for example, both in X + 8 and in 8X the two number-words for X and 8 are clearly recognizable; for only then was the character of the system preserved, and with it the purpose attained for the sake of which it was formed at all.

With the exception of a single language from the interior of Africa (Bomu), which expresses the multiples of ten by specific new words unconnected with the number-words from 1 to 10, we find this demand, as far as our knowledge reaches, everywhere fulfilled in the main. [Hankel's note: W. v. Humboldt, Kawisprache, vol. I, p. 22.]

But the manner in which those two number-words were grammatically combined with one another, to express now their aggregation, now their multiplication, was left entirely to the free-reigning sense of language, and has therefore shaped itself very differently among the different peoples. Interesting as it would be to come to know this manifold more closely, which embraces almost all possibilities, we should stray with it further into the territory of linguistics than our space allows.



Number-words for the step-numbers

We must add, further, that all languages have felt the need to replace the directly given but cumbersome designation of the higher step-numbers — X² by "X times X," X³ by "X times X times X," and so on — by a simpler one, and to lay down particular names for these step-numbers. Not seldom words may have been used for these names which originally express an indefinite multiplicity, such as "multitude," "heap," and so on; in fact, in Greek the number-word for ten thousand at the same time means an indefinite great multitude, for the difference of accent (mýrioi, myrioí) in these two meanings may well be regarded as a later invention of the grammarians. The designations of hundred in the Indo-Germanic languages (Sanskrit sata, Gothic hunda, Latin centum, Greek hekatón, and so on) have recently been derived from a hypothetical word of the primitive language formed according to the scheme "ten tens" (dakanta-dakanta), just as Gothic in fact names the second step-number in this way. [Hankel's note: Schleicher, Compendium der vergleichenden Grammatik, II, p. 397 ff.]

The series of step-numbers designated absolutely in this way will presumably have extended itself along with their extended use. As an example of this one may cite the interesting fact that in the different Indo-Germanic languages the number-words for 1000 show no resemblance to one another at all, while those for the numbers 2–9, 10, 100 show a clear kinship, so that one may well assume: the diverging branches of this language-stock possessed as common inheritance only the number-words up to 100, and created the number-word for 1000 independently of one another only after their separation.

Beyond the unit of the 3rd step, the simple designation of step-numbers in older times extends in only a small number of languages (thus in Greek myrioi = 10,000).

For the higher steps, number-words have formed among the European peoples only gradually, as need required. The "million," which in Italian seems originally to have designated a concrete measure, 10 tons of gold, I find as an abstract number-word for the first time in 1494, in a widely circulated Italian arithmetic. [Hankel's notes: Baltzer, Berichte der Sächsischen Gesellschaft der Wissenschaften, math.-phys. Classe, 1865, p. 2; Pacioli, Summa de arithmetica, fol. 18: "mille migliaria, che fa secondo el volgo el milione."] "Billion," which is not yet found there, was invented alongside "trillion" and so on at the beginning of the seventeenth century, but was used more frequently by astronomers and mathematicians only in the last century. The word "Milliarde," which in France has appeared for about half a century as a determinate number-word in the language of the financial world, has also become naturalized in Germany in recent times.

The only people which since ancient times, exceeding every possible need, out of a particular inclination toward the monstrous, has developed a great series of step-numbers, is that of the Aryan Indians, which already in mythical times possesses popular, even poetically usable, number-words up to the 17th step. [Hankel's note: In the Mahabharata, the national epic composed many years before Christ, these number-words occur in decimal progression up to 100,000 billion. A. Weber, Zeitschrift der Deutschen Morgenländischen Gesellschaft XV, 1861, p. 132.]

It is beyond doubt that the consistent carrying-through of the principle of the number system in language also demands specific names of the step-numbers up to the limit to which one intends to utter numbers, since only so is it possible to avoid the offence against the purity of the system which we Germans, for example, must commit in "thirty thousand" or "three hundred thousand" for want of particular names for the step-numbers of the 5th and 6th order, and everywhere to avoid multiplying the step-numbers beyond the tenfold. But how different the transparency of a number can be according to the manner in which it is uttered, an example may prove. Let it be a matter of uttering the number 86789325178.

1) Adam Ries, the famous German master of reckoning, divides it for this purpose thus: 86-789-325-178 and speaks: [Hankel's note: Rechenung auff der Linien und Federn, 1585 — as printed.] "Six-and-eighty-thousand, thousand-times-thousand, seven hundred thousand-times-thousand, nine-and-eighty thousand-times-thousand, three hundred thousand, five-and-twenty thousand, one hundred, eight and seventy."

2) With the help of the new number-word "million," the number written 86789-325178 is now uttered thus: eighty-six thousand, seven hundred eighty-nine millions, three hundred twenty-five thousand, one hundred seventy-eight. [Hankel's grouping; the German runs: "sechs und achtzig tausend, sieben hundert neun und achtzig Millionen, drei hundert fünf und zwanzig tausend, ein hundert acht und siebzig."]

3) The Indian uses no preliminary grouping of the figures at all; he reads: 8 kharva, 6 padma, 7 vyarbuda, 8 koti, 9 prayuta, 3 laksa, 2 ayuta, 5 sahasra, 1 sata, 7 dasan, 8. [As printed.]

How much this simplicity in the verbal expression of great numbers contributed to making reckoning with great numbers easier for the Indians, even before the invention of their later cipher-system, we shall show later; here it may however still be remarked that the Indian scholars created names for step-numbers far beyond the limit designated above, which admittedly remained without any practical use. [Hankel's note: The Arab Al-Biruni, who visited India at the beginning of the eleventh century, lists the names of the step-numbers up to 10^17, but says …; see Wöpcke, Journal asiatique, Paris 1863, pp. 275–290. The quotation is truncated in the original note.]

It hangs together most closely with a direction toward the measureless, the fantastic, the infinite, peculiar to the Indians in general, whose idea they sought, as it were, to snatch in rapid progression through the single steps, and to grasp in monstrous numbers. Already the oldest literature of the Brahmins, the Vedas, contains not a few examples of this; in the Mahabharata a king, in affect magnifying himself in decimal progression, gives his riches as 100,000 billion; and in the somewhat later Ramayana the monkey-prince sets against his enemy 10,000 sextillions of monkeys in battle! [Hankel's note: A. Weber, "Vedische Angaben über Zeittheilung und hohe Zahlen," Zeitschrift der Deutschen Morgenländischen Gesellschaft XV, 1861, p. 135.]

But all this remains far behind what the Buddhists achieved in hollow fantasticality. Thirty million sons of gods; six hundred thousand million sons of the Buddhas; twenty-four thousand billion divinities — under such pictures they conceive the sublime. Of the third or great system of numbers, whose ground-number is an immense power of ten, a later writer says "that Buddha alone could comprehend them, and that they are to awaken an idea of what there is: of the inexhaustible, unbounded nature; of the pure merits of the sages; of the periods of the existences which the fate of changed spirits runs through; of the ocean of wishes which are formed for the happiness of living beings, and of the concatenation of the laws which form the infinite development of the worlds." [Hankel's note: S. Abel Rémusat, Mélanges posthumes d'histoire et de littérature orientales, Paris 1843, p. 68.] Just because of this edifying character which was attributed to great numbers, the continuation of the series of names of step-numbers beyond the usual limit up to the 54th may have been put into the mouth of the founder of the religion, Buddha himself. "And this," Buddha adds, "is only one addition; of higher orders there is nothing but an exaggeration of the pedants." Above this counting there is still another, above this again another, and above this five or six others still. [Hankel's note: In the Lalitavistara; see Wöpcke, Journal asiatique, Paris 1863, I, pp. 256, 275–290.]

Indeed, as if all this were not enough, names have later been formed in Buddhist writings for the step-numbers which, setting m equal to a high power of ten, we can write m, m², m³, …, so that the number of figures with which the last step-number would have to be written is itself expressed by a number of some forty places! [Hankel's note: Schiefner, Bulletin de l'Académie impériale des sciences de St.-Pétersbourg, 1863, t. V, p. 299. The exponents are illegible in the machine-read text of the scan; flagged for the page-image pass.]

A similar drive to bring the infinite to intuition under the picture of great numbers, as it was a national disposition among the Aryan Indians, further developed by Buddhism, and with it also impregnated into the neighbouring peoples in Tibet and China, we find nowhere else.

The Greeks in their classical period altogether lacked the pull toward the transcendental infinite. In poetry the numbers keep within moderate bounds, and when Homer has the wounded Ares scream like 10,000 men, or Hephaestus, hurled down from Olympus, fall a whole day toward the earth, this has more of a humorous than a sublime character. [Hankel's note: Iliad V, 860; I, 590.] The numerical statements never exceed myriads of myriads, and Archimedes was the first to create, in purely scientific interest, a system for the expression of greater numbers.

The Pythagorean and Platonic mystics indeed also sought secrets in numbers, but not in their magnitude: in their properties and forms.

In a somewhat different sense, number serves the Semitic mind as a symbol of mysterious relations of realities, and is systematically employed in this way in the Kabbala; but it is only seldom the means of representing the sublime, and even the mystical-prophetic writings of the Bible do not go beyond myriads of myriads. [Hankel's note: Daniel VII, 10; Revelation V, 11; IX, 16.]

If the imagination is to be lifted to the infinite, it is directed to the stars of heaven, which Jehovah alone can number, or the dust on the earth. [Hankel's note: Genesis XIII, 16; XV, 5; Numbers XXIII, 10; Psalm 147, v. 4.] Among the Indians, as we shall see, is found the first attempt to count the dust; but here too it is Buddha himself who solves this sublime task.



The diversity of the ground-numbers

By the principle of a purposeful system of numbers established above, everything essential is determined except the ground-number. Theoretically taken, every number has an equal right to this place of honour; yet here too the demand arises to build the whole system out of as few elements as possible. Since one will certainly not use the larger prime numbers, such as 7, 11, as ground-numbers — one not being accustomed to grouping things by such companies of 7 or of 11 — the most natural candidates for ground-numbers are about 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 10, 12, 20. If one chooses one of the smaller numbers, one indeed needs few ones, but all the more step-numbers to name absolutely; with larger ground-numbers, the reverse. If, for example, one wanted to develop a binary system, then in order to express the numbers up to about 1000 — whither most languages usually go — one would need, besides the number-word "one," particular names for the ten step-numbers 2, 4, 8, 16, 32, 64, 128, 256, 512, 1024; in the decimal system, names for the nine ones together with the step-numbers 10, 100, 1000 are necessary — in the first case 11, in the second 12, primitive number-words. For the ground-numbers 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 10, 12, 20 there would be necessary, up to a thousand: 11, 8, 8, 8, 9, 12, 13, 21; up to a million: 21, 15, 12, 13, 13, 15, 17, 24 simple number-words. If one wanted to let the smallest possible quantity of these stand as the sole motive for the choice of the ground-number, then by this reckoning — even if one extends the number-realm up to a million — the systems with the ground-numbers 4, 5, 6 would have the preference, which moreover enjoy the convenient property that in them the multiplication table, which one must necessarily have in one's head for reckoning (and which in the binary system contracts into the single formula 1·1 = 1), would be of very small extent. Yet these ground-numbers have again the disadvantage of splitting a somewhat large number into too many parts, and thereby making its verbal (and written) expression altogether too cumbersome. So one will indeed reach for a larger ground-number, and, in order not to let the multiplication table swell too much, give the preference to the decimal or the duodecimal system.

But the peoples, in carrying out their number system, had no such abstract considerations to make, which did not correspond to their nature at all. The way they had to take in forming their number system was marked out for them beforehand by their spiritual and bodily nature. For as it already says in a children's verse: "Ten fingers have I, on each hand five, and twenty on hands and feet"; these members of their body they had already used for counting, and so the ground-numbers 5 or 10 or 20 were inborn in them by nature. In fact, no people on the whole earth has ever developed its number system with any consistency from any other ground-number than one of those named — in which fact one will at the same time find the striking proof that the systems of number-words everywhere attach themselves to the older counting with the fingers. [Hankel's note: The report (Pott, Die quinäre Zählmethode, p. 75) that the New Zealanders produced the monstrosity of a consistent system with the ground-number 11 is too little attested for us to believe it. The assertion going back to Leibniz, that the Chinese used, besides the decimal, also the binary system, rests on an error.]

The ground-number 10 is the most widespread; all civilized peoples, with few exceptions soon to be mentioned, possess the decimal system, which rests on the use of both hands for counting off.

Among the uncivilized peoples one meets the quinary system about as often as the decimal, arising from the custom of using only the fingers of one hand for counting — at least expressed in this, that simple number-words exist only up to 5, and the following number-words are formed according to the scheme 6 = 5 + 1, 7 = 5 + 2, 8 = 5 + 3, 9 = 5 + 4. Nowhere, however, is 10 expressed by 2·5, but everywhere by a simple number-word; and so the counting falls back in the end always into the decimal.

The vigesimal system, which is to be explained from the use — foreign indeed to us "booted" peoples — of fingers and toes for counting, but which in no way indicates a low state of culture, is found in fact not only in some African and Oceanic languages in imperfect development, but also among other peoples of higher intellectual capacity: so above all, in the greatest consistency, among the anciently civilized Aztecs of Mexico and the Maya Indians of Yucatan. The numbers up to 19 are formed among them decimally or quinarily; 20 possesses its own number-word, from which then, according to the scheme 30 = 1·20 + 10, 31 = 1·20 + 11, 40 = 2·20, 50 = 2·20 + 10, and so on, the following numbers up to 399 = 19·20 + 19 are formed. The step-numbers 400 = 20², 8000 = 20³ — among the Maya even 160,000 = 20^4 — have each their own number-word. In a remarkable way, two branches of the Indo-Germanic language-stock, the Caucasian and the Celtic, have also been seized by the vigesimal system, which otherwise has only a very small distribution in Asia and Europe. From the Celtic Bas-Breton, still spoken today in western Brittany, it has crept into French, where it displaces the otherwise decimal counting from 60 = soixante onward, as 70 = soixante-dix, 71 = soixante-onze and, more clearly, 80 = quatre-vingts, 90 = quatre-vingt-dix prove. Also 120 = dix-vingts, 140 = sept-vingts, 160 = huit-vingts, 300 = quinze-vingts are still used, if less frequently. Strangely enough, traces of this manner of counting are also found in Danish, where 50 is expressed by "two-and-a-half times twenty," and 70 and 90 similarly, while all other designations are purely decimal. [Hankel's German: "drittehalbmalzwanzig."]

Not always do even highly developed languages form their number-words quite consistently according to the system. We already remarked that the quinary and vigesimal systems often overflow into the decimal; likewise in the latter system there are found at times number-words of abnormal formation, namely such as point to the ground-numbers 3 or 4, or to a number divisible by these. It is only to be wondered that such do not appear more often, since in counting we are accustomed to combine things in groups of 3 or of 4, and halving and quartering are the most intuitive and natural divisions for us. Thus in the reckoning with fractions, and in the measures, coins, and weights, where what matters is essentially dividing, the ground-numbers divisible by 3 and 4 — namely 12 — have everywhere played the principal role, while decimal division appears almost nowhere.

So one may indeed designate the quaternary or duodecimal system as the one preferably corresponding to the spiritual nature of man, which however, in the domain of whole numbers, could nowhere defeat the decimal system given by bodily conditions. Indeed, in the most recent times the duodecimal is — with what right we will not investigate here — being ever further displaced by the decimal from the territory it has hitherto held.

The regular development of the system of number-words is disturbed, however, not only by the intrusion of foreign ground-numbers, but at times also — if in lesser degree — by subtraction and division being used for the formation of number-words alongside the addition and multiplication that properly correspond to the systematic fundamental thought (for example, in Basque 50 = "half hundred," in Danish 50 = "two-and-a-half times twenty").

An interesting example, which displays the various normal and abnormal formations, is given by the number 18; it is formed according to the schemes:

10 + 8, in Latin: decem et octo; 8 + 10, in Greek: oktokaideka [as transliterated]; 10, 8, in French: dix-huit; 8, 10, in German: acht-zehn ["eight-teen"]; 20 − 2, in Latin: duodeviginti ["two-from-twenty"];

in decimal, and correspondingly the forms:

3·6, in Bas-Breton: tri-omc'h [as printed; Breton]; 2·9, in Welsh: deu-naw [as printed]; 15 + 3, in Aztec: caxtolli-om-ey [as printed];

in otherwise vigesimal systems.
