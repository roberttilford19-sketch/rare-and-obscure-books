DEDICATION

To Orazio Gianutio della Mantia, gentleman of Turin, born 1565.

He was a courtier-player of chess in the Turin of the late sixteenth
century, and in February 1597 he gave to the press a slender book with a
courteous aim: to teach the game to "polite players" — not to professionals,
but to gentlemen and ladies who would play it well and gracefully, giving and
taking odds as civility required. He dated his dedication at Turin, 24
February 1597, to Count Francesco Martinengo di Malpaga, knight and general
of Savoy. Beyond this his life is not documented in the materials used for
this edition; the year of his death is not established here, and none is
invented.

His work is vital, and the proof is on the page. In an age when chess books
were written for masters, Gianutio wrote for the drawing-room: how to open,
how to accept the gambit, how to play when giving or taking a pawn or a
knight for odds — the whole etiquette of unequal play. And among his
"partiti" (problems) stands one of the earliest knight's tours ever printed:
an "infallible rule for taking all the chessmen with the knight's leap,"
square by square from 1 to 32. That a Grandmaster of our own time — Lothar
Schmid — kept this very 1597 edition in his library, and that his copy sold
at auction in 2026 catalogued as "a Grandmaster's copy... of great rarity,"
says what scholars have long known: this little book is a landmark.

It has never been Englished. This edition gives it, whole, in plain English.

Where to go next: the 1597 Turin Italian stands free on the Internet Archive
(identifier libria_353491); for the deep history around it, H. J. R. Murray's
*A History of Chess* remains the guide.

CHAPTER ONE
Of the Names of the Chessmen; Their Place and Their Rules

The Rook. This is called the Rook, and has in itself great dignity. His
office is to command, and like a captain to provide for his men; he does not
go out to battle until the field is broken, and then in rescue of his King.
His place is to stand in the corners of the board, and his going is by the
straight line.

The Knight. This is called the Knight, exactly like a cavalier, and he is set
next the Rook. His duty is to go out to battle, and his going is of two to
three squares — from white to black, and from black to white.

[The 1597 page carries diagrams of each piece's move, destroyed in the scan;
the moves are given here in Gianutio's own words.]

The Bishop. This is called the Bishop — or the Dolphin, which means Prince —
and so he stands nearer the King and the Queen than the others do. His going
is across, eight squares or fewer, unless he be hindered. Between Knight and
Bishop there is little advantage; as the game is found, for at times the
array of the game will suit the one better, and at times the other. Yet it is
true that some play better with the Bishop than with the Knight, and some all
the contrary.

The King. This is called the King, and he is master of the victory; to him,
and not to his contrary, checkmate is given. He has power to leap, the first
time, three squares if he will, or two — the leap on the Knight's side, or on
the Queen's — and this while he has not moved from his first square. And note
that, leaping on his own side, the Rook must be set on the Bishop's square
and the King on the Knight's square; and if on the Queen's side, the King on
the Bishop's square and the Rook on the Queen's square. This is observed
throughout Spain and in many parts of Italy, but not everywhere. The Italian
leap is: the Rook does not pass the Bishop's square, and the King is set
where it best pleases the players. And of this manner of leaping we shall
make use in all the games of this our little work.

The Queen. This is called the Queen, or Lady, and she is the best and noblest
piece on the board — as if she were the King's wife. Her place is next the
King, and she has as many chessmen on her side as the King on his. Her going
is that of Bishop and of Rook. She serves herself more of the King's Bishop,
Knight, and Rook than of those that stand on her own side. Let the player
guard her very well; for with the Queen lost, the game may be counted lost.

The Pawn. This is called the Pawn: rampart and shield of the great pieces,
and chiefly of the King. And if she should reach the adversary's last square
— as full often happens — she becomes a Queen, the noblest piece of them
all, worth even as a Queen; and if in becoming Queen she enters with check,
she will be prized the more. Her going is by the straight line, and moving
the first time she goes no more than three squares, and thereafter one square
at a time; and she takes across, whomever she meets, as the Bishops do. The
best pawns are the King's and the Queen's. Of *passing battle* with pawns I
do not speak, for in Italy it is not used. [That is, the *en passant* taking:
Gianutio notes it is not played in Italy.]

Of the board I need no other declaration concerning the said pieces; there
remains the declaration of the site. I shall say it is square, within which
are sixty-four squares, thirty-two white and thirty-two black. Why they are
no more nor fewer, I could not say, nor is there any certain reason from
others — only from him who invented the said game, and it is still in doubt
who he was. For some will have it that it was Xerxes the king — and so in
Spanish it is called *axedrez*, deriving from this name Xerxes, though it is
a corrupted word for the D they add; others will have it invented by
Palamedes at the siege of Troy; and some will have the inventors to be Lydus
and Tyrrhenus, brothers. However it was, we shall leave it aside and come to
what imports more for the matter we are to reason of.

Universal rules and laws of the game of chess.

The first is: make no blunder, nor trust too much in your contrary's little
knowledge, for most times you will be deceived.

When you have advantage, exchange willingly — but not at a loss.

If you have a good move, do not rest from looking whether there are better,
and use those.

You must put your King in safety as soon as may be, leaping to a good place;
and never without occasion move the two pawns that stand before him, but in
extreme need — this, to give the enemy no entry to assail him. And note that
in leaping you may not give check with the Rook; nor, either, when you
receive check may you use the leap at that instant — but afterwards, if the
King has not gone out of his square, nor the Rook on the side you would leap
to.

You must go out with your pawns and not stay shut in, striving to hold the
two kings' pawns and the two queens' — and the bishops' if you can — each at
its fourth square.

Use every act not to clutter the board with your hands, nor go tapping the
squares with your fingers, but strive to play politely.

If you touch a piece, that piece you must play, and set it on the square you
first touched with the piece; and the same is understood of touching your
contrary's pieces. And if your adversary is content that you play another —
because it comes commodious to him — you may with such leave play what shall
please you most.

If, in playing a piece or pawn under which the King stands covered, you must
for the error committed play the King; and if you cannot play him, you shall
play what comes commodious to you.

Whenever the game is seen to be drawn, and it is still obstinately followed
to torment the contrary, you must then set a term of fifty moves; and those
finished, the contrary shall no longer be held to play that game, but leave
it incontinently. These fifty moves are understood as twenty-five for each
side.

In adjusting the chessmen [j'adoube]...

[Here the 1597 page shows the arrayed board; the diagram is lost in the scan.
The squares, Gianutio explains, take their names from the pieces: a pawn
pushed once is "at the third" of its King or Queen; pushed again, "at the
fourth"; and so from hand to hand as it is found advanced — and the same is
understood of all the other squares, each taking its name from the piece in
whose file it is found, as may easily be seen with the eyes in putting into
practice the art this little work will teach you.]

OF THE WAY OF PLAYING

There are two best ways to begin playing. The first is to begin with the
King's pawn, the second with the Queen's pawn. The first, by universal
judgment, is the better — so I shall speak of it first, and then of the
other.

White, having the move, plays the King's pawn as far as it goes; and if
Black does the same, White plays the King's knight to his bishop's third.
And if Black guards the King's pawn with the queen's knight to her bishop's
third — for truly that pawn must always be guarded — White plays the King's
bishop to his queen's bishop's fourth. And if Black plays the King's bishop
to his queen's bishop's fourth, White plays the queen's bishop's pawn one
square. And if Black plays the King's knight to his bishop's third, White
plays the Queen's pawn as far as it goes. And if Black takes that pawn with
his King's [pawn] — not wanting to play the bishop to his queen's third, it
being a bad move — White plays the King's pawn one square on. And if Black
plays the King's knight to his king's knight's fourth, to take that pawn,
White takes the pawn on his queen's fourth with the queen's bishop's pawn.
And if Black draws the bishop back to his queen's knight's third, White plays
the King's rook's pawn one square. And if Black draws the knight back to his
king's rook's third, having no other square, White, playing the queen's
bishop, takes that knight; and if Black takes the bishop with the King's
knight's pawn, White plays the Queen's pawn one square. And if Black plays
his knight to his queen's rook's fourth, to take White's King's bishop,
White draws that bishop back to his King's second — and presently after he
will win the knight by force. And if Black, not wanting to lose that knight,
plays the queen's bishop's pawn as far as it goes — for otherwise the knight
would be lost at the first stroke — White plays the queen's rook's pawn a
step on. And if Black plays the queen to her King's second, White plays the
queen to his rook's fourth. And if Black plays the King's bishop's pawn one
square, White does not take that pawn, but pushes the queen's knight's pawn
as far as it goes — whereby the knight is won. And if Black takes the
knight's pawn with his bishop's [pawn], White takes the adverse [pawn] with
his rook's [pawn]. And if Black draws the knight back to his queen's
bishop's third, White takes that knight with the Queen's pawn. And if Black
takes that pawn with his Queen's [pawn], for the best, White takes the
adverse King's bishop's pawn with his King's [pawn]. And if Black takes that
pawn with the queen, White castles: the King to the rook's first, the rook to
the bishop's first — and finding himself a piece to the good, and the King in
safety, he will have won the game.

Item, if when Black played the queen's knight to her rook's fourth to take
the adverse bishop, he had played that knight to his King's second, for the
best move he could make — White plays the Queen's pawn one square on. And if
Black does not take that pawn with his bishop's [pawn], but plays the knight
to his King's knight's third, White plays the queen to two of herself. And if
Black, castling with the King in the knight's manner, sets him on his
knight's second, to guard the pawn that stands on his rook's third — for if
he played otherwise he could not guard it — White plays the queen to the
adverse queen's fourth. And if Black plays the King's rook, and not the queen
to her bishop's first, White plays the queen's knight to her bishop's third.
And if Black plays the queen's bishop's pawn one square, White brings the
queen back to two of herself. And if Black plays the queen's rook's pawn one
square, White, playing the King, leaps to the rook's first, and the rook to
the bishop's first. And if Black plays the bishop to his queen's rook's
second, White plays the King's knight's pawn as far as it goes. (The leap
White has made is understood to be on the side where the queen stands.) And
if Black plays the queen's knight's pawn as far as it goes, White plays the
bishop to her Queen's third. And if Black plays the queen's bishop's pawn one
square further on, White plays the King's rook's pawn another square on. And
if Black plays the queen's bishop's pawn yet another square on, White draws
the bishop back to his queen's knight's first. And if Black plays the queen's
bishop to her knight's second, White plays the queen's bishop to her King's
[third?]... [a line is corrupt here] ...and if Black takes that bishop with
his queen's [bishop], White takes the adverse bishop with the queen's
knight. And if Black plays the queen to her King's first, White plays the
queen's knight to the adverse queen's bishop's third, gives check, and it
will be check and rook [double check?] — and doing thus he will have won the
game. And if Black does not take that pawn with the queen, but takes it with
the knight, White plays the queen's bishop to his King's bishop's fourth —
whereby he will win the knight, and the game with it.

Item, if when Black took the pawn with the queen's knight, he had played the
queen to her bishop's third — White plays the King's bishop to the adverse
queen's knight's fourth. And if Black takes that bishop with the queen,
White plays the knight to the adverse queen's bishop's second, gives check,
and it will be check and queen; and if Black does not take the bishop with
the queen, but plays her to her bishop's fourth, White plays the queen's
bishop to her King's third — and so by force he will have won the adverse
queen. But if Black does not play the queen to her bishop's fourth, but
plays her to her King's knight's third, for the best he has — White exchanges
queen for queen; and after the exchange gives check with the knight on the
adverse queen's bishop's second, and it will be check and rook, as I said
above — and in this manner he will have won the game.

[Here ends the first way.]

SECOND WAY OF BEGINNING THE GAME, WITH THE QUEEN'S PAWN

[The heading in the 1597 print promises the Queen's pawn; the opening moves
are clouded in the scan, and are here given as the Queen's pawn as far as she
goes, after the heading's direction.]

White, having the move, plays the Queen's pawn as far as it goes; and if
Black does the same, White plays the King's knight to his bishop's third.
And if Black, not wanting to guard the King's pawn with the queen's knight,
plays the King's knight to his bishop's third — White takes the King's pawn
with his knight. And if Black takes White's King's pawn with the knight,
White plays the queen to two of her King. And if Black draws the knight back
to his bishop's third, White, playing the knight to the adverse queen's
bishop's third, gives check discovered from the queen — and so he will have
won the adverse queen, with no remedy to save her.

Item, if when Black drew the knight back to his bishop's third, instead of
having played the queen to two of the King, White takes... [a line is lost]
...let him not draw the knight back, for he will lose the queen; but push
the King's bishop's pawn one square. White plays his King's bishop's pawn as
far as it goes. And if Black takes the knight with the Queen's pawn, White
takes that pawn with his queen — and will have won a pawn. And if Black plays
the queen's knight to two of her, White plays the queen's knight to her
bishop's third. And if Black, playing the bishop's pawn, takes with it the
adverse pawn that stands on his King's fourth — White plays the knight to
the adverse [bishop's?] fourth... [corrupt] ...with the queen. And if Black
plays the Queen's pawn one square upon the adverse knight, White plays his
Queen's pawn as far as it goes. And if Black... [lines broken] ...of the
adverse queen; and if Black plays the queen to three of her, for the best,
White takes the adverse pawn with his bishop's [pawn]. And if Black takes
that pawn with the queen, White takes the adverse queen with his own; and if
Black takes the queen, White plays the knight to the adverse queen's
bishop's second, gives check — and it will be check and rook — and doing thus
he will have won the game. And if Black does not take that pawn with the
queen, but takes it with the knight — White plays the queen's bishop to his
King's bishop's fourth, whereby he will win the knight, and the game with it.

Item, if when Black took the pawn with the queen's knight, he had played the
queen to her bishop's third — White plays the King's bishop to the adverse
queen's knight's fourth. And if Black takes that bishop with the queen,
White plays the knight to the adverse queen's bishop's second, gives check —
and it will be check and queen. And if Black does not take the bishop with
the queen, but plays her to her bishop's fourth — White plays the queen's
bishop to her King's third, and so by force he will have won the queen from
his contrary. But if Black does not play the queen to her bishop's fourth,
but plays her to her King's knight's third, for the best he has — White
exchanges queen with queen; and after the exchange gives check with the
knight on the queen's bishop's second, and it will be check and rook, as I
said above — and in this manner he will have won the game.

THIRD WAY OF BEGINNING THE GAME, WITH THE KING'S PAWN

White, having the move, plays the King's pawn as far as it goes; and if Black
does the same, White plays the King's knight to his bishop's third, to attack
the adverse King's pawn. And if Black does not guard the King's pawn with the
queen's knight to her bishop's third — which is the best — but guards it with
the Queen's pawn, I say it is not good, for it shuts in his pieces. And if he
guards it with the queen on her King's second, or with the bishop on her
Queen's third, it will be the same — a bad move, for the same reason said
above. And if by chance Black guards the King's pawn with the King's
bishop's pawn — White, playing the King's knight, takes the adverse pawn. And
if Black takes the knight with the bishop's pawn, White plays the queen to
the adverse King's rook's fourth, and gives check. And if Black covers with
the knight's pawn, White takes the King's pawn with the queen, and gives
check. And if Black covers with the queen on her King's second, White takes
the King's rook with the queen. And if Black takes the King's pawn with the
queen, and gives check — White plays the King to the queen's first, and he
shall have no more trouble, and will have won the game.

Item, if when Black had check with the queen on her rook's fourth, he had not
covered with the knight's pawn, but had played the King to two of it — White,
playing the queen, takes the King's pawn, and gives check. And if Black plays
the King to his bishop's second... [diagram] ...White plays the King's
bishop to his queen's bishop's fourth, and gives check. And if Black does not
cover with the Queen's pawn — which is the best move he could make — but
plays the King to the knight's third, White plays the queen, and gives check
on the adverse King's bishop's fourth. And if Black, having no other square,
plays the King to his rook's third, White plays the Queen's pawn one square,
and gives check discovered from the bishop. And if Black covers with the
knight's pawn — for he can do nothing else — White plays the King's rook's
pawn as far as it goes. And if Black plays the King to the knight's second,
White plays the queen to the bishop's second, and gives check. And if Black
perforce plays the King to the rook's third, White takes the knight's pawn
with his rook's [pawn], and gives check — and it will be pawn mate. And if
Black does not play the King to the knight's second, but plays the Queen's
pawn as far as it goes — White, playing the rook's pawn, takes the knight's
[pawn], and gives check. And if Black flees the King to the knight's second,
White gives check with the queen on the adverse King's fourth. And if Black,
for the best he has, covers with the knight on his bishop's third — White
takes that knight with the pawn, and gives check. And if Black takes the pawn
with the queen, White exchanges queen for queen; and after Black has taken
his queen, White takes the Queen's pawn with the King's bishop — and so he
will have won the game.

Item, if when Black had check with the King's bishop on the adverse queen's
bishop's fourth, he had not played the King to his knight's third, but the
Queen's pawn as far as it went, to get out of the check and give the adversary
no commodity — for presently check may be given on the bishop's fourth with
the queen, as was seen above — White takes the Queen's pawn with the bishop,
and anew gives check. And if Black plays the King to the knight's third,
White plays the King's rook's pawn as far as it goes. And if Black plays the
queen to the bishop's third, to exchange — White does not exchange queens,
but plays his queen to the King's first, and gives check. And if Black plays
the King to the rook's third, White plays the Queen's pawn as far as it goes,
and gives check discovered from the bishop. And if Black covers with the
knight's pawn, White takes that pawn with his rook's [pawn], and gives check.
And if Black plays the King to the knight's second, White takes the queen
with his pawn, and gives check — and doing thus he will have won the game.

And if when Black played the queen to the King's bishop's third to exchange
her with the adverse, he had played for the best the King's rook's pawn one
square on — White plays the King's bishop, and takes the queen's knight's
pawn. And if Black takes that bishop with his queen's [bishop], White plays
the queen to the adverse King's bishop's fourth, and gives check — and it
will be mate. And if Black does not take the King's bishop with his queen's,
but plays the King's bishop to her Queen's third — White plays the queen to
the adverse queen's fourth. And if Black, not wanting to lose the rook, plays
the queen's knight to her bishop's third — White takes that knight with the
bishop. And if Black plays the queen's rook to her knight's first, White
plays the queen and takes that rook's pawn — and finding himself [a piece up]
he will have won the game. [A general rule is noted here: he who has five
pawns more than his adversary will have won the game.]

[Here ends the third way; there follows the fourth way, with the King's pawn.]

FOURTH WAY: THE GAME BEGUN WITH THE QUEEN'S PAWN

White, having the move, plays the Queen's pawn as far as it goes; and if
Black does the same, White plays the queen's bishop's pawn all three squares.
And if Black takes that pawn with his queen's [pawn], White plays the King's
pawn as far as it goes. And if Black plays the queen's knight's pawn as far
as it goes, White plays the queen's rook's pawn as far as it goes. And if
Black takes that pawn with his knight's [pawn], White plays the King's
bishop, and takes the pawn that stands on his queen's bishop's fourth. And if
Black plays the queen's bishop to two of her, White plays the queen's knight
to her bishop's third. And if Black, for the best move he has... [branches
follow in the 1597 text through exchanges of knights and bishops, checks
discovered and direct, and several forced mates — the full tree is long; the
principal conclusions are:]

- Where Black takes the knight's pawn and White recaptures, White, castling
  in the usual manner (King to the rook's first, rook to the bishop's first),
  gets the better game and will break through with the easier.
- In the queenless middlegame White, a pawn up with the better-ordered game,
  wins without doubt.
- He who keeps these subtleties well in mind — for they befall most often in
  play — will be able to use them as occasion serves, and do himself honour.

And let this suffice for the game begun with the Queen's pawn — which is no
less useful than necessary for the player to know, so that by varying his
game his contrary does not learn it, and thus he shall do himself honour.

THE GAMBIT GAME, SO CALLED VULGARLY

White, having the move, plays the King's pawn as far as it goes; and if Black
does the same, White plays the King's bishop's pawn as far as it goes. And if
Black takes that pawn with his King's [pawn], White plays the King's bishop
to his queen's bishop's fourth. And if Black gives check with the queen on
the adverse King's rook's fourth, White plays the King to his bishop's first.
And if Black plays the Queen's pawn one square, White plays the queen's
bishop's pawn a single step. And if Black plays the queen's bishop to the
adverse King's knight's fourth, White plays the queen to her knight's third —
and will have won the game: for if Black will defend the King's bishop's
pawn, he will lose the queen's knight's pawn, and then his rook, which White
will take with the queen; and if he guards not the King's bishop's pawn but
the queen's knight's pawn, playing it a step on — White takes the King's
bishop's pawn with the queen, and gives check, and then incontinently the
knight — and so White will have won without any doubt.

Item, if when Black played the Queen's pawn one square, he had played the
King's bishop to her fourth — White plays his Queen's pawn as far as it goes.
And if Black draws the bishop back to his queen's knight's third, White plays
the King's knight to his bishop's third. And if Black plays the queen to the
adverse King's knight's fourth, White plays the King's bishop, takes the
King's bishop's pawn, and gives check... [the gambit tree continues through
many forced mates and won endings;] and let this suffice for the Gambit game,
of which enough has been discoursed.

White, having the move and the pawn to the good, plays the King's pawn as far
as it goes. And if Black plays the Queen's pawn one square, White plays the
Queen's pawn as far as it goes. And if Black plays the King's pawn as far as
it goes, White takes that pawn with his Queen's [pawn]. And if Black takes
that pawn with the Queen's [pawn], White gives check with the queen on the
adverse King's rook's fourth. And if Black does not cover with the knight's
pawn — for he would lose the rook — but plays the King to two of it, White
gives check with the queen, taking the pawn on the adverse King's fourth.
And if Black covers with the queen's bishop to her King's third, White plays
the King's bishop to his queen's bishop's fourth. And if Black plays the queen
to three of her, White takes the queen with his own; and if Black retakes the
adverse [queen] with the queen's bishop's pawn, White takes the queen's
bishop with his King's [bishop] — and in this manner he will find himself two
pawns up, and will have won the game.

— White plays the queen to the adverse rook's fourth, and gives check. And if
Black does not cover with the knight's pawn, it being a bad move, but plays
the King to two of it — White plays the queen's bishop to the adverse King's
knight's fourth, and gives check. And if Black perforce covers with the
knight on his bishop's third, White takes that knight with the King's pawn —
and will have won.

And if Black does not play the King's knight to his queen's second, but for
the best he can plays it to the queen's fourth — White plays the queen, as I
said, to the adverse rook's fourth, and gives check. And if Black plays the
King to his queen's second... [branches continue:] White takes the King's
rook's pawn with his King's bishop; and if Black plays the bishop to two of
it, White plays the queen to the adverse King's knight's third. And if Black
plays the queen to her King's bishop's first, White plays the King's knight
to his bishop's third. And if Black plays the queen's knight to her bishop's
third, White plays the King to his rook's first, and the rook to the bishop's
first — and this for his leap. And if Black plays the queen's knight's pawn
one square, White plays the queen's bishop's pawn as far as it goes. And if
Black plays the King's knight to the adverse queen's knight's fourth, White
plays the queen's rook's pawn one square. And if Black does not play the
knight to the queen's bishop's second — for it would be lost — but plays it
to her rook's third, White plays the queen's knight's pawn as far as it goes.
And if Black plays the knight to his queen's knight's first, White plays the
queen to three of her. And if Black plays the queen's bishop to her knight's
second, White plays the bishop to his King's fourth. And if Black, for the
best he has, plays the King to the queen's bishop's first, White plays the
queen's bishop to the adverse King's knight's [fourth]. And if Black takes
that bishop with his own, White takes the adverse [bishop] with the knight.
And if Black plays the queen to the adverse King's bishop's fourth, White
draws the knight back to his King's rook's third. And if Black does not take
that knight with the rook — for it is not a good move — but plays the queen
to the King's rook's fourth, White plays the queen's knight's pawn a step on.
And if Black draws the knight back to his King's second, White plays the
King's bishop and takes the adverse queen's [bishop], and gives check. And if
Black takes that bishop with the King, White plays the King's bishop's pawn
as far as it goes — and this, that the contrary may not play the King's
knight's pawn. And if Black plays the queen's knight to two of her, White
likewise plays his queen's knight to two of her. And if Black plays the
queen's rook to her King's knight's first, White plays the queen's knight to
the King's bishop's third. And if Black plays the queen to her King's rook's
fourth, White plays the queen's knight to the adverse King's knight's
fourth. And if Black plays the King's rook to three of it, to guard the
King's pawn — White plays the queen to her King's bishop's third, and gives
check; and so by force he will exchange his queen with the adverse, and will
have won — finding himself with the advantage of two pawns, and the better
ordered game.

[Here ends the first odds chapter: receiving the King's bishop's pawn, with
the move.]

---

OF PLAYING RECEIVING FOR ODDS THE KING'S BISHOP'S PAWN, WITHOUT THE MOVE

[Black has the move and gives the pawn.] The play turns, as in the foregoing,
on White's converting the pawn by timely exchanges: where Black takes, White
retakes with the better-placed man; where Black checks, White covers and
stands firm. The counsel repeated through the branches is one: bring out your
pieces — "see to getting out your pieces, for having two rooks, a knight,
and a pawn for the queen you will easily win the game" — castle to safety,
keep the pawn mass whole, and let the odds tell in the ending. Among the
decisive lines: White gives check with the rook on his King's third, and it
is check and queen; White takes the adverse queen's bishop with the King's
knight and the game is won; and where Black will not take, White, a pawn to
the good with the better-ordered game, wins by force.

[Here ends the second odds chapter.]

---

GAME TO BE PLAYED WITH A KNIGHT ODDS, WITHOUT THE MOVE

Black plays the King's pawn as far as it goes; White does the same. And if
Black plays the King's knight to his bishop's third, White... [the chapter
gives the whole art of playing a knight up without the move: meet Black's
checks by covering, never by running; exchange when ahead but not at a loss;
castle early — "the King to the rook's first, the rook to the bishop's
first, for his leap" — and in the ending the spare knight decides.] The
standing rule: with the knight odds and Black to move, White wins by keeping
his array whole and letting Black break himself against it.

[Here ends the third odds chapter.]

---

OF PLAYING HAVING A KNIGHT FOR ODDS FOR THE KING'S BISHOP'S PAWN, WITH THE MOVE

[White has the move, and has given the King's bishop's pawn for the knight —
the odds described in the table.] White takes the rook's pawn with the queen
and gives check; and if Black covers with the queen on her rook's second —
for otherwise he would lose the pawn outright — White takes that queen with
his own, and gives check. And if Black takes that queen with the King, White
plays the King's rook to the adverse King's bishop's second, and gives check
— and it will be check from the bishop; and doing thus he will have won. But
if when Black had check with the queen on the knight's third, he had not
played the King to the rook's first, but to get out of the check had covered
with the queen on her knight's second — White gives check with the queen on
the adverse queen's third. And if Black covers neither with the queen on her
bishop's second nor with the rook — for in two or three moves at most he
would lose the queen, or have checkmate — but for this reason plays the King
to the rook's second, for the best he has — White plays the knight to the
adverse queen's bishop's fourth. And if Black plays the queen to her knight's
third, White gives check with the rook on the King's bishop's second, as I
said above — for thus he will win the queen, and the game with it.

And if when Black played the pawn to his knight's third, to get out of the
adverse knight's attack, he had taken that knight with her queen's rook —
White takes that rook with the queen, and gives check. And if Black plays the
King to the rook's first, White... [the branches close, as ever, with White's
odds converted by force:] ...and so without any remedy he will have won the
game.

[Here ends the fourth odds chapter — and with it the teaching of odds.]

OF GAMES [PROBLEMS]

[The 1597 edition here sets sixteen "partiti" — problems — each with a
diagram whose lettered squares are named in the solutions. The diagrams are
lost in the scan; the solutions are given with their letters, referring to
the printed figures.]

The Second. A most strong problem of four moves. White to Black [to play]:
he will give him mate in four moves, neither more nor less. White plays the
bishop to A, and gives check; and if Black perforce plays the King to B,
White plays the bishop to C. And if Black takes the adverse queen in D with
his own, White plays the King to E, and gives check discovered from the
bishop. And if Black, having nothing else to do, covers with the queen in F
or in G — White takes that queen with the bishop, and gives check, and it
will be [mate]. And if Black does not take the adverse queen with his own,
but plays the queen to H, and gives check — White plays the King to E, and
on the next move will give him mate with the queen in I, or by taking the
queen with the bishop wherever Black plays her. And if Black does not play
the queen to H, but plays the rook to K, or indeed to L — White always plays
the King to E, and gives check discovered from the bishop. And if Black,
finding himself with the rook in L, takes the bishop in C with that rook —
White plays the queen to I, and gives check, and it will be mate. And if
Black will not take the bishop with the rook, to get out of check, but will
cover with the queen in H — White takes that queen with the bishop, and
gives check — and it will be mate, as promised, in four moves.

[There follow, in the 1597 order, problems of five moves (several, including
pawn-mates "ne più ne meno"), of six moves, a most subtle of ten moves, two
most subtle of fifteen moves, one of sixteen moves "of the greatest
judgment," and a most subtle of twenty-three moves — each White to play and
mate in the stated number, neither more nor less, with solutions in the same
lettered manner as above. Two are noted as "often happening in play."]

The Twelfth. A very beautiful and delightful problem of twenty-three moves.
It is an infallible rule for taking all the chessmen with the knight's leap
— on this condition: that the knight is never played without taking, nor
ever set on an empty square. If White plays the knight that stands on 1, and
takes on 2, on 3, on 4, on 5, on 6, on 7, on 8, on 9, on 10, on 11, on 12, on
13, on 14, on 15, on 16, on 17, on 18, on 19, on 20, on 21, on 22, on 23, on
24, on 25, on 26, on 27, on 28, on 29, on 30, on 31, on 32 — doing thus, he
will have taken all the chessmen with the knight's leap. The chessmen must
be set on the board in the order the present figure shows; and if they were
jumbled it matters not, for it suffices that the board have thirty-two
squares full, in the order seen.

[The 1597 table of contents follows, then the register of signatures. The
table lists, last, "Of the games of Draughts" — whose text is not recovered
in the scan used; see the Notes.]

PRIMACY NOTE

None found after research. No English translation of Gianutio's 1597 chess
book has been found. This is a fresh rendering from the Italian; no modern
rendering has been used or borrowed from.

Caveats, stated plainly:
- The 1597 diagrams — of the pieces' moves, the arrayed board, and every
  problem — are lost in the scan used; moves and arrays are given in words,
  and problem solutions keep the edition's own square-letters referring to
  the printed figures.
- The table of contents lists a closing chapter "Of the games of draughts";
  its text is not recoverable from the scan and is not translated here.
- The dedication's addressee is Count Francesco Martinengo di Malpaga; the
  author's dates beyond "of Turin" and the 1597 dedication are not
  established in the materials used, and none are asserted.
- "Black" renders Gianutio's "il Negro" (the black side), as opposed to
  "il Bianco" (the white side); no other sense is intended.
