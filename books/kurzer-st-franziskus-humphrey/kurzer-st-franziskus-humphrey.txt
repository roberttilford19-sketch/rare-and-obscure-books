Editor's Note

Humphrey is a town in Platte County, in east-central Nebraska. It arose in 1879 with a branch line of the Union Pacific and by 1908 counted somewhat over a thousand inhabitants, about half of them Catholic. The Catholic parish of St. Francis of Assisi was formally founded with the dedication of its first church on 10 July 1883 — three years after the town itself. Before that the Humphrey Catholics had belonged to St. Mary's, some four and a half miles southeast, or to St. Bernard's, and the whole region had been served since 1876–77 by Franciscan friars resident at Columbus. The friars who wrote this book's story were men of the Order's Sacred Heart Province: their Kansas motherhouse province of the same name served German-speaking parishes across the plains, and Father Kurzer himself — pastor at Humphrey from the end of 1898 — had come to America in 1875 as a young friar driven out of Westphalia by the Kulturkampf. The parish sits in the Diocese of Omaha; its bishops in these pages are Jacob O'Connor and his successor Richard Scannell, whose portraits stand at the front of the original booklet beside Pope Pius X's.

This jubilee book is a parish looking at itself at twenty-five years: how the town grew out of $5-an-acre prairie, how a debt-free frame church rose in 1883 and the great brick church of 1894 succeeded it, how a school, a friary, thirteen parish societies, daughter missions across four counties, and 203 households were woven into one institution. Its final third is practical and personal: the order of services and devotions, house rules for the sacraments, then the names of every member family in 1908, then the advertisements of the merchants who paid for the printing. Nothing has been cut. To Robert Tilford's Kansas readers the book will feel like a neighbor: the same Volga-German and Westphalian names, the same railroad towns, the same Franciscan province — one state north.



Preface.

On 10 July 1908, 25 years have passed since the founding of St. Francis Parish at Humphrey, Platte County, Nebraska. This writing now has the purpose, on the occasion of the silver jubilee of the parish, to bring to the remembrance of its members what has all happened in the last 25 years for the building up of the parish, and so to fill their hearts with gratitude toward God, the giver of all good, and to spur on [uncertain: spur on / urge on] the growing generation to walk in the footsteps of their fathers and to hold in high esteem, to preserve, and to perfect what those men built up with so many sacrifices. It is also to show to fellow believers of other parishes what can be achieved when only good will is present, and unity among the members of a parish and between parish and pastor. This unity has existed in this parish from the beginning, and only so could there be achieved in so short a time what has been achieved, so that Humphrey is known in the whole state and beyond the borders of the state for its large and beautiful church and splendid parish school. May this harmony always be preserved! This is the wish of the author of these lines.

Fr. Florentius Kurzer, O.F.M., Pastor.

First Chapter.

The Town of Humphrey.

The year 1879 may be taken as the year of the founding of the town of Humphrey. In that year, namely, the branch line of the Union Pacific was built from Columbus to Norfolk, with the station of Humphrey. After the erection of the railroad station, dwelling houses and business houses were soon erected. The first Catholic who settled in the newly arisen Humphrey and opened a general store and also dealt in lumber, coal, and grain was Mr. Wilhelm Eimers [uncertain: Eimers/Einers]. In the same year 1879 Mr. Thomas Ottis bought from Wilhelm Tieskötter [uncertain: Tieskötter/Tiestötter] 10 acres of land, which he laid out in building lots and sold. In the following year Mr. Thomas Ottis also erected a new dwelling and business house. Humphrey grew rapidly. The town received a new impetus when in the year 1887 a new railroad was built from east to west, the Elkhorn Railroad, now a branch line of the C. & N. W. Railroad. Thus Humphrey now has railroad connections in all four directions of the compass. Situated in one of the most fertile parts of the state, it could not fail that the town developed more and more and flourished. At present the town has somewhat over 1,000 inhabitants. About half of them are Catholic. The Protestants are represented in three sects with three small churches, namely the German Baptists, the Methodists, and the Lutherans. That much business is done in the little town can be seen from the fact that for many years two flourishing banks have been located here. Four grain elevators, so-called “elevators,” buy the produce from the farmers, mainly corn, wheat, and oats. In the town there are three physicians, two dentists, one veterinarian, two pharmacies, three blacksmiths, one baker, two saddlers, one hotel, two restaurants, five taverns (saloons), three hardware stores, two lumber dealers (lumber yards), two watchmakers, one cigar maker, two shoemakers, one photographer, one furniture dealer and undertaker, one steam-powered grist mill, one brickyard (brick-yard), one nursery for ornamental and fruit trees (Humphrey Nursery), one newspaper in the English language, the “Humphrey Democrat,” one clothing store, three general stores (stores). The little town is also provided with the most modern improvements. In the year 1901 electric lighting with steam power was installed. Most dwelling and business houses are provided with telephones (telephone). In the year 1900 a municipal waterworks was installed. The side-

walks (side-walks) are for the most part made of cement (concrete).

All of this, built and established in so few years, shows us that Humphrey must be situated in a very fertile region, especially when one considers that the first settlers were poor, indeed for the most part began here with nothing. Twenty-five years ago the farmers received the land very cheaply at $5.00 an acre. At present an acre near the town costs $100 to $125.00 [uncertain: $100]. Many a farmer who 25 years ago began here with nothing now possesses property worth 30 to 50 thousand dollars and more. Working the land here is also connected with less labor than in other regions, for there have been no forests here, and so the first settlers did not have to clear away trees and tree stumps [uncertain: clear away / dig out]; they needed only to set the plow into the virgin prairie soil, and already in the first year they could obtain an abundant harvest. Working the soil is also not hindered by stones, for far and wide there is not a stone to be found here, not even as large as a walnut. What took away the courage of many farmers in the first years and moved them to move away again were the plagues of locusts, which in two years destroyed everything, and likewise hail, which repeatedly visited them. However, in the last 24 years they have been spared these plagues, and with the exception of the year 1894 they have always had a good harvest.



Second Chapter.

Founding and History of St. Francis Parish.

St. Francis Parish in Humphrey dates its existence as an independent parish from the day of the dedication and opening of the first church, Tuesday, 10 July 1883. Of the 80 families who belonged to this new parish, up to that time about 60 had belonged to St. Mary's Parish and 20 to St. Bernard's Parish. St. Mary's Church, situated 4½ miles southeast of Humphrey, had been built about the middle of the seventies, and with it the parish had been founded. It had at first been served by secular priests from Omaha and West Point. In March of the year 1877 the Franciscans, who in the preceding year had settled in Columbus, at the request of the Most Reverend [uncertain: abbreviation expanded] Bishop Jacob O'Connor had taken over the pastoral care of St. Mary's Parish. St. Bernard had been founded in the year 1878 by the Franciscans from Columbus; Fr. Cyprian Bonscheid [uncertain: Bonscheid], at present Provincial of the Sacred Heart Prov-

ince, was the first pastor of St. Bernard. Splendid reports about the Catholic settlement of St. Bernard and about Platte County in general had been widely spread, whereby many Catholics were moved to settle near St. Bernard and in the region between St. Bernard and St. Marys, that is, in the region of Humphrey. As the number of Catholics became ever larger, and for many the way to St. Marys or St. Bernard was too burdensome, the general wish became loud that a parish of their own should be founded in Humphrey, especially as it was also considered very advantageous for the advancement of the place. This wish was accordingly laid, in the spring of the year 1882, before the Reverend Fr. Ubaldus Weberinke [uncertain: Weberinke], O.F.M., who as Visitor General was visiting the friaries at Columbus and St. Bernard. He charged the pastor of St. Marys at that time, the Reverend Fr. Theodor Arenz, O.F.M., to send an exact report on the matter to the Reverend Father Provincial for the Provincial Chapter to be held in July. At this Chapter the founding of the parish was approved, and permission for it was granted by the Most Reverend Bishop of Omaha. Mr. Thomas Ottis donated 8 very beautifully situated acres of land for church purposes on the southwest corner of the 10 acres bought from W. Tieskötter [uncertain: Tieskötter/Tiestötter], and Mr. W. Tieskötter [uncertain: Tieskötter/Tiestötter] donated 3 acres for a cemetery. The Reverend Father Provincial Vinzenz Halbax [uncertain: Halbax] thereupon, on 9 September 1882, gave in writing his approval for the building of a church, under the condition that it be built without debts. As a building committee the following gentlemen were chosen: Thomas Ottis, Heinrich Lohaus [uncertain: Lohaus/Lohans], Nikolaus Thille, and Adolph Schmied. The plans prepared by Brother Adrian Wewer, O.F.M., for a frame church 64 feet long, 32 feet wide, and 16 feet high, with an annex 20 by 20 feet for sanctuary and sacristy, with a room over the sacristy for the priest and with a tower 64 feet high, were willingly accepted. After agreement had been reached on the site for the church to be built, the solemn laying of the cornerstone took place on 10 December through the Reverend Fr. Seraphin Lampe, O.F.M., superior of the friary in Columbus. Mr. Franz Brockhaus [uncertain: Brockhaus/Brokhans] directed the building. A large part of the work was done without pay by the members of the parish. Through the great sacrificial willingness of the people and in constant unity and harmony, the church was completed by the beginning of July 1883. On 10 July 1883 the solemn dedication of the church took place through the Reverend Fr. Seraphin, on behalf of the Most Reverend Bishop, who had promised to perform the dedication himself but was prevented from doing so. Taking part in the celebration were the Reverend Fathers Theodor Arenz, pastor of the parish, Fr. Rudolph Horstmann of St. Bernard, and Fr.

Anastasius Czech of Columbus. The church and parish were placed under the protection of St. Francis of Assisi.

Up to that point, receipts for the building and for items to be procured amounted to $3,187.75; expenditures, $3,164.67; cash on hand, $23.08. No debts had thus been incurred.

At a meeting on 10 October of the same year it was decided to build a parish school. By the end of November $2,000.00 had already been subscribed. Excavation began on 17 December. Frank Brockhaus received the contract for the carpentry work. On Sunday, 2 November 1884, the school with the sisters’ residence was dedicated and opened.

On 30 June 1884 the holy sacrament of Confirmation was administered in Humphrey for the first time by the Most Reverend Bishop J. O’Connor. On the afternoon of the same day the bishop blessed a bell for the church. He gladly acknowledged the progress that had been made in religious and economic respects in Humphrey and in this part of Platte County in general. As he expressed it, he entertained high expectations for the future.

From 23 to 30 November 1884 a holy mission was given by the Reverend Fathers Augustin McGorry and Eumphorian Horstmann. Its fruits were truly blessed.

Since the parish was now provided with a church and a parish school and had grown to 120 families, many members believed the time had come for the parish to receive a resident priest. Until then Humphrey had been served from Columbus. In May 1885 Mr. Ottis therefore wrote to the Reverend Father Provincial in this matter. He received in reply that from the beginning it had indeed been intended that a friary should be erected here. The Provincial, however, could make no determination by himself alone. He would lay the matter before the Chapter that was to take place in July. In the meantime, subscriptions should be taken up for the building of a friary. $2,000.00 was subscribed.

At the Chapter held in St. Louis on 10 July 1885, the erection of a residence in Humphrey was approved. Brother Damian, O.F.M., a carpenter brother, was charged by the Reverend Father Provincial Mauritius Klostermann with directing the building. On 21 October excavation for the foundations was begun. Since the church had meanwhile become too small, it was decided at a parish meeting on Sunday, 27 September 1885, to enlarge the church, namely by moving the sanctuary outward and erecting an intermediate section 30 by 18 feet, so that the church would take the form of a cross. The cost was estimated at $1,000.00. In order to raise the necessary means, it was decided to assess the parishioners proportionally. This addition was completed in November of the same year. Brother Damian also directed this work.

At the same time as the addition to the church, a one-story addition was erected on the Gospel side, which formed the connection between the church and the residence and was to serve as an oratory for the religious and, in winter, also as a winter chapel.

On 13 October 1887 the bishop again administered the sacrament of Confirmation and on the afternoon of the same day blessed a new, larger bell. The means for procuring the bell had been raised through donations by parishioners. The bishop again expressed himself very pleased with the progress made and very hopeful for the future.

On Sunday, 26 February 1888, after High Mass, a meeting of the men of the parish was held to deliberate concerning the enlargement of the school building, because the number of schoolchildren had increased so much that the school building had become too small. At this meeting it was decided to extend the school building on the west side to double its size, so that the addition would have the same size and the same appearance as the school building first erected and the whole would form one building. Inside, it was to be arranged so that on the second floor a meeting room would be reserved, which could be combined with the adjoining schoolroom to form a hall. Construction was begun in the spring and by the beginning of August was for the most part completed. At the Chapter held in St. Louis on 25 July 1888, Fr. Theodor Arenz was elected Guardian of the friary in Cleveland, Ohio, and appointed pastor of St. Joseph’s parish there. In his place Fr. Jacobus Nolte, O.F.M., was appointed superior of the residence and pastor of St. Francis parish in Humphrey.

From 22 to 29 November 1891 a holy mission was held for the second time in St. Francis Church by Fr. Victor Kertker [uncertain: Kertker], O.F.M., and Fr. Servatius Rasche, O.F.M. In the year 1891 the parish already numbered 180 families, and since the church had again become too small, the resolve was taken to build a large, beautiful new church that would meet the needs for many years. In October 1891 Fr. Jacobus gathered subscriptions for this new building and received in that same year $1,645.00 for this purpose. In the year 1892 he received $1,975.00. He was, however, unable to carry out the planned building himself, for on account of an illness contracted on a sick call in winter he had to resign his office. He was transferred to San Francisco, California, and in his place there came on 9 March 1892 Fr. Rudolph Horstmann, O.F.M., from Jordan, Minne-

sota. In the following year the new pastor at once set about carrying out the planned building. On 1 January 1893 there was $7,341.00 on hand. The plans furnished by Brother Adrian were accepted, and Brother Aldephons Lethert [uncertain: Lethert], O.F.M., was charged by the Reverend Father Provincial with directing the building. The new church was to be erected on the same site where the first had stood. The latter was therefore moved several hundred feet farther west, so that divine service could continue to be held in it until the new church was ready. On Wednesday, 19 June 1893, the solemn laying of the cornerstone for the new church took place. The building progressed vigorously. As soon as it was under roof, divine service was held in the lower story (basement) and the old church was torn down.

The year 1894 will remain unforgettable for Nebraska as the dry year in which the farmers had an almost complete failure of the crop. Instead of being able, as usual, to bring produce to town to sell, they had to come to town to buy grain brought in from other regions at enormously high prices in order to keep some livestock alive. That was certainly discouraging for priest and parish, who had such a great building in hand. Yet they did not let themselves be discouraged; they trusted in God, hoped for better times, and calmly continued to complete the building that had been begun.

On Thursday, 20 September 1894, the church was solemnly dedicated by the Most Reverend Bishop Richard Scannell of Omaha, with great participation by the people. The High Mass on this occasion was celebrated by the Reverend Fr. Mauritius Pauckholt [uncertain: Pauckholt/Bankholt], O.F.M., superior at Columbus, assisted by Fathers Florentius Kurzer of St. Bernard and Marcellinus Kolfmener [uncertain: Kolfmener]. The German sermon was preached by the Reverend Father Provincial Michael Richardt, O.F.M.; the English sermon by the Reverend Wunibald Wolf, pastor and dean of Grand Island, Nebraska.

The building cost, without interior furnishings, $32,116.00. Much work, especially the hauling, was performed without pay by the members of the parish. When the church was completed, the parish had debts of $16,045.00. The church is built in the Romanesque style; it is 172 feet long, 64 feet wide, and the tower is 172 feet high. For the lower story (basement) finely dressed stone was used, and for the upper structure red brick from Omaha, so-called “pressed bricks.” The church has seating for 800 persons. Before the scaffolding was removed from the church, Mr. Klier of St. Louis decorated it very tastefully for the price of $1,000.

The entire interior furnishing and equipment of the church, with the exception of the pews, is a gift of individual benefactors from the parish. The high altar was donated by Mr. Thomas

Ottis and costs $1,200.00. The side altars were donated by the St. Joseph’s Men’s Society and together cost $600.00. The Communion rail is a gift of the Young Men’s Society and costs $225.00. The beautiful Stations of the Cross are a gift of Mr. Gerhard Brockhaus and cost $670.00. The window in the sanctuary with the image of St. Francis is a gift of the members of the Third Order. The window with the image of St. Anthony was donated by Anton Pelle, Anton Eissenmenner [uncertain: Eissenmenner/Eisenmenger], Anton Osterhoff, Anton Weibel, Anton Simon, and Anton Langmann. The window with the image of the Sacred Heart of Jesus is a gift of Daniel O’Sullivan; each of these three windows in the sanctuary costs $150.00; the others cost $55.00 each and were obtained from the firm of Misch, Chicago. A large third bell was procured by the St. Joseph’s Men’s Society for $190.00; it weighs 1,300 pounds. A beautiful green carpet for the sanctuary was procured by the Christian Mothers’ Society for $275.00. The organ, at $1,100.00, was paid for through theatrical performances and collections taken up by the school sisters. Mr. Nikolaus Thille donated a second monstrance for $150.00 and a beautiful chalice for $72.00. The statue of the Sorrowful Mother is a gift of the since-deceased Mrs. Maria Pfeifer. On Sunday, 23 September 1894, the new, splendid Stations of the Cross were blessed by the Reverend Father Provincial Michael Richardt, O.F.M.

At its completion the church was the most beautiful and the largest in the diocese, indeed in the whole State of Nebraska, and it is so still today, as the bishop has repeatedly declared from the pulpit.

At the so-called Half-Chapter held in St. Louis in July 1896, Fr. Rudolph Horstmann, O.F.M., was transferred to Minnesota, and his successor as superior and pastor of Humphrey was Fr. Angelus Bill, O.F.M., who came here from Petoskey, Michigan. Until the year 1897, even on Sundays in winter divine service had to be held in the so-called basement (lower story) of the church, since no heating apparatus had yet been installed. In the autumn of 1897 Fr. Angelus therefore collected the necessary money to install a good steam-heating system. At the beginning of December it was finished and cost $1,630.73 [uncertain: $1,630.73/$1,030.73].

In the same year he also had an addition made to the school in order to gain room to take children for boarding. This addition cost $937.56 [uncertain: $937.56]. Fr. Angelus also had the side statues for the side altars — St. Joachim and Anna, Joseph Calasantius [uncertain: Calasantius], and Henry — brought from Mr. Naletta [uncertain: Naletta], St. Louis, Missouri. The church received another adornment in that Mr. H. Echolt [uncertain: Echolt] donated a beautiful lamp for the sanctuary light, which cost $85.00.

At the Half-Chapter held in St. Louis on 28 December 1898, Fr. Angelus was transferred to Joliet, Illinois, and in his place came Fr. Florentius Kurzer, O.F.M., until then superior and pastor of St. Bernard, Nebraska. At the wish of the inhabitants of the town, his predecessor, Fr. Angelus, had already ordered a tower clock from A. C. Pollhaus [uncertain: Pollhaus], St. Louis. By 15 April 1899 it was finished and installed at the price of $725.00. It strikes the quarter-hours on the two small bells and the full hours on the largest bell.

In the spring of the same year Fr. Florentius laid out the processional way for the feast of Corpus Christi on the land belonging to the church and had trees planted on both sides. In November of this year a small chapel was erected in the cemetery, which cost $150.00 and in which the Crucifixion group was set up that had stood on the high altar of the old church. Gerhard Brockhaus, who had already made so many gifts to the church, gave another $213.00 for a beautiful Nativity scene, which was obtained from the firm of Daprato, Chicago.

When in the year 1901 an installation for electric light was established in the town, the pastor of the parish also had this light installed for the friary and the church.

In this same year the sidewalks (side-walks) and steps before the church were laid in cement and stone (concrete) for $360.00. In November of this year 1901 a new, magnificent pulpit and two confessionals, made by E. Hackner, La Crosse, Wisconsin, were installed. Brother Leonard, O.F.M., furnished the plans for both. The pulpit costs $275.00; the confessionals $100.00 each. From 1 to 10 November of this year 1901 a holy mission was again held by the Franciscan Fathers Daniel Finkenhöfer and Titus Hugger [uncertain: Hugger]. It was very well attended, and 740 persons received the holy sacraments.

In order to pay off the debts, which at his arrival in Humphrey still amounted to $13,880.00, Fr. Florentius every year in the autumn visited all the families of the parish and gathered subscriptions, so that at the reading of the church accounts for the year 1903 he could make the joyful announcement to the parish that all debts had been paid off and more than $700.00 was on hand as the result of a bazaar that was held on Thanksgiving Day 1903 in the school.

In November 1904 storm windows were installed on the church to keep out the cold and snow in winter and the rain in summer, which in strong wind always came through the windows. They were obtained from the Emil Frei “Art Glass Co.” of [St. L]ouis and cost $361.00.

On 18 December 1904 two new, beautiful Munich statues, the Sacred Heart of Jesus and St. Anthony — the gift of a benefactor who does not wish his name to be made known — were blessed by the pastor of the parish. Together they cost $209.50. With them the church received a new adornment. The old statues, the Sacred Heart of Jesus and St. Anthony, were set up in the lower story of the church, where on weekdays in winter divine service is held because of the easier heating.

Since the number of schoolchildren kept increasing and the schoolrooms were therefore too cramped, it was decided in the year 1904 to erect a new, large school with 8 schoolrooms, with a ground floor and a hall. After the approval of the bishop and of the Reverend Father Provincial had been obtained and Brother Adrian, with the help of Brother Anselm of St. Louis, had completed the plans, excavation for the foundation and ground floor was begun on 5 October 1904. In the spring of 1905 this work was completed by the farmers of the parish, and on 28 March the masonry work was begun. Mr. Joseph and Bruno Zürlein [uncertain: Zürlein], with their sons, undertook this work for $3,316.75. Mr. G. Wirdemann [uncertain: Wirdemann], architect of Columbus, Nebraska, directed the building until everything was well under way. Before winter the roof was completed, so that work could proceed inside the building the whole winter from 1905 to 1906, until at last on 17 May 1906 the solemn dedication of the splendid building took place.

Since the bishop was prevented by Confirmation journeys from performing the dedication, the Reverend Father Provincial Hugolinus Storck, O.F.M., did so. He also celebrated the solemn High Mass and preached the German sermon, while the Reverend Fr. Pacificus Kohnen, O.F.M., of Omaha preached the English sermon.

The building costs, including the steam-heating plant, $35,855.00. The ground floor is built of finely dressed stone from Kansas; the upper structure of red, very hard bricks (“pressed bricks”) from Coffeyville, Kansas. The building has three stories and is 110 feet long, 70 feet wide, with an annex on the east side of 24 by 42 feet. On the lower floor there is a large hall in which the children can stay in bad weather and which is also used as a dining hall at festivities. Opposite this hall is a spacious kitchen. On this floor there is also a large society hall for the Catholic Foresters’ Society and the steam-heating plant. The second floor contains seven beautiful schoolrooms provided with ventilation. The third floor forms one magnificent, large hall with a well-appointed stage furnished with beautiful scenery. This hall offers room for more than 800 persons. On

the east side of the hall there is another spacious room, which for the present is used as a dressing room at theatrical performances but later, if necessary, can also be used as a schoolroom. The hall, as well as the rooms on the lowest floor, are provided with electric light. With the erection of this building the parish is provided for many years with the necessary rooms for holding divine service in the church, for instruction in the school, and for entertainment in the hall.

Servatius Rasche, who since 5 August 1883 had served St. Mary’s from Columbus and from now on was to reside in Humphrey. Fourth Chapter.

The St. Francis School.

The parish school is the foundation of a parish. If the parish is to flourish and endure, Catholic education in the parish school must be provided for. Parishes without a parish—

school cannot long endure. The young people who are educated in the public schools grow up without thorough instruction in religion, and the consequence of this is that they become indifferent toward religion and the Church and fall away from the faith. Therefore the German parishes in this country have from the beginning taken care to lay a solid foundation for their parishes, that is, to found a parish school. The first members of the St. Francis parish at Humphrey did this also. As already mentioned above, in the same year in which the first church was erected, in 1883, it was already decided to build a parish school. On 2 November 1884 the school was solemnly dedicated and opened with 100 children. The Congregation of the Poor Franciscan Sisters, who have their motherhouse in Germany at Olpe, Province of Westphalia, and in America at Lafayette, Indiana, took over this school. The provincial superior had already promised in August 1884 to send sisters to Humphrey. On 21 October the first two sisters arrived, Sister Andrea and Conrada, whom a third soon followed. Sister Andrea was the first superior. When in 1888 the school had to be enlarged by an addition, the school was opened in September of that year with three classes, to which a fourth class was added in 1891. In 1897 an addition was again made on the south side of the schoolhouse, and thus room was gained to take in as boarders children who live too far from the school. Since the parish kept growing and there was no longer sufficient room for the increasing number of children, the pastor saw himself compelled in 1901 to request a fifth teaching sister. In order to gain room for this fifth class, the room previously used as a theater hall was fitted out as a classroom. For a long time the wish had been expressed by the members of the parish to engage a lay teacher for the older boys. This wish was complied with, in that from 1 September 1903 Mr. Anton Wolf was engaged as teacher and organist with a salary of $500. Mr. A. Wolf, however, resigned this position after only two years. His place in the school was again taken by a sister. When the new school was finished in 1906, the sixth school class was opened in September of that year. In the last school year, from September 1907 to the end of June 1908, 286 children attended the school, who were taught in six classes up to the ninth grade by the Sisters Stanislaus, Michelina [uncertain], Eleutheria [uncertain], Antona, Electa, and Leocadia. All the subjects are taught in the school that are taught in the public school; in addition there is instruction in religion and instruction in the German language. The girls also receive—

[Photograph: St. Francis Church and Friary.]

instruction in women's handwork every Friday afternoon. The salary of the sisters amounts to $200 a year for each teaching sister, besides free lodging, light, and heat. This salary is raised for the most part by the tuition, which amounts to 50 cents a month for each child. Children who have already received their first Holy Communion are free.

The church choir also was directed by the sisters from the beginning with only a short interruption. The organists up to now have been the following sisters: Sr. Andrea, Romana [uncertain], Constantia, Antonia, and at present Cäciliana.

The superiors of the sisters were the following: Sr. Andrea, Angolina [uncertain], Ignatia, Modesta, Antonia, and at present Sr. Stanislaus.

The rules for parents and children with regard to the school are the following:

1. The parents or their representatives are strictly obliged to send their children or foster children to the parish school, because as a rule only in this way can the children be formed into good Catholics. The public schools, because all religion is excluded from them, make [children] very indifferent in the faith; indeed, they are often dangerous to it.

2. For this reason the children should not first attend the public school for several years and only later, perhaps in the last year, attend the parish school in order to prepare quickly for First Holy Communion, but should begin attending the parish school right away, that is, when the children are 6 years old, either in the spring or in the fall.

3. Even after First Holy Communion one should send the children to school for at least one more year, because that is the best time for learning. The little money that they earn does not outweigh the great harm that they suffer for their whole lives by leaving school too early.

4. The parents should send their children right at the beginning of the school year and not only a few weeks later, because otherwise they cannot keep step with the other children. For the same reason the parents should not keep their children out of school without an urgent reason; they should also, in case a child must stay at home, communicate the reason for the absence to the teachers.

5. Parents should see to it that their children attend the school Mass every morning, whenever possible.

6. The parents should see to it that their children learn their schoolwork at home. They should look at the monthly report on the report card, reward the diligent and the well-behaved, and punish the negligent and the lazy.

7. They should not tolerate their children complaining about and reviling teachers and priests; they should not believe everything they say at once, but in the event of any difficulties should inquire, in all modesty, of the priest and the teacher; they should instill in them reverence, obedience, and love toward their superiors.

8. There is no school instruction: 1) On all Saturdays, Sundays, and holy days of obligation. 2) From the eve of the holy feast of Christmas until 2 January. 3) From Wednesday of Holy Week until Easter Monday, inclusive. 4) On Whit Monday, on the feast of Corpus Christi, on the feast of St. Francis, on the name day of the pastor, and on Thanksgiving Day [uncertain]. 5) From the end of June until the end of August.

9. The children go to holy confession four times a year, usually in the Ember weeks.

10. Only those children are admitted to First Holy Communion who in the year in question have reached or will reach the age of 12 years and who, in the judgment of the pastor, have been sufficiently instructed in religion.



Fifth Chapter.

The Societies.

The present time is a time of societies, in the ecclesiastical as well as in the secular sphere. An individual cannot accomplish much, but much can be achieved when several unite to attain a definite purpose. The Catholic Church itself is indeed the greatest society that exists, spread over the whole earth, embracing all nations. Every parish again forms a kind of society, and so the individual parishes again have the most varied societies in order to attain various purposes. The following societies exist in our parish:

1. The St. Joseph's Men's Society.

This society has existed since 23 August 1885. On this day the statutes of the society were adopted and the first election of officers took place. The following were elected: Gerhard H. Brockhaus, President. Wilhelm Weber, Vice-President.

Nikolaus Fleischer, Secretary. Jakob Fischer, Treasurer.

The solemn erection of the society, however, did not take place until 4 October of the same year. Thirty members were received. On 8 May 1887, on the feast of the Patronage of St. Joseph, the society banner, which had been purchased for $110.00, was solemnly blessed.

The chief purpose of the society, as stated in the statutes, is a closer union of Catholic men among themselves and with their ecclesiastical superiors in the principles of their faith and in the fulfillment of their religious and civic duties. Secondary purposes are: 1) Support of the parish school by contributions from the society treasury. 2) Beautification and adornment of the church. 3) Works of love and mercy toward needy, sick, and deceased members.

A meeting is held every month, and four times a year the society has corporate Communion.

In 1899 the society affiliated with the German Roman Catholic Central Verein, which is spread over the whole United States and counts over 100,000 members.

Since its existence the St. Joseph's Men's Society has contributed yearly about $100.00 toward the support of the parish school and has thus splendidly fulfilled the purpose for which it was founded. This year's officers are: Richard Olmer, President; Frank Brockhaus, Vice-President; Joseph Braun, Secretary; Conrad Fuchs, Treasurer.

2. The Society of Christian Mothers.

This society arose from the former St. Anna Women's Society. The St. Anna Women's Society was founded on 26 November 1882, at the time when preparations for the building of the St. Francis Church were begun. The women of Humphrey and the surrounding area, who had formerly belonged to the St. Anna Women's Society existing in the St. Mary's parish, from then on, with the approval of the then pastor of the parish, the Reverend P. Theodor, formed a society of their own, retaining the same name and the same statutes. The first officers elected were: Maria Olmer, President; Anna Wickham [uncertain], Vice-President; Maria Pfeifer, Secretary; Elisabeth Eimers [uncertain], Treasurer.

[Photograph: Interior of St. Francis Church.]

At its founding the society had 30 members. After several preparatory meetings, on 20 September 1885 the members of the Frauen-Verein (Women's Society) joined the Christlicher Mütter-Verein (Christian Mothers' Society). The canonical erection of the society by the bishop took place on 22 September 1885. On 1 November 1886 the aggregation to the Erzbruderschaft (Archconfraternity) existing in St. Augustine's Church in Pittsburg took place. As the principal patronal feast the Feast of the Seven Sorrows of Mary was chosen, which is celebrated on the third Sunday in September with general Communion and the admission of new members. Besides this, the society has general Communion six more times in the year and a meeting every month with instruction on the duties of their state in life. If a member dies, the society members take part with their insignia in the funeral. For every deceased member a Requiem is ordered by the society. This society is the oldest in the parish and also the largest. 130 women belong to the society. Through the monthly dues of 10 cents the society has already accomplished much for the procurement of vestments and other things for the church. In the year 1888 a beautiful society banner was also purchased. At present the society has the following officers: Dorothea Zürlein, President; Margaretha Enning, Vice-President; Helena Steffes, Secretary; Anna Keller, Treasurer.

Through this society a truly Christian spirit is to be preserved and promoted in the families and in the parish. For a truly Christian mother, as the society seeks to form her through prayer, frequent reception of the sacraments, and instructions on the duties of her state, will also bring a truly Christian spirit into the families. May this society continue to exist and flourish, for the blessing of the parish and of every individual family, and bear abundant fruit!

3. The St. Antonius-Jünglings-Sodalität (St. Anthony Young Men's Sodality).

On 13 September 1885 the St. Antonius-Jünglings-Verein (St. Anthony Young Men's Society) was founded by the adoption of the society rules and the election of a board. Elected were: Conrad Hettich, President; Joseph Baumann, Vice-President; Hyacinth Eiche, Secretary, and Joseph Brockhaus, Treasurer. After several years of existence the society had dissolved, but it was newly organized again in a meeting that had been called on 31 January 1897 by the pastor at the time, Fr. Angelus Bill. The society also received a new constitution.

The society has repeatedly given dramatic performances for the benefit of the church, at which a considerable sum was realized each time. The society possesses a beautiful banner and sashes,

which are worn at processions and general Communions. The society also possesses a library of German and English entertainment books, which are at the free disposal of the members of the society. Six times in the year the society has general Communion, and every month a meeting, at which a short lecture is given and the monthly dues of 10 cents are paid.

On 31 May 1903 the Young Men's Society was raised to the dignity of a Marianische Sodalität (Marian Sodality). At the general Communion that followed, the members were solemnly received into the sodality. The sodality at present has 45 members and the following officers: Heinrich Brockhaus, President; Wilhelm Smith, Vice-President; Thomas Werner, Secretary; Bernard Ward, Treasurer.

4. The St. Clara Jungfrauen-Sodalität (St. Clare Young Women's Sodality).

The St. Clara-Jungfrauen-Verein (St. Clare Young Women's Society) was founded on 12 September 1886. The society consists of young women of the parish who enjoy a good reputation. The purpose of the society is: the promotion of an honorable and virtuous conduct among the young women and mutual encouragement to what is good through good example. To attain this purpose the members of the society are to venerate the Mother of God diligently, place themselves under her maternal protection, and strive to imitate the virtuous example of St. Clara, their patroness. They have general Communion every month and likewise a meeting every month, at which they are exhorted by a short instruction to fulfill the duties of their state, whereupon they pay their dues, which amount to only 5 cents for the month. This society also, on 31 May 1903, together with the Young Men's Society, was raised to a Marian sodality and so becomes a sharer in the many graces and indulgences granted to the members of a canonically erected sodality. This sodality now counts 85 members. The officers are: Maria Fuchs, President; Emma Schäfer, Vice-President; Karolina Foltz, Secretary; Dorothea Zürlein, Treasurer.

5. The Verein der Katholischen Förster, Court No. 486 (Society of Catholic Foresters, Court No. 486).

This society was introduced into the parish on 19 March 1895. The purpose of the society is mutual assistance in case of sickness and support of the family in case of death. For with this society life insurance is combined, up to the amount of $1000.00 to $3000.00.

This society was introduced into the parish chiefly to keep Catholics from joining forbidden secret societies, with which the whole country is flooded. Whoever wishes to join a society for the sake of life insurance thus has the opportunity to join this Catholic society and is not exposed to the danger of suffering shipwreck in his faith. The society counts 106 members in this parish. The following are the officers of the society:

Thomas Werner, Chief Forester. Johann Weber, Deputy Forester. Johann Hugg, Financial Secretary. Gerald Graham, Recording Secretary. Joseph Lachnit, Treasurer. Jakob Fischer and August Widhalm, Marshals.

6. The Kindheit Jesu-Verein (Holy Childhood Society).

As early as the year 1885 this society was introduced in the St. Franziskus-Schule (St. Francis School). It is intended chiefly for the schoolchildren, but very small children can also be admitted. Its purpose is to unite the Catholic children for the rescue of the poor heathen children.

Obligations: 1. One pays 1 cent every month. 2. One prays daily one “Hail Mary” with the addition: Holy Virgin Mary and St. Joseph, pray for us and for the poor heathen children.

The members become sharers in many spiritual graces and indulgences. Every year in the Christmas season, on a Sunday before Vespers, the society feast is celebrated. The children hold a procession in the church, in which a beautiful statue of the Christ Child is carried while suitable hymns are sung. Then a short address is given to the children, the blessing is bestowed on them, and a collection is taken up. Almost all the children of the local school belong to the society, and every year a goodly sum could be sent to the central administration of the society.

7. The 3te Orden (Third Order).

The first reception into the Third Order took place on 8 December 1883. Twelve members were received at that time. Since then the number has increased, if slowly, yet steadily, and at present the Third Order has about 80 members in this parish. Frank Brockhaus has for many years been the director of the Order.

[Photograph: St. Francis School, erected in 1905.]

This Order was founded by the holy Father Francis for the faithful of both sexes who live in the world. It is to help them to a pious, penitent life. Pope Leo the Thirteenth considerably mitigated the original Rule, to make it possible for everyone to join.

The conditions for admission are: 1) One must be at least 11 [uncertain: 11] years old. 2) A married woman should have the permission of her husband, unless her confessor deems it better otherwise. 3) Everyone should, if desired, present a written recommendation from his pastor or confessor. 4) One must be received by an authorized priest.

Special obligations: 1) One wears a scapular and cord of the Order. 2) One prays daily: 12 Our Fathers, Hail Mary, and Glory be to the Father, etc. 3) One observes the two fast days: 3 October and 7 December. 4) One attends the meetings of the Order if possible.

Advantages: 1) One becomes a member of an Order approved by the Church. 2) One has a share in all the good works done in the three Orders of St. Francis. 3) One can gain many plenary and partial indulgences, general absolutions, and the papal blessing. 4) In the Third Order one also has a powerful safeguard against human inconstancy, an incentive to the practice of many good works and virtues. The Holy Father Leo XIII writes: “Nothing can be more gratifying to our heart than when the Third Order finds the widest diffusion; it offers its members the remedies for the great evils of our time and holds out glorious goods both for the common welfare and for that of each individual.”

8. The Herz Maria-Bruderschaft für die Bekehrung der Sünder (Confraternity of the Heart of Mary for the Conversion of Sinners).

To the preceding societies there is yet joined the Herz Maria-Bruderschaft für die Bekehrung der Sünder (Confraternity of the Heart of Mary for the Conversion of Sinners), which was already canonically erected in this parish on 14 November 1884. It was the wish and the direction of the pastor at the time, the

Reverend Fr. Theodor, that all the members of the societies already existing should also belong to this confraternity. On every first Sunday of the month the confraternity devotion is held instead of Vespers.

9. The St. Franziskus Kirchen-Chor (St. Francis Church Choir).

The church choir also deserves special mention. Its members have to make great sacrifices, since some of them must travel a long way every week in order to be present at the necessary practices. But they shrink from no trouble and effort in order by their beautiful singing to contribute their part to the glorification of God and the beautifying of divine worship.

At present the following members belong to the choir: 1. Tenor: Johann Fuchs, Thomas Werner, Johann Englisch, Aloys Schmitt, Adolph Gröger, Franz Pfeifer. 2. Bass: Franz Schmied, Joseph Brockhaus, Johann Weidner, Theodor Krüger, Franz Gröger. 3. Soprano: Katharina Eckholt, Dorothea Düsman, Eleonora Schüth, Agnes Zürlein, Anna Claßen, Margaretha Fischer, Franziska Olmer, Dorthea Zürlein. 4. Alto: Anna Eckholt, Elisabeth Lubischer, Theresia Schneider, Valeria Schmied.

Of these, Mr. Franz Schmied and Miss Katharina Eckholt deserve special mention and recognition, since they have belonged to the choir from the beginning and have always been zealous members of it.



Sixth Chapter.



The Parishes Served from Humphrey.

1. St. Marys.

This parish is the oldest German parish in Platte County. As early as the beginning of the seventies of the last century, German priests from West Point and from Omaha occasionally came into this region to visit the scattered German Catholics, to hold divine service for them in individual private houses, and to administer the sacraments to them. The first German priest who read the first holy Mass in this region was the Reverend Friedrich Uhing of West Point. That was in March of the year 1872 in the house (built of sod, a so-called “Sodhouse”) of the Braun family. In 1875 the Reverend Johann Bernard (the later Franciscan Father Alexius) visited the abandoned Catholics of this region and encouraged the people to build a church. In

the autumn of the same year a little wooden church was completed. The parish, however, did not yet receive regular divine service, but was visited only occasionally by the German priests Fr. Bernard, Grünebaum, and Schulled [uncertain: Schulled]. When in the year 1876 the Franciscans were called to Columbus by the Apostolic Vicar of Nebraska, the Reverend J. O'Connor, in the following year 1877 the pastoral care of the young St. Marys parish also fell to them. From then on St. Marys was served regularly from Columbus (20 English miles away), namely by the Fathers Ambrosius Janssen and Sebastian Zebulla [uncertain: Zebulla], until in August 1877 Fr. Anselm Pütz became the first resident pastor of the parish. In the year 1878 he enlarged the church to double its size. In October 1879 Fr. Meinolphus Schmitz received the pastoral care of the parish. In 1881 he erected a parish school, toward which the Leopoldine Foundation in Vienna also contributed $130.00. As teachers Fr. Meinolphus called the Franciscan Sisters of Lafayette, who have conducted the school to this day. In the year 1882 Fr. Theodor Arentz, O.F.M., took over the administration of St. Marys. In the year 1883 Humphrey, 4½ miles northwest of St. Marys, branched off as an independent parish, under the direction of the Reverend Fr. Theodor, who for the time being served both parishes. In August 1883 St. Marys again received a pastor of its own in the person of the Reverend Fr. Servatius Rasche, O.F.M., who from the year 1886, when the monastery at Humphrey had been built, was to serve St. Marys from Humphrey. In December Fr. Antonius Moll followed; in August 1888 Fr. Suitbertus Albersmann; in July 1891 Fr. Patrizius Degan; in July 1892 Fr. Valentin Dörenkemper; the latter erected a new, beautiful church in 1893. In August 1895 Fr. Severinus Westhoff followed him; in August 1896 Fr. Edmund Rödiger; in September 1900 Fr. Stanislaus Riemann; in June 1901 Fr. Ludovicus Döring; and in September 1903 Fr. Romuald Rheindorff. The latter at once, at the beginning of his administration, erected a beautiful, solid rectory. Until then the priest had only a small, narrow room behind the sanctuary of the church for his dwelling. And in this year 1908 Fr. Romuald erected a new, beautiful school with a sisters' residence, built of brick. St. Marys has about 50 families, all German farmers, with only one exception.

2. Madison.

The parish in the little town of Madison, 10 miles north of Humphrey, at present has about 70 to 80 families, of whom half are Germans. Until the year 1876 there were only a few Catholics in Madison and vicinity. In April of the year 1877 the Franciscans of Columbus took charge of the Catholics in

[Photograph: The Reverend Fr. Angelus Bill, O.F.M.]

[Photograph: The Reverend Fr. Cyprian Kanscheid, O.F.M., Provincial.]

Madison and visited them often, which was connected with great hardship, since there was as yet no railroad from Columbus to Madison, and the 35 miles had to be covered in heavy wagons and on bad roads. When a few years later the monastery at St. Bernard was erected, Madison was served from there. Fr. Benignus Schütz built a little church near the town in 1881. Until then divine service had been held in private houses, especially in the house of the O'Shea and Lindner family. From 1881 on Madison regularly had divine service every 14 days. After Fr. Benignus the following Fathers served Madison: Fr. Maximilian Klein, Fr. Lukas Mierjowsky, Fr. Bonaventura Faulhaber. From July 1890 on Madison was served from Humphrey, namely by the following Fathers: Fr. Marzellinus Kollmeyer, from July 1890–1894; Fr. Hieronymus Hellhake, from September 1894–1897; Fr. Bartholomäus Feldmann, from August 1897–1905; from 1905 until now Fr. Hildebrand Fuchs.

In the year 1898 Fr. Bartholomäus acquired a beautifully situated plot in the town for church purposes and had the church moved there and enlarged. But since the parish kept growing, he saw himself obliged to build a new, larger church. This was done in the year 1902. For lack of funds, however, the church could not be finished at once; only the lower structure (basement), which is later to form the winter chapel, was completed, provided with a roof, and so arranged for divine service. Fr. Bartholomäus had the old church converted into a parish school and sisters' residence and opened the school in the same year. Since August 1902 Madison has divine service every Sunday.

3. Tarnov.

About 6 miles south of Humphrey is a Polish settlement named Tarnov. Until the year 1880 the Polish families there, together with the Germans of that region, formed one parish, namely the St. Antonius-Gemeinde (St. Anthony Parish). In the year 1880 the Poles separated from the Germans and formed a parish of their own. The Reverend Franciscan Father Cyrillus Augustynski built them a little wooden church, 2½ miles northeast of the St. Antonius-Kirche (St. Anthony Church). In 1883 a little rectory was built. In the year 1886 a parish school was erected. At first a lay teacher was employed, but in the year 1891 the Franciscan Sisters of Lafayette were called to take over the school. In 1892 the church was enlarged and a sacristy added. Until 1889 Fr. Anastasius Czech was the very zealous and prudent pastor of the parish; he died on 13 March 1896 in the hospital at Omaha and was buried in Columbus.

Ninth Chapter.



Order of Divine Service and Special Devotions.

I. Order of Services.

1. On Sundays and holy days: At 8 o'clock is the first holy Mass with an English sermon. At 10 o'clock is High Mass with a German sermon. At 2:30 is catechism instruction.

At 3 o'clock Vespers and Benediction; on the first Sunday of the month, the Heart of Mary Confraternity [uncertain: Herz-Mariä-Bruderschaft] and Benediction.

2. On weekdays: At 6 o'clock the first holy Mass. At 8 o'clock the school Mass; during vacation the last holy Mass is at 7:30.

II. Special Devotions.

1. The Lenten Devotion.

During the forty-day season of Lent, on Sundays instead of Vespers there is the Way of the Cross devotion in the German language and Benediction; on Wednesdays at 7 o'clock in the evening the Way of the Cross in the English language and Benediction. Also throughout the whole year on Friday evening at 7 o'clock there is the Way of the Cross and Benediction.

2. The May Devotion.

This beautiful devotion is joined with the school Mass on weekdays, but on Fridays it is held in the evening instead of the Way of the Cross, and on Sunday afternoons instead of Vespers.

3. Devotion to the Blessed Sacrament.

a) On Holy Thursday the High Mass begins at 9 o'clock. After it there is adoration of the Most Blessed Sacrament the whole day, in the same order as at the Forty Hours' Prayer.

b) On the Feast of Corpus Christi there is a Levite Mass at 9 o'clock, after it an outdoor procession on the procession path planted on both sides with shade trees. The order in it is as follows:

1. Cross-bearers and schoolchildren. 2. The boys who in the last two years have received their first holy Communion, with their insignia. 3. The Young Men's Sodality with sashes. 4. The girls who in the last two years have received their first holy Communion. 5. The Young Women's Sodality. 6. Girls dressed in white, who strew flowers. 7. Altar servers and priests with the Most Blessed Sacrament. 8. Singers and band. 9. St. Joseph's Men's Society. 10. The Catholic Foresters' Society. 11. The men and young men who belong to no society. 12. The Christian Mothers' Society. 13. The women and young women who belong to no society.

During the octave of Corpus Christi, every day the last holy Mass is celebrated before the exposed Most Holy Sacrament, with Benediction.

[Photograph: The Reverend Fr. Jakobus Nolte, O.F.M.]

c) The Forty Hours' Prayer is held every year in Lent on the days appointed by the Right Reverend Bishop, on which occasion the whole parish receives the holy Sacraments. The order of the hours of adoration is as follows:

9–10 the Third Order. 12–1 the schoolboys. 1–2 the schoolgirls. 2–3 the young women. 3 o'clock Vespers. 4–5 the women. 5–6 the young men. 6–7 the men.

On the last day of the adoration, at 7 o'clock in the evening there is a closing devotion with a solemn procession, in which, besides the schoolchildren, the various societies also take part.

4. The Sacred Heart Devotion.

On every first Friday of the month, after the last Mass, a special devotion is held. On that day a great number receive holy Communion, in order to keep the so-called 9 Fridays.

5. The St. Anthony Devotion.

On all Tuesdays of the year the last holy Mass is read before the exposed Most Blessed Sacrament; after it there is a short devotion in honor of St. Anthony and Benediction. All the faithful can gain a plenary indulgence on every Tuesday if they receive the holy Sacraments and pray for a time before the exposed Most Blessed Sacrament in a Franciscan church according to the intention of the Holy Father.

6. The Portiuncula Indulgence.

All the faithful can gain, on the Feast of Portiuncula, as often a plenary indulgence as, after worthily receiving the holy Sacraments of Penance and of the Altar, they visit a Franciscan church and there say some prayers according to the intention of the Holy Father, about 5 Our Fathers and 5 Hail Marys. Holy confession can be made on 30 and 31 July, or on 1 and 2 August. Holy Communion must be received on 1 or 2 August. Confession and Communion can take place in any church. The visits to the church can begin already on the afternoon before, about 2 o'clock, and be continued until sunset on 2 August. The first indulgence one gains for oneself, the rest for the poor souls in Purgatory.



Tenth Chapter.



Practical Remarks concerning the Holy Sacraments.

#### 1. Baptism.

1) Since Baptism is the most necessary sacrament, parents should see to it that the child is baptized as soon as possible after birth. Truly Christian parents do not put off Baptism beyond the third day. To postpone Baptism too long is dangerous and therefore sinful.

2) See to it that there are good sponsors (godparents). One is enough. If the desired sponsor is prevented from appearing in person, another can take his place. The actual sponsor, however, must know of this and have given his consent beforehand. Catholics who neglect their religious duties can be admitted as sponsors just as little as Protestants.

3) Emergency baptism (Nothtaufe, baptism in case of necessity) can be conferred by anyone; if it can conveniently be done, call the priest.

Baptism is conferred by pouring holy water, or, in the absence of it, other natural water over the head of the child and saying the words: "I baptize you in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit." Sponsors are not required for this. If the child gets better again, bring it to church so that the ceremonies can be supplied afterward. Make the priest aware that the child has already received emergency baptism. If the priest has reason to doubt the validity of the emergency baptism, he will repeat the Baptism conditionally. Give the child only Christian names.

Note. Women after childbirth (Wöchnerinnen) who, following an old pious custom, wish to be blessed (the "churching" of women), should present themselves at the monastery gate or at the Communion rail.

#### 2. Confirmation.

Confirmation is administered every three or four years, according to the direction of the bishop. Reception is preceded by Confirmation instruction, in which all who are to be confirmed take part.

#### 3. The Blessed Sacrament of the Altar

is kept day and night in the church. Believing Christians greet the Saviour when they pass by the church or meet a priest who is carrying the holy Sacrament to a sick person, and they conduct themselves with reverence in the house of God. They kneel on the right knee to the floor when they come into the church or leave it; on both knees when the holy Sacrament is exposed. To receive holy Communion there is

opportunity daily, and one should not content oneself with going to the Table of the Lord only once or twice a year, but if possible every month.

#### 4. The Sacrament of Penance.

In the local church there is opportunity for confession daily. On Saturdays and on the eves of holy days of obligation confessions are heard from 2 o'clock in the afternoon; on Sundays and holy days in the morning before the 8 o'clock Mass.

#### 5. Extreme Unction (the holy Anointing).

If someone is dangerously ill, have the priest come in time so that he can administer the last rites to him. If the way is long, provide a conveyance. If the priest carries holy Communion to the sick person, refrain on the way from all unnecessary talk. At home, have a table prepared, covered with a white cloth, on which there are a crucifix between two burning candles, a vessel with holy water, another with drinking water, a spoon, a plate with cotton wool, and a vessel with salt or bread. When the priest enters the house or room, all fall on their knees at once and adore the Divine Saviour.

During the sick person's holy confession, all should leave the sickroom, and indeed so far away that they can hear nothing of the confession.

During holy Communion all are present and pray kneeling. Also, when one approaches the holy Sacrament on the table, or passes before it, one should kneel.

During the holy Anointing, if possible let someone lead the prayers, while all kneel.

#### 6. Holy Orders (the Ordination to the Priesthood).

Since this parish has existed, the happy event has not yet occurred that one of its sons has received the sacrament of Holy Orders. But a number of young men are preparing themselves, as already mentioned above, through their studies for the sublime dignity of the priesthood; and, God willing, in two years the first priest of St. Francis Parish will already stand at the altar.

#### 7. The Sacrament of Marriage.

"This is a great mystery, but I speak in Christ and in the Church." (Ephes. 3:32 [Eph. 5:32 in modern numbering].)

1. Those who intend to enter the holy state of marriage should consider that they are taking a most important step, in which this applies quite especially: All depends on God's blessing.

[Photograph: The Reverend Fr. Florentius Kurzer, O.F.M.]

Therefore they should prepare themselves for it through purity of life, earnest prayer, worthy reception of the holy Sacraments (a general confession is always to be recommended), and other good works.

2. They should register with the pastor a few days before the first publication of the banns and on this occasion give not only their own names but also the names of their parents. They must also tell the pastor when and where both parties were baptized, because according to the newest provisions of the Church the pastor must see to it that after the wedding it is recorded in the baptismal register concerned that they have received the holy Sacrament of Marriage. It is best if they present the baptismal certificate to the pastor, in case they were baptized in another church.

If there should be an impediment to marriage (relationship to the 4th degree inclusive, affinity, betrothal, etc.), this should be conscientiously communicated to the pastor, so that if necessary he can apply for a dispensation.

3. Before the wedding, instruction for the bridal couple is given according to need.

4. The publication of the banns as a rule takes place three times.

5. The wedding is performed by the pastor or his representative. If bride and bridegroom are from different parishes, the pastor of the bride as a rule blesses the marriage.

6. The wedding takes place, in accordance with the wish of the Church, during holy Mass.

7. After the wedding, one appears with the witnesses, who should always be Catholic, in the pastor's room and pays the fees.

8. If a wedding celebration is held, conduct yourselves as if Jesus and Mary were present, as at the wedding at Cana.

#### Mixed Marriages

are a great evil;

1. Because the Catholic party is exposed to great danger of losing his faith or becoming indifferent toward it.

2. Because the Catholic upbringing of the children is usually defective and not seldom impossible.

3. Because the non-Catholic can separate from the Catholic spouse and marry again according to his religion, which is not permitted to the Catholic.

4. Because the happiness of married life together depends above all on communion in faith.

Therefore the Catholic Church has always disapproved of such marriages and gives the required dispensation only in quite urgent cases.

In doing so, however, she requires:

1. That the free exercise of religion be guaranteed to the Catholic party;

2. That all the children be baptized and raised in the Catholic Church.

But even in this case the priest may not publicly announce the names of the marriage candidates in church, still less perform the wedding at the altar, but only be present as a witness at the marriage in the rectory or elsewhere.

So strongly does the Church disapprove of mixed marriages, and she seeks to warn her children against them.

Catholics should guard against beginning an acquaintance with non-Catholics. Once a courtship exists, it is very hard to break it off.

To let oneself be married by a Protestant minister is a betrayal of the holy faith and is punished by the Church with exclusion from her communion. Such a marriage is also invalid before God and the Church; it is likewise invalid if it is contracted before a civil judge.
