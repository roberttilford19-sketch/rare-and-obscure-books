## DEDICATION

This one has no great translator tragedy behind it — its story is the text's
own. It is dedicated to the unknown.

Some nineteen hundred years ago, somebody — a name lost to history — sat down
and wrote a small book about a painted tablet showing the whole of human life:
the gate we all walk through, the drinks we are all given, the wrong roads,
the steep road, and the mountain at the end. He (or she) signed it, in effect,
to Cebes of Thebes, the friend of Socrates who sits with the mourners in
Plato's *Phaedo*. Whether that was homage, a joke, or a marketing decision, we
will never know; what scholars agree on is that the real Cebes did not write
it, and the true author is Anonymous.

And yet the book would not die. Century after century, teachers kept copying
it, printers kept printing it, and readers kept reading it — not because of
whose name was on it, but because it works. It is, in Kenneth Guthrie's happy
phrase, the Greek *Pilgrim's Progress*: a road-map of a human life, drawn so
plainly that schoolchildren and philosophers alike could follow it. This draft
is offered to the unknown author in thanks — the one whose name did not
survive, but whose picture did.

## A NOTE ON AUTHORSHIP — READ THIS FIRST

This work is **attributed to** Cebes of Thebes, the friend of Socrates who
appears in Plato's *Phaedo*. That attribution is almost certainly wrong.
Scholars judge the *Tablet* (Greek *Pinax*) to be **spurious** — most likely
written in the **1st or 2nd century AD**, some four to five hundred years after
Cebes lived, by an author whose name is unknown.

Nothing in this draft presents the work as genuinely by Cebes. The title page,
the running heads, and this note all say "attributed to." The ancient habit of
putting a famous name on a book does not make the name true, and this project
does not repeat the claim.

## METHOD STATEMENT

**Base text (exactly one):** Kenneth Sylvan Guthrie's 1910 English translation,
*The Greek Pilgrim's Progress. Generally known as the Picture, by Kebes*
(London: Luzac; Philadelphia: the Platonist Press), as transcribed on
Wikisource. Guthrie translated from the Greek text published by B. G. Teubner
of Leipzig. The other public-domain English renderings — Hugh Exton Seebohm
(1906) and R. Thomson Clark (1909) — were consulted for the record but were
**not** used; this draft follows Guthrie alone. Fitzgerald & White (1983) was
not touched: it is under copyright.

**Attribution:** the work is *attributed to* Cebes of Thebes; scholars judge the attribution spurious — see the authorship note above.

**Public-domain verification:** Guthrie's translation was published in 1910,
before 1929; Kenneth Sylvan Guthrie died in 1940. It is in the public domain in
the United States. The underlying Greek text is some nineteen centuries old.

**What this draft is:** a rendering of Guthrie's English into plain, modern
English — the kind of wording a humble peasant could understand. No thee, thou,
or thy. Guthrie's numbered sections (1–41) and his five-part division are
kept, so every passage can be checked against the base.

**What was changed in the wording:** Guthrie's most awkward coinage,
"Scientific-Recognition" (Greek *epistêmê*), is rendered here as
**Understanding** — pure knowledge, real understanding of what is good. His
"Good Genius" (Greek *daimôn*) is kept as the figure's name and glossed in the
word list. Capitalized personifications (Delusion, Luck, Opinion, Culture, and
so on) are kept, because the whole book is an allegory and the capitals are
the map.

**What was not carried over:** Guthrie's own 1910 introduction is his personal
commentary, not the ancient text, so it is not reproduced here. Its one point
worth keeping is noted plainly: Guthrie himself suspected that the closing
discussion — whether wealth, health, and the like are really good or evil —
may be a later addition with a Stoic flavor. That suspicion is Guthrie's, not
this draft's; the closing section is rendered whole, like everything else.

**What this draft does not claim:** This is not a first translation, not the
oldest, not definitive, and not complete in any scholarly sense. It is a
working draft of one public-domain rendering, made readable. Nothing more.

## FIRST: THE GATE OF LIFE AND ITS DELUSIONS

**1.** One day we happened to be walking thoughtfully through a temple of
Saturn, looking with reverence at the offerings people had hung there. Fixed
prominently to the front of the temple was a strange painted tablet, with
certain peculiar words on it whose meaning we could not make out.

It did not seem to show a city or an army camp, but a triple ring — three
circular walled enclosures, one inside the other.

Within the outermost circular wall you could see a crowd of women, while
outside, around the outer gate, a great mob was pressing forward. An old man
seemed to be standing in front of the streams of people entering, giving them
some command by his gestures.

**2.** We stood there a long while, asking each other what the picture could
mean, when an old man who happened to be passing stopped and spoke to us:

"Strangers, there is nothing unusual in your being puzzled by this picture.
It is a riddle even to many of the people who live here. This offering did not
come from this place. Long ago a stranger arrived here — a man full of
understanding and impressive in his wisdom, who followed with zeal, in word and
deed, the way of life of Pythagoras or Parmenides. He is the one who
dedicated both the temple and the picture to Saturn."

"So you saw and knew this man yourself?" I asked.

"Indeed I did!" he said. "And what is more, I admired him for a long time in
my youth. It was his way to hold many serious conversations, and many is the
time I have heard him explain this symbolic picture!"

**3.** "By Jupiter!" I cried, "unless you have some pressing business
elsewhere, please explain the picture to us too! I assure you we are most
anxious to understand what this symbol means!"

"With pleasure, strangers!" he said. "But first you must hear that such an
explanation is not without real danger."

"How so?" we all cried.

"If you understand and take in what I tell you," he said, "you will become
wise and happy. But if not, you will live badly — you will become foolish,
unlucky, bitter, and ignorant.

"For the explanation is much like the riddle of the Sphinx, which she put to
all men. Whoever solved it was saved; but the Sphinx destroyed those who could
not. And this was the Sphinx's question: *In our life, what is a good? What is
an evil? What is neither?*

"If anyone does not solve this, the Sphinx destroys him — not all at once, as
in ancient times, but gradually, through his whole life, he wastes away, like
victims tortured to death.

"But if he understands, he is saved, and gains happiness.

"So pay attention, and make sure you understand!"

**4.** "By Hercules!" I said. "What a fire you have lit in us, if what you say
is true!"

"Indeed it is!" he said.

"Then start explaining at once! We will listen with a will — especially
considering the price of failure."

"Well," said the old man, pointing with his staff, "do you see that outer
circular wall?"

"Yes, indeed!"

"First, you must know that the name of this whole place is **the Life**. This
countless crowd pressing in front of the gate are those who are about to enter
into life.

"The old man who holds a scroll in one hand and points with the other is the
**Good Genius**. He is telling those who are entering what they must do once
they have entered, and showing them which road they must walk if they intend
to be saved in the Life."

**5.** "Which way does he tell them to go? And why?" I asked.

"Do you see," he said, "beside the gate where the crowd is to enter, a throne
on which sits a woman dressed in showy clothes — indeed, overdressed — holding
in her hands what look like cups? Do you see her?"

"Indeed I do," I answered, "but who is she?"

"Her name is **Delusion**," he answered, "because she deludes and misleads
every human being."

"But what is her business?"

"She quenches the thirst of every soul that comes into life by making it drink
of her own essence."

"And what might her drink be, I wonder?"

"**Error and Ignorance**," he said.

"Why so?"

"Because they would not enter into life unless they were under that
influence."

"I wonder whether all drink of this Delusion, or only some?"

"All drink," he said, "though in different measure — some drink more, others
less."

**6.** "Then do you not see, inside the gate, a crowd of women dressed in the
flashy clothes of prostitutes?"

"Indeed I see them!"

"Well, their names are **False Opinion, Desire, and Pleasure**. They fall upon
the souls as they enter, and each one takes a soul in her arms and leads it
away."

"And where to? I would like to know!"

"Some to be saved, indeed — but others, alas, to be destroyed by Delusion."

"O Good Genius of ours, how fateful is that drink!"

"Surely, for each of those women promises the soul she has taken in her arms
that she will lead it to the best things, and to a happy and profitable life.
And she succeeds — because of that drink, the souls cannot discover which is
the true road in the Life, but wander around, as you see. For those who have
already entered drift about as if by chance."

**7.** "I see them indeed," I said.

"But tell me, who can that woman be, who looks both wild and blind, standing
on a round stone?"

"Her name," he answered, "is **Luck**."

"Not only blind and wild, but deaf too."

"And what might her business be?"

"She goes around everywhere," he said. "From some she takes their goods and
freely gives them to others. Then again, she suddenly takes back what she has
given and hands it to others, with no plan and no steadiness. So you see that
her symbol fits her perfectly."

"Which symbol?" I asked.

"Why, the round stone she stands on."

"And what does that mean, I wonder?"

"That round stone means that none of her gifts is safe or lasting. For whoever
puts any trust in her is sure to suffer great and grievous misfortune."

**8.** "But what do that great crowd standing around her want, and what are
they called?"

"Oh! They are known as the **Unthinking** — those who desire whatever Luck
may throw to them."

"But then how is it that they do not all act the same? Some seem to rejoice,
while others are in anguish, with their hands stretched out?"

"Well, those who seem to rejoice and laugh are the ones who have received
something from her — and you may be sure they call her Good Fortune! On the
other hand, those who seem to weep and stretch out their hands are the ones
from whom she has taken back what she gave — they call her Misfortune!"

"And what sort of things does she deal in, that those who receive them laugh
while those who lose them weep?"

"Why, what seems good to the great crowd — of course **wealth**; then fame,
good birth, children, power, palaces, and the like."

"But are such things not really good?"

"That question, let us put off for later!"

"Willingly," I said.

**9.** "Now, as you enter within the gate, do you see the second circular
enclosure, and outside it certain women dressed like prostitutes?"

"Clearly!"

"Well, their names are **Lack of Self-Control, Indulgence, Never-Satisfied,
and Flattery**."

"And why do they stand there?"

"They are watching for those who may have received something from the goddess
of Luck."

"And then what happens?"

"The women spring upon those souls, take them in their arms, flatter them, and
coax them not to go away but to stay for a life of comfort, without effort or
trouble. If any soul lets them persuade him into a life of enjoyment, it seems
a pleasant pastime until he is sated — but no longer. For when he sobers up,
he finds that he has not eaten, but that he has been eaten by her, and
mistreated. So when he has used up all the goods he received from Fortune, he
is forced to slave for those women, to suffer all things, to be disgraced, and
on their account to do many pitiable deeds — to steal, to rob temples, to
swear falsely, to betray, to plunder.

"But when he has sunk himself to utter beggary, then he is handed over to
**Punishment**."

**10.** "But who is she?"

"Do you not see behind them," he said, "something like a small door, and a
narrow, damp place?"

"Yes, indeed!"

"And do you notice in it some women — shameful, bedraggled, and ragged?"

"Why, yes!"

"Well, among them, the one holding the whip is called **Punishment**, while
the one with her head bowed down to her knees is **Sorrow**, and the one
tearing her own hair is **Grief**."

"But what about that thing standing by them — so ugly, thin, and naked — and
near it that other shameful female like it? Who is she?"

"Ah," he said, "that is **Lamentation**, and his sister is **Despair**. To
these, then, the soul is handed over, and is punished by keeping company with
them. From there he is thrown into another dwelling, into **Unhappiness**,
where he drags out his life in every misery — unless, indeed, **Repentance**
should unexpectedly meet him, having planned it."

**11.** "Well, what happens if Repentance chances to meet him?"

"She frees him from his evils and gives him a new companion — another Opinion
and Desire — who will lead him to true Culture. Though indeed he might just as
well be misled even then to **Sham Culture**."

"Well, what happens then?"

"If he is taken in hand," he said, "by this Right Opinion, who will lead him
to true Culture, then, being purified by her, he is saved, and his life grows
blissful and happy. Otherwise, he wanders again, deceived by Sham Culture."

**12.** "By Hercules! What another great danger is here! Tell me more plainly
about Sham Culture!"

"Well, do you see, standing by the gate of the inner enclosure, a woman who
looks neat and well-groomed?"

"She is called **Culture** by the unthinking crowd," he said, "but that is a
mistake, for she is nothing but a sham.

"Nevertheless, those who are being saved must pass by her in order to reach
true Culture. There is no other way."

"So there is no other way?"

"No, there is not."

**13.** "And can you tell me who those men are, walking about within the
second enclosure?"

"Those," he said, "are the deluded followers of Sham Culture — they honestly
labor under the impression that they are, right now, keeping company with true
Culture!"

"And what might they be called?"

"Some," he answered, "are poets; some are so-called orators. Some are
debaters; others are musicians, mathematicians, geometricians, astronomers,
critics, followers of Aristippus who chase pleasure, and Aristotelian
walking-and-talking scholars!"

**14.** "But those women who seem to move among them — indeed, they look like
the first ones, among whom was Pleasure and her companions — who are they?"

"They are the very same," he said.

"But how did they get in?"

"By changing their looks; for here they look needy, and not as before."

"And have those False Opinions stayed the same?" I asked.

"That potion which they drank from Delusion is still working in them — and so
are Ignorance, Senselessness, Prejudice, and other badness. None of this fades
from them until they leave Sham Culture, take the right road, and drink the
purifying medicines. When this cleansing has stripped off all their evils,
such as Prejudice and Ignorance — then, and not before, shall they be saved.

"But if they choose to stay with Sham Culture, they shall never be released;
nor shall any science release them from a single evil."

## SECOND: THE ROAD UPWARD TO HAPPINESS

**15.** "What then is the road that leads to true Culture?" I asked.

"Do you see up there," he said, "a place where no one lives, and which looks
like a desert?"

"I do."

"Do you not see a small door, and a path not much traveled — only a few are
climbing it, for it is almost impassable, rough and rocky?"

"Yes indeed," I said.

"And do you not see something like a steep hill, which can only be climbed by
a narrow ascent between cliffs?

"That, then, is the road to Culture."

"And hard enough it looks!"

"But it leads up Culture's rock, which is large, high, and inaccessible."

**16.** "Now do you not see further, standing on the rock, two healthy,
well-built women stretching out their hands invitingly?"

"Yes, but who are they?"

"**Self-Control and Endurance** — two sisters."

"But why are they stretching out their arms so invitingly?"

"They are urging the pilgrims who reach that place not to despair, but to take
heart — for they will reach a fair road if only they will be brave for a
little while longer."

"Encouragement is good," I said, "but what use is it? For I see, further up
the road, a gap where there is no road."

"Those women will themselves come down from the cliff and draw the pilgrims up
to where they stand. Then the women will let the pilgrims rest; and after a
little while they will give them **Strength and Courage**, and promise to lead
them into the presence of true Culture.

"Then the women will show the rested pilgrims the road beyond — which is, as
you see, fair, level, passable, and free from all evils."

"Clearly, by Jupiter!"

**17.** "Do you not see, in front of that grove, a place that looks fair and
grassy, lit by a white light?"

"Yes, indeed!"

"Do you see in the middle of the meadow another enclosure, with its gate?"

"It is so, but what is the name of that place?"

"It is the **Dwelling of the Blessed**," he said. "Here live all the Virtues,
and Happiness."

"Is it possible? How beautiful that place must be!"

**18.** "Now do you see by the gate a woman, fair and calm, middle-aged or
rather older, dressed in a simple, unadorned robe? She stands, not on a globe,
but on a solidly founded cube. With her stand two other, younger women who
seem to be her daughters."

"So it looks."

"Well, the woman standing in the middle is **Culture**; the others are
**Truth** and **Conviction**, her daughters.

"But why does Culture stand on a cube?"

"As a sign that, for pilgrims approaching her, the road to her is certain and
safe — as indeed are her gifts."

"And what might her gifts be?"

"**Courage and Fearlessness**!"

"But what do they consist of?" I asked.

"Courage and Fearlessness consist in the realization that nothing that could
ever happen to us could turn out to be a misfortune."

**19.** "By Hercules," I said, "what fine gifts! But why does she stand
outside the circle like that?"

"In order to heal the new arrivals," he said. "She gives them the cleansing
medicine; and when they have been purified, she leads them in to the Virtues."

"But how does this happen? I do not understand that."

"But you will," he said. "It is as if a sick man, ambitious to get well,
should go to a physician, who first removes the cause of the sickness, and so
leads him through recovery to health. But if the sick man will not follow the
prescription, it is only fair that he should be left to the ravages of the
disease."

"Oh, I understand that," I said.

"Just so acts Culture," our guide went on. "Whenever any pilgrim reaches her,
she heals him and doses him with her own power, so as first to cleanse him of
all the evils lodged in him — Ignorance and Error, with which Delusion had
infected the pilgrim; Arrogance, Lust, Lack of Self-Control, Anger, Love of
Money, and all the rest of the evils with which the pilgrim was infected in
the first enclosure."

**20.** "Now when the pilgrim is cleansed, where does she send him?"

"In there, to **Understanding**, and to the other Virtues."

"And which are these?"

He said: "Do you not see within the gate a company of women, who look
attractive and neat, in simple, unadorned robes? See how sweet they look in
their simple dress, and not overdressed like the others?"

"I see," I said. "But what are their names?"

"The first is **Understanding**; the others are her sisters: **Fortitude,
Righteousness, Fairness, Wisdom, Poise, Freedom, Temperance, and
Gentleness**."

"O kindly guide of ours, what great things we may hope for!"

"Surely! But only on condition that you understand what you see, and take good
heed of what you have heard from me."

"But we most surely do!" we all cried as if with one voice.

"Then you shall also be saved!" he cried.

**21.** "Now, when they have received the pilgrim, where do they lead him?"

"To their mother **Happiness**," he said.

"But who and where is she?"

"Follow the road up that mountain which stands at the heart of the enclosures,
and you come to a temple porch, beside which sits on a high throne a glorious
woman, dressed nobly but simply, and crowned with a splendid wreath of
flowers. Well, she is Happiness."

**22.** "Now, whenever anyone reaches her, what does she do?"

"Happiness, with all the other Virtues, crowns him as victor in the greatest
of struggles — namely, against the most terrible beasts, who before had
enslaved, tormented, and devoured him. All these he has now overcome and
driven away from himself, holding himself well in hand — so that those whom he
formerly served must now serve him."

**23.** "I am anxious to know who these wild beasts you mentioned are!"

"**Ignorance**," he said, "and **Error**. Or do you not consider them wild
beasts?"

"And pretty savage ones too!" I agreed.

"Then **Sorrow, Despair, Love of Money, Lack of Self-Control**, and all other
badness. All these he now rules, instead of, as before, being ruled by them."

"O glorious deeds," I cried, "and splendid victory! But what is the power of
the victor's crown?"

"It blesses with unspeakable felicity. He who is crowned with this virtue
becomes truly happy, resting his hope of gaining happiness, and of keeping it,
not on others, but on himself."

## THIRD: THOSE WHO FAIL, AND WHY

**24.** "Glorious, the victory you speak of! But after the pilgrim is crowned,
what does he do, and where does he go?"

"The Virtues who welcomed him lead him back to the place he came from, and
show him how badly off are those who live there so wretchedly — as if
shipwrecked in their lives, straying and wandering, dragged about by enemies
such as Lack of Self-Control, Arrogance, Love of Money, Fancies, and other
such evils.

"These misfortunes keep them from rescuing themselves from this endless
tossing by reaching the Mountain of Safety.

"They suffer this only because they cannot find the road here — for they have
forgotten the instructions they received from the Good Genius."

**25.** Then I said, "You seem to speak rightly. But I am not yet quite clear
on one point: namely, why the Virtues show the pilgrim the place he
originally came from."

Our guide said: "None of these things could the pilgrim accurately understand
or grasp while he was in doubt, because of the Ignorance and Error he had
drunk in — so that he called good what was not good, and evil what was not
evil. That is why, like those who stayed there, the pilgrim dragged out a
miserable life.

"But now, since he has gained understanding of what is truly beneficial, he
lives pleasantly, seeing how badly off those others are."

**26.** "Well, now that he sees all these things, what will he do, and where
will he go?"

"Wherever he pleases; for now he is as safe anywhere as if he were in the
Korykian Cave. Wherever he arrives he will live fairly and safely — for just
as the sick welcome the physician, so all will receive him with pleasure and
gratitude."

"And he no longer fears suffering anything from those women who, you say, are
really wild beasts?"

"No, indeed! No more can he be troubled by Grief or Sorrow, by Lack of
Self-Control or Love of Money, by Need or any other evil — for now he is lord
over all those by whom he was formerly grieved.

"Just like a snake-charmer, whose snakes kill everyone but him, yet do not
harm him, because he has an antidote against them — just like this snake-charmer
who cannot be harmed, the crowned pilgrim is no more grieved by any of them,
being safe because of the antidote he carries."

**27.** "It seems to me you have spoken well. But tell me further: who are
they who seem to be coming down from the mountain? For some of them are
crowned and make joyful gestures, while others are uncrowned, grieved and
distressed. They seem so weary in head and limb that they truly need to be
held up by certain women!"

"The crowned are those who were saved by Culture, and they rejoice at having
reached her. The uncrowned, however, are those whom Culture rejected, and who
are returning to a miserable, wretched life — or those who, while climbing up
to Endurance, grew timid and turned back, wandering without a path."

"And who are the women holding them up?"

"**Griefs, Sorrows, Faint-Heartedness, Scorn, and Ignorances**."

**28.** "Why, I thought you said that all the evils went with them?"

"And they all do, by Jupiter! For when they return into the first enclosure,
to Sensuality and Lack of Self-Control, they do not blame themselves — far
from it! At once they speak ill of Culture and of those who go to her, saying
that they are wretched, miserable, and unhappy, and that by leaving their
accustomed way of life they live badly, enjoying none of the good things —
that is, none of the things counted as good among themselves."

"And what goods may they be referring to?"

"Why, to debauchery and lack of self-control, to speak plainly — for their
highest ideal is to feed like cattle."

**29.** "And what about those other women coming down from there, cheerful in
face and all wreathed in smiles?"

"They are **Opinions**; and whenever they have successfully led any pilgrim to
Culture and introduced him to the Virtues, they return to lead up others, and
announce to them the blessedness of those they led up before."

"But why do they not themselves go in among the Virtues, and stay?"

"Because it is not fitting for mere Opinions to enter in to Understanding.
The most they can do is introduce a pilgrim to Culture. Then all they can do
is return and bring up others — just as ships, when unloaded, return to be
loaded again."

---

## FOURTH: THE VALUE OF LEARNING

**30.** "It seems to me you have explained these things very well. But there
is one thing you have not yet made plain to us — namely, what the Good Genius
teaches those who are entering into life."

"'Take heart, and be courageous,' he says. So take heart yourselves, for I
shall explain all things and leave nothing out."

"Well spoken!" I cried.

Then the old man, stretching out his hand again, said: "Look at that figure
who seems to be blind, standing on the round stone — whose name, as I told
you before, is Luck."

"We see her."

**31.** "Well, he warns the souls not to trust her. Nothing received from her
should be counted reliable or safe, nor counted as one's own — for nothing
stops her from taking it back and giving it to someone else. Why, that is a
common occurrence. So, he warns, let no man be moved by her gifts — neither to
joy when receiving them, nor to sorrow when losing them; neither to praise nor
to blame them. For nothing she does comes from thought — only by chance, just
as things happen, as I told you before.

"So the Good Genius warns men to pay no attention to anything she does, and
not to be like bad bankers, who rejoice when they receive money from some man
and count it as their own, but as soon as they get notice of withdrawal they
take offense and consider themselves badly wronged — forgetting that they
received the deposit on this very condition, that the depositor may take it
back without difficulty.

"The Good Genius advises the same attitude toward the gifts of the goddess of
Luck: to remember that it is no more than her nature to take back what she
gave, and soon to give many other gifts again, then to take back again — not
only what she gave, but whatever a man possessed before. So, he warns, accept
whatever she may give; and as soon as you have it in hand, take it and go at
once to the blessings that are reliable and lasting."

**32.** "But what may these be?" I asked.

"What is received from Culture."

"And what may that be?"

"True understanding of what is beneficial — a safe, reliable, lasting gift,"
he said. "To flee to her at once is his command. And when the pilgrim reaches
those women who, as I said before, are called Lack of Self-Control and
Sensuality, he is not to trust them but to leave them and go on to Sham
Culture. There he should stay a little while, gathering from her whatever
accomplishments may serve as travel rations to keep him going until he reaches
true Culture.

"Whoever disobeys this warning, or even only misunderstands it, wastes away
miserably."

**33.** "Now, strangers, this is the meaning of the picture. Do not hesitate
to ask any further questions about it; I shall be glad to answer."

"Well then, I will ask you: what sort of accomplishments does the Good Genius
advise the pilgrim to take from Sham Culture?"

"Whatever a man may think might be useful to him."

"And what is your advice in the matter?"

"Knowledge of languages, first; and then enough of the other sciences to act,
in Plato's phrase, as a check-rein against eccentricities. Make no mistake —
they are not necessary, but they are useful for traveling more efficiently.
Of course, they are no help morally."

"So then you declare flatly that these accomplishments do not help a man
become better?"

"Not at all. For a man must improve without them, though they have their
place. We may catch the meaning through an interpreter, yet it is not useless
to understand the words themselves, if we care about accuracy. Still, nothing
stops us from becoming better without those accomplishments."

**34.** "Well then, according to what you say, learned men have no advantage
over other men in becoming better?"

"What advantage could they have, when they are caught in the same delusion
about the nature of goods as the unlearned, and are still ruled by their
vices? For nothing stops a man from knowing languages and being an expert in
every field of science, and still being drunken and lacking self-control, fond
of money, unjust, treacherous — and even a fool."

"Yes indeed! One can see many such!"

"What advantage, then, could these men have in becoming better, merely because
of their scientific accomplishments?"

**35.** "Certainly none, according to what you say. But why then do they stay
within the second enclosure, as if they still wished to approach true
Culture?"

"And what use to them is that nearness? For how often do you see later
pilgrims arriving from the first enclosure, with its Lack of Self-Control and
other evils, and passing them by to enter in to true Culture in the third
enclosure, leaving those learned men behind! So what advantage have they? Are
they not rather worse off — less open to impression, and harder to correct?"

"How so?" I asked.

"Why, because what is known by those in the second enclosure is never put
into practice. As long as they cling to the speculative side of opinion, they
cannot possibly take any practical step toward true Culture. Do you not see
that, just as much as the more practical pilgrim, they have the chance to make
use of the Opinions who lead out of the first enclosure? But are not Opinions
useless without meeting Repentance, and without the resulting conviction that
the culture they possess is a sham and a trap? Being satisfied with where they
live, they never progress to salvation.

"And you also, strangers — you must practice what I said until you have
grasped its meaning. Often indeed will you have to study my instructions
afresh, and never give up the sacred quest, putting all other matters second.
If not, all you hear will remain useless."

## FIFTH: WHAT IS GOOD, AND WHAT IS EVIL?

**36.** "Indeed we shall do so. But explain this to us: how is it that the
things men receive from Luck — namely life, health, wealth, honor, children,
victory, and more such — are not goods? And again, how is it that lacking them
is not an evil? For what you have said seems to us to lack common sense and
reliability."

"Very well, come on!" he said. "Give me your views on what I shall ask you."

"I shall most certainly do so," I said.

"Well, if a man lives badly, is life a good to him?"

"It would seem not," I said. "Rather, an evil."

"How then could life be a good, if to him it is an evil?"

"Well, I should say it was an evil to those who live badly, and a good to
those who live well."

"So then life might be both an evil and a good?"

"So I said."

**37.** "Come, do not hold such unlikely opinions. For how could anything be
at the same time good and evil? Did you ever hear of anything at once useful
and harmful, desired and scorned?"

"That really is unlikely. But if living badly is an evil to the living man,
why — for such a man — is not life itself an evil?"

"Yes — but life and living badly are not the same thing. Or do you think so?"

Neither do I, answered our guide. "The evil lies in living badly, not in life
itself. For if life were an evil, it would be evil even to those who live
righteously, in the degree that they are alive — if it were an absolute evil."

"I agree with you."

**38.** "Since, then, life belongs both to those who live badly and to those
who live rightly, might it not be that life is neither a good nor an evil —
just as cutting and burning are in themselves neither harmful nor healing to
the sick? It all depends on the time and manner in which the patient is cut or
burned. Is it not the same with life?"

"Yes indeed, so it is."

"Now consider it this way: which would you prefer — to live shamefully, or to
die honorably, like a man?"

"I would rather die honorably."

"So then even dying can be no evil, since it is often more desirable than
living?"

"So it is."

"Should we not think the same of health and sickness? For there are times
when health is unbearable."

"You speak the truth," I said.

**39.** "Good! Let us consider wealth in the same way. As is often seen, there
are plainly persons who possess wealth yet live badly and shamefully."

"By Jupiter, there are many such! So then wealth does not help them to live
righteously?"

"Evidently not, for they themselves are evil. Culture, not wealth, gives
virtue."

"Very probably so — at least, according to your argument," I said grudgingly.

"Surely!" he insisted. "How could wealth be an absolute good, since it does
not always improve its owners?"

"Clearly not."

"Admit then that wealth is not at all advisable for those who do not know how
to use it!"

"I must say I think so!"

"Now then, should that whose possession is often unbearable be considered an
absolute good?"

"By no means!"

"Will not a man live well insofar as he knows how to use wealth well and
wisely — and badly if not?"

"What you say seems entirely true."

**40.** "Well, it seems to me that this is the cause of men's restlessness and
harm: they err in honoring things as goods, or scorning things as evils. They
set value on things, and suppose that through things they can improve — or for
the sake of things they commit any, even godless, acts. But this comes from
ignorance of what the real good is. They do not know that no real goods come
from evil means. Hence many are those who have piled up wealth through evil
and shameful deeds — treason, robbery, murder, spying, theft, and other
crimes."

"So it is."

**41.** "If then no good end can come from evil means, as is evident — and if
wealth can come from evil deeds — then can wealth never be an absolute good?"

"An evident consequence!"

"But then, no one can reach righteousness through any evil action — any more
than one can reach injustice or folly through good deeds. Besides, opposites
cannot well live together in one and the same thing. Wealth, fame, victory,
and other such outward goods do not shut out badness. So these things are
neither goods nor evils. They are no more than outward applications of the
inward principle: **wisdom alone is good, while folly is the only evil**."

"It seems to me that you have proved your point."

## WORDS USED IN THIS BOOK

A short list of the book's key terms, in plain English. (Guthrie's glossary
gave the Greek words; the plain meanings below are the draft worker's.)

- **Culture** — the training of character and mind taught in the Greek
  schools. The book distinguishes *Sham Culture* — book-learning, rhetoric,
  mathematics, the subjects where education begins — from *true Culture*, the
  higher training that actually makes a person good.
- **Delusion** — deceit; the power that misleads every human being.
- **Good Genius** — a guiding, guardian spirit who watches over each soul.
- **Happiness** — flourishing; doing and faring well, with a "good spirit."
- **Luck** — chance, fortune — pictured as a blind goddess on a round stone.
  Those she favors call her Good Fortune; those she strips call her Misfortune.
- **Opinion** — belief; what people think. *False Opinions* mislead; *Right
  Opinion* can lead a soul toward Culture; mere *Opinions* can introduce a
  pilgrim to Culture but cannot enter Understanding themselves.
- **Repentance** — remorse, regret; the turning point that can rescue a ruined
  soul.
- **Righteousness** — the just and right way of living.
- **Learning / science** — study, book-knowledge. Useful as travel rations on
  the road, but it cannot by itself make anyone good.
- **Understanding** — pure knowledge; real understanding of what is truly
  beneficial. (Guthrie called this "Scientific-Recognition," from the Greek
  *epistêmê*.)
- **Virtue** — excellence; the highest ideals a person can reach.
