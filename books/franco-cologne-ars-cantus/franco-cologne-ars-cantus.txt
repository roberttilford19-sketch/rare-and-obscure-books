DEDICATION

For Franco of Cologne, who taught the notes to tell time.

Who he was: Franco of Cologne was a teacher of music in the
thirteenth century. That is nearly all that can be said with
certainty. His name is attached to the treatise that follows; the
man behind the name is lost to us. (UNVERIFIED: no reliable
account of his life, birthplace, or death survives; anything more
than the name is guesswork.)

The circumstances of his work: he wrote in the middle or later
years of the 1200s, in a world where singers read rhythm out of
familiar patterns called modes, the way a reader might guess the
sense of a sentence from its shape. The treatise below is usually
dated to about 1250-1280. Copies of it were made again and again
in manuscripts across Europe, which tells us musicians found it
worth the parchment.

Why his work is vital -- proved, not praised: in this treatise
Franco lays down, rule by rule, that each written note carries its
own fixed length of time in its own shape -- the long, the breve,
the semibreve, the ligature, the rest. Before him, how long a note
lasted could only be known from the company it kept; after him, a
singer could read the length of every note by itself. The whole
system of measured writing that served Western music for the next
two hundred years -- the notation from which our own descends --
rests on the distinctions drawn in these chapters. That is not a
compliment; it is what the text itself does, and what the
centuries of copying testify.

His place in this rescue: the Boneyard exists for works that were
never finished, never carried across into English, or left to lie
in Latin where only specialists could find them. Franco's Art of
Measured Song belongs here: the birth certificate of modern
musical time-keeping, still without a full English voice of its
own. This edition gives it one -- plainly, in words a working
singer or a humble reader can follow.

Where a reader goes next: the Latin as Coussemaker printed it,
vol. 1 of his Scriptorum de musica medii aevi (Paris, 1864, pp.
117-136; reprinted Hildesheim: Olms, 1963); the critical edition
by Gilbert Reaney and Andre Gilles, Corpus scriptorum de musica
18 (1974); the searchable Latin text in the Thesaurus Musicarum
Latinarum (Indiana University). For the world around the
treatise, any good history of medieval music will do -- Gustave
Reese's Music in the Middle Ages is the old faithful.

HOW TO READ THIS BOOK

This is a fresh English rendering of Franco's treatise, made
sentence by sentence from the Latin of the 1864 edition -- not a
reworking of anyone else's translation. The chapter headings
follow the Latin chapter titles. Franco's own words are given in
plain English, as clear as I can make them; where a Latin term
carries a meaning English cannot carry in one word, the Latin is
kept in parentheses beside it.

Two things in this book are not Franco's. First, the passages in
square brackets marked [Editor's note -- in plain words] are
mine: short recaps at the end of each chapter, saying again in
today's plain speech what the chapter taught. Skip them if you
like; the treatise reads whole without them. Second, the musical
examples. Franco's pages are full of notated examples -- little
snatches of motets and songs, "In Bethleem," "Virgo viget
melius," "Dari, tradi, capi" -- and this edition describes each
one in words rather than drawing it. A note-shape on a staff can
be misread when it is copied by a hand that does not know the
system; a plain description cannot pretend to be what it is not.

A word on the note-names. Franco names notes the medieval way:
ut, re, mi, fa, sol, la (what we would call do, re, mi, fa, sol,
la), with gamma for the lowest note of all, b quadratum for B
natural and b molle for B flat, and e-la, c-fa, and the like for
notes in particular places of the scale. I have left them as he
wrote them, since to modernize them would be to rewrite his
examples.

A word on the money of the book: the "beat" (tempus) is the
smallest counted unit; the "perfection" (perfectio) is the
three-beat span that is the treatise's unit of account -- named
for the Trinity, because three is the perfect number. A "long"
perfect is three beats; imperfect, two. A breve "right" is one
beat; "altered," two. Everything else -- the plicae, the
ligatures, the rests, the hockets -- is reckoning in this coin.
Keep that table in your head and the whole book opens.

smoothed, filled in, or modernized: where the 1864 page is
unclear, the English says so.


THE ART OF MEASURED SONG
(Ars Cantus Mensurabilis)
by Franco of Cologne

PROLOGUE

Seeing that, as they say, certain learned men have written fully
enough about plain song, and have made it clear to us both in
theory and in practice -- in theory above all Boethius, in
practice the monk Guido, and most of all the blessed Gregory on
the church tunes -- we therefore propose, at the request of
certain great men, to treat of measured music, which plain song
goes before as a principal goes before what serves under it. We
take the plain song itself, as most perfectly set forth by those
learned men named above, without turning the order upside down.

Let no one say we began this work out of pride, or only for our
own profit. We began it for a plain and pressing need: so that
hearers may take it in with the least trouble, and so that all
who write down measured music may be taught it as perfectly as
may be.

For we saw many men, both new and old, saying many good things
about measured music in their books, and yet going wrong and
falling short in many places -- above all in the fine points of
the science. We judged that their teaching ought to be helped
out, lest the science itself come to harm through their mistakes
and shortcomings. So we propose to set forth measured music in
short compass. We will not refuse to bring in the good things
others have said; we will tear up their errors by the roots and
drive them off; and if we ourselves have found anything new, we
will stand it up on good reasons and prove it.

[Editor's note -- in plain words: Franco opens the way a careful
teacher opens. Plain chant -- the old unmeasured church song --
has already been written about well enough, he says, by the great
names: Boethius for the theory, Guido the monk for the practice,
and blessed Gregory for the church tunes themselves. So there is
no need to go over that ground again. Certain great men -- his
patrons, very likely churchmen -- have asked him to write about
the newer thing, measured music, the kind where every note has a
counted length. He insists he is not writing out of pride or for
profit, but because the need is plain: singers were getting the
fine points wrong, and the books they learned from disagreed with
each other. His promise is threefold, and it is the promise of
the whole Boneyard enterprise too: gather up the good things
others have said, root out the errors, and prove anything new on
solid reasons.]

CHAPTER ONE. What measured music is, and its kinds.

Measured music is song measured out in long and short beats. To
understand this definition we must see what measure is, and what
a beat is. Measure is the relation that shows the quantity, the
length and the shortness, of any measurable song. I say
"measurable" because no such measure is looked for in plain song.
A beat is the measure both of a sounded voice and of its
opposite, namely of a lost voice, which is commonly called a rest.
And I say a rest is measured in beats, because otherwise two
different songs, one with rests and one without, could never be
made to come out even with each other in due proportion.

Measured music is divided into the simply measured and the
partly measured. The simply measured is discant, because in every
part of it the beat is measured. The partly measured is called
organum, because it is not measured in every part of it. And note
that organum is taken in two ways, properly and commonly. For
organum properly taken is double organum, which is called pure
organum. Commonly, organum means any measured church song. But
because the simple comes before the compound, we must first speak
of discant.

[Editor's note -- in plain words: Here are the foundations, and
they are simple. Measured music is song in which time is counted:
every note lasts a counted number of beats. A "measure" is just
the relation that tells you how long or short a note is. A "beat"
is the unit you count -- and here is a thought worth stopping
for: a beat measures silence too. A rest is not nothing; it is a
counted nothing, a lost voice with a measured length. Without
that, two singers -- one singing through, one resting -- could
never come out even together. Then the big split: discant is
measured all the way through, every part of it; organum is only
measured in part. And "organum" itself is used two ways: properly
it means the old pure two-voice organum, and commonly it just
means any measured church song. The simple comes before the
compound, so discant first.]

CHAPTER TWO. What discant is, and its divisions.

Discant is the sounding-together of several different songs, in
which those different songs are made even with one another in due
proportion through long, breve, and semibreve voices, and are
shown in writing to be proportioned to one another by their proper
figures.

Discant is divided thus. One discant is sung out plainly; another
is cut short, which is called hocket (ochetus); another is joined
together, which is called copula. Of these we must speak in
order. But because every discant goes by the modes, we must first
treat of the modes, and then of their signs, namely the figures.

[Editor's note -- in plain words: Discant, for Franco, is several
different tunes sounding at once, held together by counted time:
the upper voices move in longs, breves, and semibreves against
the tenor, and the page shows, by the shapes of the notes, how
the voices are proportioned to each other. There are three kinds:
discant sung out plainly; discant cut short -- the hocket, where
voices trade little gasps of sound and silence; and discant
joined together -- the copula, a swift running-together of
notes. Each will get its chapter later. But first, because every
discant moves in rhythmic patterns, he must teach the patterns
(the modes) and then the written signs for them (the figures).]

CHAPTER THREE. The modes of every discant.

A mode is the knowledge of a sound measured out in long and short
beats. But the modes are counted in different ways by different
men. Some put six, others seven. We put only five, because all
the others can be brought back to these five.

The first goes in all longs, and under it we place also the one
that goes long-and-breve, for two reasons: first, because these
two join together in like restings; second, to put down the
quarrel of the old men and some of the new. The second goes
breve-and-long. The third goes long-and-two-breves. The fourth
goes two-breves-and-long. The fifth goes in all breves and
semibreves.

Now, since the voices of these modes are the cause and beginning,
and their notes are their marks, it is plain we must treat of the
notes or figures, which is the same thing. But since discant is
ruled both by sounded voice and by its opposite, that is, by lost
voice, and these are different, they will have different signs.
And since sounded voice comes before lost voice, because having
comes before lacking, we must first speak of the figures that
stand for sounded voice, and then of the rests that stand for
lost voice.

[Editor's note -- in plain words: A "mode" is a rhythmic pattern,
a way of laying long and short beats in order. Before Franco,
singers knew six or even seven such patterns. Franco cuts them
to five, saying all the others can be brought back to these. The
first mode goes in all longs (and he folds the long-and-breve
pattern into it, partly to end an old quarrel among teachers).
The second goes short-long, the third long plus two shorts, the
fourth two shorts plus a long, and the fifth runs in breves and
semibreves. Then a sentence that turns the whole art around: the
voices -- the sung sounds -- are the cause and beginning, and the
written notes are only their marks. So the figures must stand for
the modes, not the modes for the figures. In plain speech: the
music comes first, the writing serves it. And since sounded voice
comes before silence (having comes before lacking), the signs for
sounded notes come before the signs for rests.]

CHAPTER FOUR. The figures, or signs, of measured song.

A figure is the likeness of a voice, set in order in one of the
modes. From this it is plain that figures ought to stand for the
modes, and not the other way round, as some have held. Of figures,
some are simple and some joined together. The joined ones are
ligatures. Of the simple there are three kinds: the long, the
breve, and the semibreve. The first of these is divided into
three: the perfect long, the imperfect long, and the double long.

The perfect long is called first and chief. For in it all the
others are contained, and to it all the others are brought back.
It is called perfect because it is measured by three beats; for
the number three is the most perfect among numbers, because it
takes its name from the highest Trinity, which is true and pure
perfection. Its shape is a square with a stem going down on the
right-hand side, by which it shows its length. (In the book it is
drawn as a square note with a downward tail on the right.)

The imperfect long wears the shape of the perfect, but stands for
only two beats. It is called imperfect because it is never found
without the help of a breve going before or after it. From this
it follows that those are wrong who call it "right" (recta), for
what is right can stand by itself.

The double long is shaped as a doubled figure and stands for two
longs. It is doubled into one body for this reason: so that the
line of the plain chant taken in the tenors shall not be broken
up.

The breve, though it is divided into the right breve and the
altered breve (altera), is still drawn as a square with no stem at
all, for both of them alike. (In the book, a plain square note.)

The semibreve is called now the greater, now the lesser; but each
is drawn in the same way, shaped like a diamond (a lozenge). (In
the book, a diamond-shaped note.)

[Editor's note -- in plain words: Now the alphabet itself. A
figure is the written likeness of a sung voice, set in a rhythmic
pattern. There are simple figures and joined ones (ligatures, for
later). The simples are three: the long, the breve, and the
semibreve. The long is chief and comes in three forms. The
perfect long lasts three beats -- three being the most perfect
number, named for the Trinity -- and is drawn as a square with a
stem falling on the right, the stem showing its length. The
imperfect long wears the same shape but lasts only two beats, and
it is never found alone: it always needs a breve beside it,
before or after. The double long is two longs in one body, so
that the tenor's line of chant is not chopped up. The breve is a
plain square with no stem, and it is either "right" (one beat) or
"altered" (two beats, worth an imperfect long but drawn
differently). The semibreve -- greater and lesser -- is drawn as
a diamond. Learn these shapes and you can read time itself off
the page.]

CHAPTER FIVE. How the figures stand toward one another.

The worth of the simple figures is known from the order they have
toward one another. Take the order thus: either a long is followed
by a long, or by a breve. And mark that the same judgment holds
for breves and semibreves.

If a long is followed by a long, then the first long, sung under
one stress, is measured by three beats, and is called a perfect
long -- whether it be a note or a rest. (The book shows a notated
example on the words "In Bethleem": two longs standing
together.)

If a long is followed by a breve, it goes many ways, for the
breve may be one or many. If it is one alone, then the long is of
two beats and is called imperfect. (The book shows a notated
example on the words "Virgo viget melius": a long followed by a
single breve.)

Unless, between those two -- the long and the breve -- there is
put a certain little stroke, which is called the sign of
perfection, and by another name the division of the mode. Then
the first long is perfect, and the breve makes the following long
imperfect. (The book shows a notated example on the words
"Superans omnia": a long, the little dividing stroke, a breve, and
a second long.)

If the breves are many, it goes many ways: two, or three, or
four, or five, or more than five. If only two, as is plain to see
(The book shows a notated example on the words "Eximie pater et
regie": a long followed by two breves), then the long is perfect,
unless a single breve goes before it, as here (the book shows a
notated example on the words "Ave virgo regia": a single breve
before the long). Of the two breves, the first is called right
(recta), the second the altered breve (altera). A right breve is
one that holds a single beat. An altered breve is like an
imperfect long in worth, but different in meaning: for both are
measured by two beats, but under different shapes. One beat is
called that which is least in the fullness of the voice.

But if between those two breves there is put that stroke which is
called the division of the mode, as here (the book shows a notated
example on the words "Dari, tradi, capi": long, breve, the
dividing stroke, breve, long), then the first long is imperfect,
and so is the second; and each of the breves will be right. This,
however, is very seldom found.

If only three breves are taken between two longs, as appears here
(The book shows a notated example on the words "Cruci corpus
adaptari": a long, three breves, a long), you get the same as
before, except that what was first called the altered breve is
here split into two right breves.

But if the division of the mode is set between the first breve
and the two that follow, as is plain here (the book shows a
notated example on the words "Manus pedes perforari": a long, a
breve, the dividing stroke, two breves, a long), then the first
long is made imperfect by the first breve; of the following
breves, the first becomes right and the last is altered.

And mark that three beats, whether sung under one stress or under
several, make one perfection.

If there are more than three breves, as here (the book shows a
notated example on the words "Dex maudie qui par": a run of more
than three breves), then the first is always imperfect, unless
the sign of perfection is added to it, as appears here (the book
shows a notated example on the words "Gratissimum pro reatu": the
same run with the little stroke of perfection set before it).

Of the breves that follow, each is right, if in the counting they
are found in the threefold number by which perfection is made.
But if only two are left at the end, then the last of them is
called the altered breve, as here (the book shows a notated
example on the words "Aceto felle potari": breves ending in a
pair). If only one is left, it will be right, and it makes the
last long imperfect, as here (the book shows a notated example on
the words "Virginum reorum": a single breve before the closing
long).

Of semibreves and breves the judgment is the same, in the rules
said before. But mark: for one right breve no more than three
semibreves may be taken, as is plain to see (the book shows a
notated example on the words "Mentibus vocibus": three
semibreves standing for a right breve). Each of these is called a
lesser semibreve, because it is the smallest part of the right
breve.

Nor fewer than two: of which the first is called the lesser
semibreve, the second the greater semibreve. The second is called
greater because it holds two lesser ones in itself (the book
shows a notated example on the words "Sanguinem lavat": two
semibreves standing for a right breve).

But if, right after two semibreves standing for a right breve,
three follow, or the other way round (the book shows a notated
example on the words "Quid plura cedit": two semibreves and three
semibreves standing together), then the division of the mode must
be put between the three and the two, or the other way round, as
appears in the example said before.

For an altered breve, fewer than four semibreves may not be taken
(the book shows a notated example on the words "Gaude virgo
mater": four semibreves standing for an altered breve), nor more
than six, as here (the book shows a notated example on the words
"Ave Maria gratia plena": six semibreves standing for an altered
breve), because the altered breve holds two right ones in itself.
From this the error of some is plain, who sometimes put three
semibreves for an altered breve, and sometimes two.

[Editor's note -- in plain words: This is the heart of the book
-- the rules by which a singer reads the length of every note
from the notes around it. Long followed by long: the first is
perfect, three beats ("In Bethleem"). Long followed by one breve:
the long is imperfect, two beats ("Virgo viget melius") -- unless
a little stroke, the sign of perfection or division of the mode,
is set between them; then the first long stays perfect and the
breve shortens the next long instead ("Superans omnia"). Long
with two breves between longs: the long is perfect ("Eximie
pater et regie"); the first breve is right (one beat), the second
altered (two). With the dividing stroke between the two breves,
both longs go imperfect and both breves go right -- but that is
rare ("Dari, tradi, capi"). Three breves between longs: like the
last, but the altered breve splits into two right breves ("Cruci
corpus adaptari"); with the stroke after the first breve, the
first long is imperfected, and of the rest the first is right,
the last altered ("Manus pedes perforari"). Three beats, sung
together or apart, always make one perfection. Longer runs of
breves are read in threes ("Dex maudie qui par", "Gratissimum pro
reatu"), leftovers at the end becoming altered or right breves
("Aceto felle potari", "Virginum reorum"). Semibreves follow the
same rules: at most three for a right breve (each a "lesser"
semibreve), at least two -- first lesser, second greater, the
greater holding two lessers ("Mentibus vocibus", "Sanguinem
lavat"); groups of two and three are divided by the stroke ("Quid
plura cedit"); and for an altered breve no fewer than four and no
more than six ("Gaude virgo mater", "Ave Maria gratia plena").
The whole chapter is one great engine for reading rhythm at
sight.]

CHAPTER SIX. The plicae in simple figures.

Besides these there are certain other simple figures, standing
for the same things as those said before, and called by the same
names with the addition of this word, plica. So we must see what
a plica is. A plica is the mark of the dividing of the same sound
into low and high.

Of plicae, one is long, one is breve, one is semibreve. But of the
semibreve we mean nothing for the present, since no semibreve
plica can be found in simple figures. In ligatures, however, and
in orderings of semibreves, a plica may be taken, as will appear
later.

Again, of plicae one goes up and one goes down. The ascending
long plica is a sort of square figure carrying only a stem going
up on the right-hand side, as is plain to see (the book draws a
square note with a single upward stem on the right); or more
properly two stems, of which the right is longer than the left,
as is plain to see (the book draws a square note with two upward
stems, the right one longer). I say "more properly," because
through those two little stems it earns the name of plica.

The descending long plica likewise has two stems, but going down,
the right, as before, longer than the left, as here (the book
draws a square note with two downward stems, the right longer).
The ascending breve plica is one that has two stems going up, the
left however longer than the right, as here (the book draws a
square note with two upward stems, the left longer). The
descending breve has two stems going down, the left longer, as
here (the book draws a square note with two downward stems, the
left longer).

And mark that these plicae have the same power, and likewise the
same worth in the regular way, as the simple figures said before.


[Editor's note -- in plain words: The plica is a small ornament
mark -- literally a "fold" -- that tells the singer to divide one
note into a low and a high sound, a kind of catch or grace in the
voice. It comes on longs and breves, going up or going down. The
long plica going up is a square with a rising stem on the right
(more properly two stems, the right longer); going down, two
falling stems, right longer. The breve plica going up has two
rising stems with the left longer; going down, two falling stems
with the left longer. In plain speech: it looks like a note with
a little tail or two, and it means "bend this note." Its length
is the same as the plain note it is built on -- a plica-long is
worth a long, a plica-breve a breve.]


CHAPTER SEVEN. Ligatures and their properties.

Having dealt with the simple figures, we must speak of the
joined-together ones, which is the same as ligatures, which are
properly called ligatures.

A ligature is the joining of simple figures, set in order by due
strokes. Of ligatures, one goes up. The ascending one is that
whose second note is higher than the first, as here (the book
shows several ascending ligatures: note-groups climbing upward on
the staff). The descending one is that whose first note is higher
than the second, as here (the book shows several descending
ligatures: note-groups falling downward on the staff).

Again, of ligatures one is with propriety, one without, one with
opposite propriety. And this is on the side of the beginning of
the ligature. But on the side of the end, one is with perfection,
one without. And mark that these differences are essential and
belong to the very kind of the ligatures. So a ligature with
propriety differs in its essence from one without, as a reasoning
animal differs from an unreasoning one; and likewise in the other
differences said before.

The kinds also stand under the genus; but no name has been set on
those kinds -- the differences named, and their genus, go round
about them in words, just as is found in other real genera, as
"living body," which goes round about in words a certain kind on
which no name has been set.

But on the side of the middle of ligatures no essential
difference is found. From this it follows that all the middles of
ligatures agree in what they stand for. By this it is plain that
the position of those men is false who hold that in a three-note
ligature some middle note is a long, but in all others a breve.

So we must see what propriety is, what "without," and what
"opposite" is; what perfection is, what imperfection, and what
all these stand for. Propriety is the mark of the first finding
of the ligature, given by plain song, at its beginning. Perfection
says the same thing, but at the end. From this follow the rules
of the differences said before.

Every descending ligature having a stem going down from the first
note on the left-hand side is said to be with propriety, because
it is so shaped in plain song (the book shows descending
ligatures with a downward stem on the left of the first note).

If it lacks every stem, it will be said to be without propriety,
as is plain to see (the book shows descending ligatures with no
stem at all).

Again, every ascending figure will be said to be with propriety
if it lacks every stem, as here (the book shows ascending
ligatures with no stems). But if it has a stem going down on the
left-hand side of the first note, as appears here (the book shows
ascending ligatures with a downward stem on the left of the first
note), or on the right, which is more proper, as here (the book
shows ascending ligatures with the stem on the right), then it
will lack propriety.

Once more: every ligature, whether ascending or descending,
carrying a stem going up from the first note, will be said to be
with opposite propriety (the book shows ligatures whose first
note bears an upward stem).

On the side of the end, the rules for ligatures are given thus.
Every ligature whose last note stands upright above the next-to-
last is perfect, as appears here (the book shows ligatures ending
with the last note upright above the one before). Or below the
next-to-last, as here (the book shows ligatures ending with the
last note upright below the one before).

But a ligature is made imperfect in two ways. First, if the last
note stands upright with its head turned away, above the next-to-
last, without a plica, as here (the book shows ligatures ending
with the last note's head turned aside). Second, if the last two
notes of the ligature are mixed together in one slanting body,
going up or down, as is plain to see (the book shows ligatures
ending in a slanting two-note body).

Yet such an ascending imperfection is not in use, nor is it
needed, except when a breve must be plicated in a ligature ending
by going up, as will appear later.

Moreover, note that just as through these differences one
ligature differs from another in form, so also in worth. From this
follow the rules of all ligatures.

Every ligature with propriety makes its first note a breve.
Again, every one without makes it a long. Again, every
perfection is a long, and every imperfection a breve. Again,
every opposite propriety makes semibreves of the note it is added
to, and of the one that follows -- not by itself, but by what
follows from it, because no semibreve can be found standing
alone.

Again, every middle note is a breve, unless it is made into
semibreves by opposite propriety, as was said before. Understand
also that in ligatures longs are made perfect in the same way as
was said for the simples; and breves are made right and altered.


[Editor's note -- in plain words: A ligature is several notes
written joined together in one body -- the medieval answer to
beamed notes. It is "ascending" if the second note is higher than
the first, "descending" if the first is higher. Now the fine
points. "Propriety" is the mark at the beginning of the ligature,
inherited from the old plainchant writing: a descending ligature
with a downward stem on the left of its first note has propriety;
one with no stem at all lacks it; an ascending ligature with no
stem has it, with a stem loses it. "Opposite propriety" is an
upward stem on the first note. "Perfection" is the mark at the
end: if the last note stands upright above or below the one
before it, the ending is perfect; if its head is turned away, or
the last two notes melt into one slanting body, it is imperfect.
And here are the reading rules, the payoff of it all: with
propriety, the first note is a breve; without, a long; a perfect
ending is a long, an imperfect ending a breve; opposite propriety
makes semibreves of its note and the next (no semibreve stands
alone); and every middle note is a breve. Franco even swats down
a rival school that wanted middle notes of three-note ligatures
to be longs: the middle, he says, has no essential difference
anywhere -- all middles are breves.]


CHAPTER EIGHT. The plicae in ligated figures.

Moreover, note that every ligature, whether perfect or imperfect,
can be plicated, and this on the side of the end. What a plica
is was said before, where we spoke of the simple figures. For a
perfect ligature is plicated in two ways, going up and going down.
Going up thus (the book shows perfect ligatures with the plica
stems rising at the end); going down thus (the book shows perfect
ligatures with the plica stems falling at the end).

An imperfect ligature is likewise plicated in two ways. And mark
that the imperfect is always plicated in the slanting
imperfection, going up or down, as is plain to see (the book
shows imperfect ligatures plicated in the slanting ending). And
in such a case, where the imperfect must be plicated, the
slanting must be made going up, so that the last note is made a
breve. For if a straight imperfection were plicated, it would be
made perfect by the plica, because it would share in the nature of
the perfect. Yet a slanting ascending imperfection must not be
put without a plica; for the setting of a straight imperfection
is enough wherever there is no plica, and it is more proper and
more in use. Thus the plicae of all ligatures are made plain.

There are also certain joinings of simples and ligatures which
partly share in ligatures and partly in simple figures, which can
be called neither ligatures nor simple figures, as is plain to
see (the book shows mixed figures, part ligature and part simple
note). Of the worth of such joined figures no other rules can be
given than those already given for simples and ligatures.
Besides, there are certain other orderings of simple figures and
ligatures, filling the lack of those on which no name has been
set, which are known only by the rules of the simples, as is
plain to see.

[Editor's note -- in plain words: Every ligature, perfect or
imperfect, can take a plica at its end -- the bending mark --
going up or going down. An imperfect ligature is always plicated
in the slanting kind of imperfection, never the straight kind;
and where the imperfect must be plicated, the slant must go up,
so the last note comes out a breve (a straight imperfection with
a plica would turn perfect, which would be wrong). But never put
a slanting ascending imperfection without a plica: where there is
no plica, the plain straight imperfection is enough, and it is
the more proper and usual way. Then Franco tidies up the
leftovers: there are mixed figures, part simple note and part
ligature, that are neither one nor the other, and stray orderings
of notes that fill the gaps where no name was ever set -- and for
all of them, the rules of the simple figures are judge enough.]

CHAPTER NINE. The rests, and how the modes change into one another
through them.

Having spoken of the signs that stand for sounded voice, we must
see the rests, which stand for lost voice. A rest is the leaving
out of sounded voice, in the due quantity of some mode. Of rests
there are six kinds: the perfect long, the imperfect long -- under
which the altered breve is gathered, because they take in the same
measure -- the right breve, the greater semibreve, the lesser
semibreve, and the end of notes (finis punctorum).

The resting of the perfect long is the leaving out of a perfect
sounded voice, taking in three beats. The imperfect likewise of
an imperfect voice, and also of the altered breve, measured by
only two beats. The breve is the leaving out of a right breve,
holding in itself a single beat. The greater semibreve leaves out
two parts of a right breve. The lesser, only a third. The end of
notes is called unmeasured; for it is found even in plain song.
It marks only that the next-to-last note is a long, in whatever
mode it comes, even if that next-to-last, by the nature of the
mode it is in, would be a breve.

Moreover, these six rests are marked out by seven fine strokes,
which are also called rests. Of these, the first, which is called
perfect, touching four lines, takes in three spaces, because it is
measured by three beats. The imperfect, taking hold of three
lines, by the same reckoning covers two spaces. The breve, one.
The greater semibreve, two parts of one space; but the lesser
covers only one. The end of notes, touching all the lines, takes
in four spaces. The shapes of all these are plain in the example
here set (the book shows the seven rest-signs drawn on the staff,
from the tallest to the shortest).

And mark that rests have a wonderful power; for through them the
modes are changed into one another. So the proper rest of the
first mode is the right breve or the perfect long; the rest of
the second mode is the imperfect long; the rests of the third and
fourth are properly perfect longs, but improperly the right breve
and the altered; the fifth properly deserves to have the breve
rest or the semibreve.

So if the first mode, which goes long-and-breve-and-long, has
after the breve an imperfect long rest, as is plain here (the
book shows a notated example on the words "Maris stella fervens":
the first mode with an imperfect-long rest after its breve), the
first mode is changed into the second.

But if the second, after the long note, takes a breve rest, as is
plain here (the book shows a notated example on the words "O
Maria mater dei flos": the second mode with a breve rest after
its long), it is changed into the first.

The fifth mode, when it is taken in some discant with the first,
is ruled by the rests of the first, and makes the long and the
rest into a note, as here (the book shows a notated example on
the words "O Maria virgo davitica": the fifth mode resting by the
first mode's rule). But if with the second, it has the rests of
the second, and before the rest at the end it takes a breve, as
here (the book shows a notated example on the words "Povre secors
ai encore recouvre": the fifth mode resting by the second mode's
rule). But if neither so nor so, then it is ruled by its own
rests, as here (the book shows a notated example on the words
"Respondi que ne lerroit": the fifth mode with its own proper
rests).

And mark that in one single discant all the modes can run
together, because through perfections all modes are brought back
to one. Nor should any fuss be made over such a discant, as to
which mode it is to be judged by. It may be called by that mode in
which it stays most or oftenest. And see that the fifth mode can
above all be taken together with all the others.

And let this be enough for the present on rests, and on the
changing of the modes.


[Editor's note -- in plain words: Rests are the counted silences,
and there are six kinds: the perfect long (three beats), the
imperfect long (two beats, and the altered breve goes with it),
the right breve (one beat), the greater semibreve (two thirds of
a breve), the lesser semibreve (one third), and the end-of-notes
mark, which is unmeasured -- it only says "the note before me is
a long, whatever the pattern says." On the staff they are drawn
as fine strokes of different heights: the perfect touches four
lines and covers three spaces, the imperfect three lines and two
spaces, and so down to the end-of-notes, which spans the whole
staff. And rests have a wonderful power: they change the mode.
Put an imperfect-long rest after the breve of the first mode
("Maris stella fervens") and you have turned it into the second;
put a breve rest after the long of the second ("O Maria mater
dei flos") and it turns back into the first. The fifth mode
borrows the rests of whichever mode it runs with ("O Maria virgo
davitica", "Povre secors ai encore recouvre"), or uses its own
("Respondi que ne lerroit"). Best of all: all the modes can run
together in one piece of music, since every perfection brings
them back to one -- so do not fuss over which mode a piece is
"in"; call it by the one it lingers in most.]


CHAPTER TEN. How many figures can be ligated together.

Item, note that a figure that can be ligated but is not ligated
is faulty -- but more faulty still is one ligated that cannot be
ligated. So mark that several longs cannot be ligated to one
another, except in the two-note ligature that is without propriety
and with perfection. Nor even in such a place are they faulty if
they are not ligated, because a long is nowhere found ligatable
with a long.

From this it follows that those err greatly who ligate three
longs together on any occasion, as in tenors. Likewise those who
ligate a long between two breves, since by the setting of middles,
as was seen before, all middles are made breves.

Item, no more than two semibreves can be ligated together, and it
is taken at the beginning of the ligature; and understand this of
a proper ligature.

But breves are ligatable at the beginning, in the middle, and at
the end. From this it is plain that any mode taken without text
is ligatable, except the one that goes in all longs. So the
first, which goes long-and-breve, first ligates three without
propriety and with perfection; then two with propriety and
perfection, and two and two as much as you like, so that it ends
upon such twos, as is plain here (the book shows the first mode
ligated: a three-note group without propriety with perfection,
then two-note groups with propriety and perfection). Unless the
mode said before is changed, as is plain here (the book shows the
same with the mode changed midway).

But the second takes a two-note ligature with propriety and
perfection, with two and two, and so on, of the same kinds, one
single breve of them remaining alone, as appears here (the book
shows the second mode ligated in two-note groups, a single breve
left over). Unless the mode is changed, as appears here (the book
shows the second mode with the mode changed midway).

The third takes a four-note group without propriety and with
perfection; with threes with propriety and perfection, and three
and three, and so on, as appears here (the book shows the third
mode ligated in fours and threes). Unless the mode is changed, as
here (the book shows the third mode with the mode changed).

The fourth first ligates threes with propriety and perfection,
with three and three, and so on, of the same kinds, and at the
end with twos with propriety and without perfection, as here (the
book shows the fourth mode ligated in threes, ending in twos with
propriety without perfection). Unless the mode is changed, as
here (the book shows the fourth mode with the mode changed).

The fifth ought to be ligated as much as it can, and it ends in
breves or semibreves, as here (the book shows the fifth mode
ligated in long runs ending on breves or semibreves). Unless the
mode is changed, as here (the book shows the fifth mode with the

And mark that the modes can be changed in many ways, as was said
before in the chapter on rests.


[Editor's note -- in plain words: Not everything may be tied
together, and a figure left untied that ought to be tied is a
fault -- though tying one that cannot be tied is worse. Longs
cannot be ligated with longs at all, except in two-note groups
without propriety and with perfection; and even there they are
not faulty left alone. So those who tie three longs together in
tenors are badly wrong, and so are those who trap a long between
two breves (all middles are breves, as we learned). Semibreves
may be tied at most two together, and only at the start of the
ligature. Breves may be tied at the start, middle, or end. From
this it follows that every mode sung without words can be
ligated, except the all-long mode. Then Franco works through all
five modes, showing the proper tying for each -- the first in
threes without propriety with perfection, then in twos; the
second in twos with propriety and perfection; the third in fours
and threes; the fourth in threes ending in twos; the fifth tied
as far as it will go, ending on breves or semibreves -- each with
its "unless the mode is changed" variant. It is a copyist's
handbook as much as a singer's.]

CHAPTER ELEVEN. Discant and its kinds.

Having seen the figures and the rests, we must speak of discant,
how it ought to be made, and of its kinds. But because every
discant is ruled by consonances, we must see what consonances
are, and dissonances made in the same beat and in different
voices.

Concordance is said to be when two voices, or more, sung out in
one beat, can agree with each other to the ear. Discordance, on
the other hand, is said to be when two voices are so joined that
they disagree to the ear.

Of concordances there are three kinds: perfect, imperfect, and
middle. Perfect concordances are said to be when several voices
are joined so that one can hardly be told from another for the
agreeing. And such are two: the unison and the octave (the book
shows a notated example of the unison and the octave).

Imperfect are said to be when two voices are heard to differ much
from each other, yet do not disagree. And they are two: the major
third (ditonus) and the minor third (semiditonus) (the book shows
a notated example of the two thirds).

Middle concordances are said to be when two voices are joined
having a better concordance than those said before, yet not as
perfect; and they are the fifth (diapente) and the fourth
(diatessaron), as is plain here (the book shows a notated example
of the fifth and the fourth).

Why one concordance concords more than another, leave to plain
music (that is a question for another book, not this one).

Of discordances there are two kinds, perfect and imperfect.
Perfect discordance is said to be when two voices are so joined
that they cannot agree with each other to the ear. And they are
four: the semitone, the tritone, the major sixth (ditonus with
diapente), and the minor seventh (semitonium with diapente), as
appears here (the book shows a notated example of the four).

Imperfect discordances are said to be when two voices can in some
way agree with each other to the ear, but disagree; and they are
of three kinds: the tone, the tone with the octave, and the minor
sixth (semiditonus with diapente) (the book shows a notated
example of the three).

And mark that both concordances and discordances can be taken
without end, as the fifth with the octave, the fourth with the
octave, as here (the book shows a notated example of compounded
intervals); and so in the double octave, and the triple, if it
were possible in the voice. Item, note that every imperfect
dissonance concords well set right before a concordance.

Discant is made either with text or without; and with text, in
two ways: with the same text, or with different texts. With the
same text discant is made in songs (cantilenae), round-songs
(rondelli), and some church song. With different texts discant is
made, as in motets which have a triplum or a tenor, because the
tenor counts as a text. With text and without, discant is made in
conductus, and in some church discant which is improperly called
organum.

And mark that in all these the way of working is the same, except
in conductus. For in all the others, first some song already made
is taken, which is called the tenor, because it holds the discant
(tenet discantum), and the discant takes its rise from it. But in
conductus it is not so; rather, the song and the discant are made
together, by the same maker.

Now discant is called double: first it is called discant because
it is the song of different ones (diversorum cantus); second it
is called discant as if taken from discant (de discantu sumptus).

The course of working in these is such: either the discant
begins in unison with the tenor, as appears here (the book shows
a notated example on the words "Virgo Dei plena" in the upper
voice and "Amoris" in the tenor: the two voices starting
together on one note); or in the octave, as here (the book shows
a notated example on the words "Arida frondescit" over "Johanne":
the voices starting an octave apart); or in the fifth (the book
shows a notated example on the words "O Maria mater Dei" over
"Flos filius": the voices starting a fifth apart); or in the
fourth (the book shows a notated example on the words "Recordare
virgo mater" over "Portare": the voices starting a fourth apart);
or in the major third, as here (the book shows a notated example
on the words "Mulier omnis peccati" over "Omnes": the voices
starting a major third apart); or in the minor third, as here
(the book shows a notated example on the words "Virgo viget
melius" over "Flos filius": the voices starting a minor third
apart).

Then, going on through the consonances, mixing in dissonances now
and then in their due places, so that when the tenor goes up the
discant goes down, or the other way round. And note that tenor
and discant, for the beauty of the song, sometimes go up and down
together, as is plain here (the book shows a notated example on
the words "Ave plena gratia" over "Johanne": the voices moving
in the same direction).

Item, understand that in all modes consonances must always be
used at the beginning of a perfection, whether it be a long, a
breve, or a semibreve.

Item, in conductus the working is otherwise. For he who would
make a conductus must first find as beautiful a song as he can;
then he must use it as a tenor, making discant over it, as was
said before.

But he who would work a triplum must look to the tenor and the
discant, so that if it discords with the tenor, it does not
discord with the discant, or the other way round; and let him go
on further through the concordances, now going up with the tenor
or down, now with the discant, so that he is not always with one
only, as appears in the example that follows (the book shows a
notated three-voice example on the word "Dulcia").

But he who would make a quadruplum or a quintuplum must take or
look to the parts already made, so that if he discords with one,
he is held in concordances with the others; nor ought he always
to go up or down with one of them only, but now with the tenor,
now with the discant, and so on.

Mark that both in discant and in triplums and the rest, the
evenness must be watched in the perfections of longs, breves, and
semibreves, so that as many perfections are had in the tenor as
in the discant or in the triplum and the rest, or the other way
round; counting both sounded and omitted voices, right up to the
next-to-last, where no such measure is watched -- there the point
is rather of the organum kind. And let this be enough for the
present on discant sung out plainly.


[Editor's note -- in plain words: Now the music itself. A
concordance is when two or more voices sung together agree to the
ear; a discordance when they do not. Concordances come in three
ranks. Perfect: the unison and the octave, where the voices melt
together so you can hardly tell them apart. Imperfect: the major
and minor thirds, where the voices differ plainly but do not
clash. Middle: the fifth and the fourth, better than the thirds
but short of perfect. (Why one concords better than another,
Franco leaves to the plainchant books.) Discordances are of two
ranks. Perfect discordances -- the semitone, the tritone, the
major sixth, the minor seventh -- cannot stand together to the
ear at all. Imperfect discordances -- the tone, the tone with the
octave, the minor sixth -- can stand together after a fashion,
and every one of them sits well right before a concordance (the
first written rule of what we would call a leading note).
Discant may be with words or without; with the same words in all
voices (songs, rounds, some church pieces) or different words
(motets, where the tenor counts as one of the texts); and the
conductus, where all voices may have words or not, is made
differently -- its tune and its discant are invented together,
while every other kind starts from a ready-made tenor, the old
chant that "holds" the new voices. The working rule: begin on a
good concordance -- unison, octave, fifth, fourth, major or minor
third (Franco gives a worked example for each: "Virgo Dei
plena/Amoris", "Arida frondescit/Johanne", "O Maria mater
Dei/Flos filius", "Recordare virgo mater/Portare", "Mulier omnis
peccati/Omnes", "Virgo viget melius/Flos filius") -- then move
through concordances, letting the voices go against each other
(tenor up, discant down, or the reverse), though they may move
together for beauty's sake ("Ave plena gratia/Johanne"), and
always landing a concordance on the first beat of every
perfection. For three voices, the triplum must agree with at
least one of the lower voices at every point ("Dulcia"); for four
or five, the same watchfulness, never glued to one voice alone.
And through it all, the perfections must come out even: as many
in the tenor as in the upper voices, counting sounded notes and
rests alike, right up to the next-to-last note, where the
measuring stops and the old organum manner takes over.]

CHAPTER TWELVE. The copula.

The copula is a swift discant joined to itself. One copula is
ligated, another not ligated. The ligated copula is one that
begins with a simple long and goes on through a two-note ligature
with propriety and perfection, after the likeness of the second
mode; yet it differs from the second mode itself, namely in the
noting and in the singing. In the noting, because the second mode
has no simple long at the beginning, but the copula has, as is
plain here (the book shows a notated example: a simple long
followed by two-note ligatures with propriety and perfection).

But if between the first simple note and the ligature the
division of the mode is set, then it is not a copula, but is
called of the second mode, as appears in the example that follows
(the book shows a notated example: the same figure with the
little dividing stroke set between).

In the singing also the copula differs from the second mode: the
second is sung as a right breve and an imperfect long; but this
copula will be sung swiftly, as if semibreve and breve, right to
the end.

The unligated copula is made after the likeness of the fifth
mode. Yet it differs from the fifth in two ways, in the noting
and in the singing. In the noting it differs from the fifth,
because the fifth, wherever it is without text, is ligatable, but
this copula is never taken over text, and yet is not ligated, as
appears here (the book shows a notated example on the word
"Amen": swift unligated notes with no text beneath them). In the
singing it differs also from the fifth, because the fifth is sung
from right breves, but the copula is joined by being sung more
swiftly. And let this be enough on the copula.


[Editor's note -- in plain words: The copula is the quick,
showy kind of discant -- notes joined together and run off
swiftly. The ligated copula starts with a plain long and runs on
in two-note ligatures with propriety and perfection, looking just
like the second mode; but it is not the second mode. In the
writing it differs because the second mode never starts with a
plain long and the copula does; in the singing it differs because
the second mode goes "short-long" while the copula flies by as
"semibreve-breve" to the end. (And if you set the little dividing
stroke between the opening long and the ligature, it stops being
a copula and becomes the second mode outright.) The unligated
copula looks like the fifth mode but differs in the same two
ways: the fifth can be ligated anywhere when there are no words,
while the copula is never sung to words and yet is never ligated
("Amen"); and it is sung faster than the fifth's plain breves.
In short: like the modes, but quicker and freer -- the virtuoso
corner of Franco's art.]


CHAPTER THIRTEEN. Hockets.

Cutting-short (truncatio) is song sung out cut short in sounded
and omitted voices; and note that cutting-short can be made in as
many ways as a long, a breve, or a semibreve can be divided. A
long is divisible in many ways: first into long-and-breve, and
breve-and-long; and from this is made the cutting-short, or
hocket (ochetus), which is the same thing -- so that in one voice
the breve is left out, in the other the long, as appears here
(the book shows a notated example on the words "In seculum": two
voices trading sounded and silent notes).

So also it can be divided into three breves, or two, and into
many semibreves. And from all these the cut-short song is sung
through sounded and omitted voices, so that when one rests the
other does not rest, or the other way round. But a breve is
divisible into three semibreves or two; and from this the hocket
song is sung, leaving out one semibreve in one voice and singing
another, as is plain here (the book shows a notated example of
breves split into semibreves, the voices taking turns).

And mark that from the cuttings-short named before, the common
hockets are born, through the leaving out of longs and breves,
and also through the singing. And mark that in all these the
evenness must be watched in the beats, and the concordance in the
sounded voices. Item, second: that every cutting-short must be
founded upon a song already made, whether it be common or Latin.
And let this be enough on hockets.

Organum properly taken is song not measured in every part of it.
Note that pure organum cannot be had except over a tenor where
the note alone is in unison -- so that when the tenor takes
several notes together, at once it is discant, as here (the book
shows a notated example of pure organum over a single-note
tenor).

The longs and breves of this organum are known by three rules.
The first is: whatever is noted in a simple long note is long,
and in a breve, breve, and in a semibreve, semibreve. The second
rule is: whatever is long needs concordance with the tenor; but
if dissonance comes, let the tenor be silent, or feign itself
into concordance, as is plain here (the book shows a notated
example of the tenor falling silent or adjusting under a
dissonance). The third rule is: whatever is taken right before
the rest that is called the end of notes is long, because every
next-to-last is long.

So mark that whenever in pure organum several figures come
together in unison, only the first ought to be struck; but all
the rest are to be held in the ornamenting (floratura), as here
(the book shows a notated example: several notes in unison, the
first struck and the rest held as ornament).

And let what has been said here be enough on discant and its
kinds, and also on the figures themselves -- that is, on the
figures and the rests -- and on organum itself.


[Editor's note -- in plain words: The hocket -- the "cutting
short" -- is music made of interruptions: one voice sings while
the other is silent, then they trade, like two people finishing
each other's sentences. It is built by dividing the note-values:
a long split into long-and-breve or breve-and-long gives the
hocket where one voice drops the breve and the other drops the
long ("In seculum"); a long can also split into two or three
breves or a shower of semibreves, a breve into two or three
semibreves. Every hocket must be founded on a tune already made,
whether a common street tune or a Latin one -- and the beats must
come out even and the sounded notes must agree. Then Franco turns
to pure organum, the old unmeasured art: it can only stand over a
tenor holding one single note in unison; the moment the tenor
takes several notes together, you are in discant. Its note-values
are read by three plain rules: what is written long is long,
breve breve, semibreve semibreve; every long note must agree
with the tenor, and if a clash comes the tenor falls silent or
bends itself into agreement; and whatever stands just before the
end-of-notes mark is long, for the next-to-last is always long.
And when several notes pile up in unison, strike only the first
and let the rest pour out as ornament. With that, the whole
treatise is done.]


[Here the edition adds, in brackets, a summary of the intervals,
gathered from the writings of Petrus Picardus, as follows:]

Unison is when several voices are taken together as one -- as
when the same note-name is spoken, as in gamma, and re, re, re on
a, and mi, mi, mi on b. (The passage is unclear in the edition;
the note-names are given as printed.)

The tone is the distance between two voices following right after
each other, except for the semitone, as mi, fa, where the
semitone remains; for the semitone is a small raising or
lowering, and if it were made smaller, it would not be perceived.

The major third (ditonus) is between ut and mi, fa and la, in
whatever place they are found, and is called ditonus as having
two tones.

The minor third (semiditonus) is the distance between re and fa,
mi and sol -- and mark this is in the same ordering of voices --
and is called from semis (half) and tonos (tone), that is, as it
were a tone and a half.

The fourth (diatessaron) is the distance between ut and fa, re
and sol, mi and la, and holds two tones with a semitone; it is
called from dya, which is "of," and tessaron, which is "four," as
of four voices.

The fifth (diapente) is the distance between ut and sol, re and
la, mi and mi, fa and fa, through the disjoining of voices, and
holds three tones with a semitone; it is called from dya, which
is "of," and pente, which is "five," as of five voices.

The tritone is the distance between f and b natural (b quadratum),
and between mi and fa through the disjoining of voices, and holds
three whole tones or their equivalent.

The tone with the octave is the distance between ut and fa, and
holds four tones with a semitone.

The semitone with the octave is made between a and f, d and b
flat (b molle), re and fa through the disjoining of voices, and
holds four tones.

The minor third with the octave is made between gamma and f, a
and g, b natural and a, and holds five tones.

The major third with the octave is made between c and b natural,
f and e-la, g and c-fa, and holds five tones with a semitone.

The octave (diapason) is made between all like letters far apart,
as between A and a, B and b, and so on. And it holds the fifth
and the fourth, or six tones, which is the same.

Of these consonances, some are concordances and some
discordances. Of the concordances, some are perfect, as the
unison, which is made on one letter, and the octave; some
imperfect, as the minor third and the major third; some middle,
as the fifth and the fourth.

Of all these concordances, the first concords better than the
second: as the unison better than the octave, and the minor third
better than the major third, and the fifth better than the
fourth.

Item, a perfect concordance concords better than an imperfect;
a middle concords better than an imperfect concordance.

All the other consonances are called discordances. Of these
discordances some are perfect, some imperfect. The perfect
discordances may not be taken in any discant; and they are four:
the semitone, the tritone, the major sixth (ditonus with
diapente), and the minor seventh (semitonium with diapente).

The imperfect ones may be taken in some discant, and this right
before a perfect concordance coming immediately after; and they
are three: the tone with the octave, and the minor sixth
(semiditonus with diapente). (The edition promises three but
prints two; the third is not given in Coussemaker's text.)

Which things, as if summing up but shortening them, Petrus
Picardus writing says.

[Editor's note -- in plain words: This last table is not Franco's
own writing but a summary Coussemaker prints in brackets, drawn
from Petrus Picardus -- a kind of appendix defining every
interval by its note-names and its size in tones. The unison is
voices taken as one; the tone is the step between neighboring
notes (the semitone, mi to fa, being the smallest thing the ear
can catch); the major third spans ut-mi or fa-la, the minor third
re-fa or mi-sol; the fourth spans ut-fa, the fifth ut-sol, the
tritone f to b natural; then the compounded intervals -- ninths,
tenths, and so on -- up to the octave, which spans the fifth plus
the fourth, six tones. The concordances are ranked: unison above
octave, minor third above major third, fifth above fourth, and
perfect above middle above imperfect. The perfect discordances
(semitone, tritone, major sixth, minor seventh) are barred from
any discant; the imperfect ones may be used, but only leaning into
a perfect concordance right after. It is the whole harmony of the
treatise in one page.]

AFTERWORD: THE BOOK THAT TAUGHT THE NOTES TO TELL TIME

What you have just read is the working manual of a revolution
that made no noise. Before this treatise, a singer reading a
page of measured music could not know how long a note lasted by
looking at the note alone; the length had to be guessed from the
pattern around it, the way you guess a word from the sentence.
Franco's book changes that, rule by rule and example by example,
until every shape on the page -- the long with its stem, the
square breve, the diamond semibreve, the joined ligature, the
little stroke of division, the tall rest -- tells the singer its
own length on its own authority. That is the whole of it, and it
is everything: time made visible, so that two or three or five
voices, each singing a different tune, can move together and come
out even at the end.

The engine of the book is the doctrine of perfection and
imperfection. A long is perfect -- three beats -- when the notes
around it let it be; a breve beside it cuts it to two. The
little dividing stroke saves a perfection here and spends one
there. It reads like bookkeeping, and it is: the most beautiful
bookkeeping ever devised, because what is being kept is time
itself, divided fairly among voices.

The book then climbs, chapter by chapter, from the alphabet to
the art. The plica bends a note into low and high. The ligature
joins notes into one body, with propriety at its head inherited
from the old chant writing and perfection at its tail, and every
middle note a breve -- Franco is fierce on that point, and names
his opponents wrong by name. The rests are counted silences, and
through them the rhythmic modes change into one another, so that
a piece may wander through all five modes and still be one piece.
The tying-rules tell the copyist what may be joined and what may
not. Then the great eleventh chapter teaches the making of
discant itself: what agrees to the ear and what does not, the
three ranks of concordance and the two of discordance, the tenor
taken ready-made, the conductus invented whole, the voices
beginning on unison, octave, fifth, fourth, or third, moving
against each other and landing together on every perfection, the
triplum watching both lower voices, the perfections kept even
from the first note to the next-to-last. The copula runs the
same ground swiftly; the hocket chops it into trading silences;
and pure organum, the old art, keeps its three plain rules under
the new reign.

The treatise was copied and recopied across Europe -- Paris,
Oxford, and beyond -- which is how we know it was used, not just
admired. The Latin you have read in English here is the text as
Edmond de Coussemaker printed it in 1864, from the manuscripts
he could lay hands on; the Thesaurus Musicarum Latinarum keeps
it searchable to this day, alongside transcriptions from the
manuscripts themselves. Read against those manuscripts, the
printed text shows the usual small scars of transmission -- a
promised third example that never comes, a passage on the unison
that the copyists themselves seem to have puzzled over -- and
this edition has marked them where they fall rather than smoothing
them away.

One caution, in the spirit of the house: this afterword praises
nothing. That the book did what it claims -- that singers learned
to read measured time from its pages for two hundred years -- is
proved by the centuries of copying and by the notation that
descends from it to our own staves. What cannot be proved is not
claimed. The man Franco remains a name on a title page; the book
is the man, and the book is here.

GLOSSARY OF THE BOOK'S TERMS

Beat (tempus): the smallest counted unit of time; what is least
in the fullness of the voice.

Perfection (perfectio): the three-beat span; the treatise's unit
of account. Imperfection is the two-beat span.

Long (longa): the chief note, square with a stem; perfect (three
beats) or imperfect (two). Double long: two longs in one body.

Breve (brevis): the short note, a plain square; right (one beat)
or altered (two beats).

Semibreve (semibrevis): the half-breve, drawn as a diamond;
greater (two thirds of a breve) or lesser (one third).

Plica: the "fold"; a mark dividing one note into a low and a high
sound, a kind of grace or bend.

Figure (figura): the written sign of a sung voice in a mode.

Ligature (ligatura): notes joined together in one body.
Propriety: the mark of the old chant-writing at its beginning.
Opposite propriety: the mark that turns notes into semibreves.

Mode (modus): a rhythmic pattern of longs and shorts; five in
Franco's counting.

Rest (pausa): a counted silence; a lost voice with a measured
length.

Discant (discantus): several different tunes sounding together in
counted time. Sung plainly, cut short (hocket), or joined
(copula).

Hocket (ochetus, truncatio): discant cut into trading bits of
sound and silence.

Copula: swift discant joined together.

Organum: song measured only in part; properly, the old pure
two-voice art over a single sustained note.

Tenor: the ready-made tune that holds the discant; the new
voices rise from it.

Triplum, quadruplum: the third, fourth voices above tenor and
discant.

Concordance: voices agreeing to the ear -- perfect (unison,
octave), imperfect (thirds), middle (fifth, fourth).
Discordance: voices disagreeing -- perfect (never to be used) or
imperfect (good before a concordance).
