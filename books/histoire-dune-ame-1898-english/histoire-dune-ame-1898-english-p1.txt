THE STORY OF A SOUL, AS THE WORLD FIRST RECEIVED IT (1898) — SŒUR THÉRÈSE DE L'ENFANT-JÉSUS ET DE LA SAINTE FACE (MARIE-FRANÇOISE-THÉRÈSE MARTIN, 1873–1897)

The Story of a Soul, as the world first received it, 1898. A fresh English translation of the French first edition of Histoire d'une âme, made from the French alone, without consultation of any later English version.

Written at the command of her sister and prioress, Mother Agnes of Jesus (Pauline Martin), who gathered, joined, and saw these notebooks into print.

Translator's note on the basis. This translation is made afresh from the French first edition of 1898 (Histoire d'une âme, écrite par elle-même, Bar-le-Duc, Imprimerie Saint-Paul, 1898). That first impression could not be lawfully fetched as a scan from this environment; the French text translated here is therefore that of the 1912 Lisieux printing preserved by Project Gutenberg (No. 36708), an impression carrying unchanged the 1912 wording of the 1898 composite, printed before the Carmel's 1914 revision. It is the pre-critical-edition Pauline text — the shape in which the world first read this book. The editor's own weaving of Thérèse's manuscripts is part of the book the world received; where she inserts notes or poses, they are printed here as hers, flagged as editorial. The footnotes of these chapters follow the 1898 book.

A matter of honesty. The 1898 book, in its twelve chapters, presented the notebooks as written at Mother Marie de Gonzague's command; the world now knows they were written at the command of, and delivered to, her sister Pauline — Mother Agnes of Jesus. This translation restores her by name on the title page, where she belongs, as editor and compiler of the book the world first received.



Chapter I

The first notes of a canticle of love. A mother's heart. Memories from two to four years of age.

It is to you, my revered Mother, that I come to confide the story of my soul. The day you asked me for it, it seemed to me that doing so would scatter my heart abroad; but since then, Jesus has made me feel that in simply obeying I would be pleasing to him. So I will begin to sing what I must repeat for all eternity: the mercies of the Lord!...

Before taking up my pen, I knelt before the statue of Mary — she who has given my family so many proofs of the motherly favor of the Queen of Heaven — and I begged her to guide my hand, so that I would not trace a single line that would not be pleasing to her. [Editorial note by Mother Agnes, printed in the 1898 book: "This precious Virgin, though of no artistic value, twice came to life, as it were, to enlighten and console, in grave circumstances, the mother of our angelic child. She herself received signal graces from this blessed statue, as we shall see later."] Then, opening the holy Gospel, my eyes fell on these words: "Jesus, having gone up onto a mountain, called to him those whom he pleased." That indeed is the mystery of my vocation, of my whole life, and above all the mystery of Jesus's privileges over my soul. He does not call those who are worthy of it, but those whom he pleases. As Saint Paul says: "God has pity on whom he wills, and he shows mercy to whom he wills to show mercy. It is not, then, the work of the one who wills, nor of the one who runs, but of God who shows mercy."

For a long time I asked myself why the good God has preferences, why all souls do not receive an equal measure of graces. I marveled to see him lavish extraordinary favors on great sinners like Saint Paul, Saint Augustine, Saint Mary Magdalene, and so many others whom he forced, so to speak, to receive his graces. I marveled again, reading the lives of the saints, to see Our Lord caress certain privileged souls from the cradle to the grave, leaving no obstacle on their path that might keep them from rising toward him, never allowing sin to tarnish the immaculate brightness of their baptismal robe. I asked myself why the poor savages, for example, died in great numbers without even having heard the name of God pronounced.

Jesus deigned to teach me this mystery. He set before my eyes the book of nature, and I understood that all the flowers he has created are beautiful, that the splendor of the rose and the whiteness of the lily take nothing from the perfume of the little violet, nothing from the ravishing simplicity of the daisy. I understood that if all the little flowers wanted to be roses, nature would lose her springtime adornment, and the fields would no longer be spangled with little blossoms.

So it is in the world of souls, that living garden of the Lord. He saw fit to create the great saints, who can be compared to lilies and roses; but he created smaller ones too, and these must be content to be daisies or simple violets, destined to gladden his divine eyes when he lowers them to his feet. The more happily the flowers do his will, the more perfect they are.

I understood something else as well... I understood that Our Lord's love is revealed just as much in the simplest soul, which resists his graces in nothing, as in the most sublime soul. Indeed, since it is the nature of love to lower itself, if all souls resembled those of the holy Doctors who have illumined the Church, it seems the good God would not come down low enough in coming to them. But he created the child who knows nothing and utters only feeble cries; he created the poor savage who has only the natural law to guide him; and it is down to their hearts that he deigns to descend!

These are the flowers of the fields, whose simplicity delights him; and by this stooping so low, the Lord shows his infinite grandeur. Just as the sun shines on the cedar and on the little flower alike, so the divine Sun sheds his light in a particular way on each soul, great or small, and everything works together for its good — as in nature, the seasons are arranged so as to make the humblest daisy open, on the appointed day.


* * *

No doubt, Mother, you are wondering in amazement where I am going with this; for so far I have said nothing yet that resembles the story of my life. But did you not order me to write without constraint whatever comes naturally into my mind? So it is not my life, properly speaking, that you will find in these pages; it is my thoughts on the graces Our Lord has deigned to grant me.

I am at a point in my life when I can look back on the past; my soul has ripened in the crucible of interior and exterior trials. Now, like a flower after the storm, I lift my head, and I see fulfilled in me the words of the psalm:

"The Lord is my Shepherd, I shall lack nothing. He makes me rest in pleasant and fertile pastures; he leads me gently beside the waters. He guides my soul without wearying it... But even if I went down into the valley of the shadow of death, I would fear no evil, because you will be with me, Lord!"

Yes, the Lord has always been to me compassionate and full of sweetness, slow to punish, and abounding in mercies! So I feel a real happiness in coming to sing his ineffable kindness beside you, Mother. It is for you alone that I shall write the story of the little flower gathered by Jesus; this thought will help me to speak freely, without worrying about style or about the many digressions I shall make. A mother's heart always understands her child, even when it can only stammer. So I am sure of being understood — and guessed.

If a little flower could speak, it seems to me it would simply say what the good God has done for it, without trying to hide his gifts. Under the pretext of humility, it would not say that it is ungainly and without perfume, that the sun has dulled its brightness, that the storms have broken its stem, when it recognized in itself quite the opposite.

The flower that is going to tell its story rejoices at having to make known the wholly gratuitous favors of Jesus. It acknowledges that nothing in it was able to attract his divine gaze; that his mercy alone has showered it with good things. It is he who made it be born on holy ground, as though steeped in a virginal perfume; it is he who set before it eight lilies dazzling in whiteness. In his love he willed to preserve it from the poisoned breath of the world: scarcely had its petals begun to unfold when this good Master transplanted it onto the mountain of Carmel, into the chosen garden of the Virgin Mary.

Mother, I have just summed up in a few words what the good God has done for me; now I shall go into the details of my life as a child. I know that where anyone else would see only a tedious tale, your maternal heart will find charms.


* * *

In the story of my soul up to my entrance into Carmel, I distinguish three well-marked periods. The first, despite its short duration, is not the least fruitful in memories; it extends from the awakening of my reason to the departure of my darling mother for the homeland of heaven — that is, to my age of four years and eight months.

The good God gave me the grace of opening my intelligence very early, and of engraving the memories of my childhood so deeply in my memory that these past events seem to me to have happened yesterday. No doubt Jesus wanted me to know and appreciate the incomparable mother he had given me. Alas! His divine hand soon took her from me, to crown her in heaven.

All my life long, the Lord has delighted to surround me with love; my first memories are stamped with the most tender smiles and caresses. But if he had placed so much love beside me, he had also put it into my little heart, making it loving and sensitive. You cannot imagine how I cherished my father and my mother; I showed them my fondness in a thousand ways, for I was very demonstrative; though the means I used then make me laugh today when I think of them.

You wished, Mother, to put into my hands my mother's letters, written at that time to my sister Pauline, who was boarding at the Visitation at Le Mans. I remember perfectly the details they contain; but it will be easier for me simply to quote certain passages of those charming letters — often too flattering toward me, being dictated by a mother's love.

To support what I said about the way I showed my affection to my parents, here is a word from my mother:

> The baby is an imp like no other, who comes to caress me, wishing me dead! "Oh! how I wish you would die, my poor little mother!" They scold her, but she excuses herself, looking quite astonished, saying: "But it's so you can go to heaven, since you say you have to die to go there!" In her excesses of love she wishes the same death on her father too.

> This poor darling does not want to leave me; she is continually beside me and follows me happily, especially to the garden. When I am not there, she refuses to stay and cries so much that they have to bring her back to me. Likewise, she would never climb the stairs alone without calling me at every step: Mama! mama! As many steps, so many mamas! and if by misfortune I forgot even once to answer, "Yes, my little girl!" she would stay right there, neither advancing nor going back.

I was going on for my third year when my mother wrote:

> ... Little Thérèse asked me the other day whether she will go to heaven. "Yes, if you're a good girl," I answered her. — "Ah! Mama," she then said, "if I weren't pretty [good], would I go to hell? But I know very well what I'd do: I would fly away with you, who would be in heaven; then you'd hold me very tight in your arms. How would the good God manage to take me?" I saw in her gaze that she was persuaded the good God could do nothing to her if she hid in her mother's arms.

> Marie loves her little sister very much. She is a child who gives us all great joy; she is extraordinarily frank: it is charming to see her run after me to make her confession. "Mama, I pushed Céline once, I hit her once; but I won't do it again."

> As soon as she has done the least mishap, everyone must know about it: yesterday, having torn a little corner of the wallpaper by accident, she put herself in a state to make you pity her; then her father had to be told quickly. When he came home four hours later, no one was thinking of it anymore; but she ran to Marie, saying to her: "Tell Papa quickly that I tore the paper." She stood there like a little criminal awaiting her sentence; but she has it in her little head that she will be forgiven more easily if she accuses herself.

In finding here the name of my dear little father, I am naturally led to some very happy memories. When he came home, I invariably ran to meet him and sat on one of his boots; then he would walk me about like that, as long as I wanted, through the rooms and the garden. Mama said, laughing, that he did everything I wanted: "What do you expect?" he answered, "she's the queen!" Then he took me in his arms, lifted me up high, sat me on his shoulder, kissed me, and caressed me in every way.

Yet I cannot say that he spoiled me. I remember very well that one day, when I was swinging merrily, my father came by and called me, saying: "Come and kiss me, my little queen!" Against my habit, I would not budge, and answered naughtily: "Move yourself, Papa!" He paid me no attention, and did well. Marie was there. "You ill-bred little girl," she said to me, "how ugly it is to answer your father like that!" At once I got down from my fatal swing; the lesson had only struck home too well! The whole house rang with my cries of contrition; I quickly climbed the stairs, and this time I did not call Mama at each step; I thought only of finding Papa, of making it up with him, which was very quickly done.

I could not bear the thought of having grieved my beloved parents; acknowledging my faults was a matter of an instant, as this childhood trait, told so naturally by my mother herself, also proves:

> One morning, I wanted to kiss little Thérèse before going downstairs; she seemed to be sound asleep; I did not dare wake her, when Marie said to me: "Mama, she's pretending to sleep, I'm sure of it." Then I bent over her forehead to kiss her; but she hid under her covers at once, saying to me like a spoiled child: "I don't want anyone to see me." — I was anything but pleased, and made her feel it. Two minutes later I heard her crying, and soon, to my great surprise, I saw her at my side! She had gotten out of her little bed all alone, had come down the stairs barefoot, entangled in her nightgown, which was longer than she was. Her little face was covered with tears. — "Mama," she said to me, throwing herself at my knees, "Mama, I was naughty, forgive me!" Forgiveness was quickly granted. I took my cherub in my arms, pressing her to my heart and covering her with kisses.

[Poem, as printed in the 1898 book:]

Of Mama I loved the smile;

Her deep gaze seemed to say:

"Eternity steals me away and draws me...

I shall go into the blue Heaven

To see God!"

I also remember the very great affection I had, already at that time, for my eldest sister Marie, who had just finished her studies at the Visitation. Without seeming to, I paid attention to everything that happened and was said around me; it seems to me I judged things as I do now. I listened attentively to what she taught Céline; and to obtain the favor of being admitted into her room during the lessons, I was very good and obeyed her in everything; so she showered me with presents which, despite their small value, gave me extreme pleasure.

I can say that my two eldest sisters made me very proud! But as Pauline seemed so far away to me, I dreamed of her from morning to night. When I was just beginning to talk and Mama asked me, "What are you thinking about?" the answer was invariable: "About Pauline!" Sometimes I heard it said that Pauline would be a nun; then, without really knowing what that was, I thought: "I too will be a nun!" That is one of my first memories; and since then I have never changed my resolution. So it was the example of this dear sister who, from the age of two, drew me toward the Spouse of virgins.

O my Mother, how many sweet reflections I would like to confide to you here about my relations with Pauline! But it would be too long.

My dear little Léonie also held a very large place in my heart; she loved me very much. In the evening, coming back from her lessons, she would keep me when the whole family was out walking; I seem to still hear the sweet refrains she sang in her gentle voice to put me to sleep. I remember perfectly her first communion. I also remember the poor little girl, her companion, whom my mother had clothed, according to the touching custom of well-to-do families of Alençon. That child did not leave Léonie for a single moment of that beautiful day; and in the evening at the great dinner, they put her in the place of honor. Alas! I was too little to stay at that pious feast; but I took a small part in it, thanks to the goodness of Papa, who came himself, at dessert, to bring his little queen a piece of the pièce montée.

Now I must speak of Céline, the little companion of my childhood. For her, memories are so abundant that I do not know which to choose. We understood each other perfectly, both of us; but I was much livelier and much less naive than she. Here is a letter that will show you, Mother, how sweet Céline was, and how naughty I was. I was then nearly three years old, and Céline six and a half.

> My little Céline is altogether inclined to virtue; as for the little ferret, one doesn't really know how it will turn out; she's so little, so scatterbrained! She is a very intelligent child; but she is much less gentle than her sister, and above all of an almost invincible stubbornness. When she says no, nothing can make her give in; you could put her in the cellar a whole day without getting a yes out of her; she'd sooner sleep there!

I had another fault my mother does not mention in her letters: it was a great self-love. Here are only two examples of it.

One day, wanting no doubt to find out how far my pride would go, she said to me with a smile: "My little Thérèse, if you will kiss the ground, I will give you a sou." A sou was a whole fortune to me. To win it in that circumstance, I hardly needed to lower my dignity, since my little height did not put a considerable distance between me and the ground; yet my pride rebelled, and standing very straight, I answered Mama: "Oh! no, my little mother, I would rather not have a sou."

Another time, we were to go into the country to some friends' house. Mama told Marie to put my prettiest dress on me, but not to leave my arms bare. I said not a word, and even showed the indifference children of that age should have; but inwardly I said to myself: "All the same, I would have been much prettier with my little bare arms!"

With such a nature, I realize perfectly well that if I had been raised by parents without virtue, I would have become very wicked, and perhaps even would have rushed to my eternal loss. But Jesus was watching over his little fiancée; he made all her faults turn to her advantage, faults which, checked early, served her to grow in perfection. Indeed, as I had self-love and also the love of the good, it was enough for me to be told once, "You must not do such a thing," for me to no longer want to start again. I see with pleasure in my dear mother's letters that as I grew older I gave her more consolation; having only good examples before my eyes, I naturally wanted to follow them. Here is what she wrote in 1876:

> Even Thérèse wants to have a hand in making sacrifices. Marie has given her little sisters a chaplet made on purpose for counting their practices of virtue; together they hold real spiritual conferences, very amusing. Céline said the other day: "How can the good God be in such a little host?" Thérèse answered her: "That isn't so surprising, since the good God is all-powerful!" — "And what does all-powerful mean?" — "It means he does all he wants!"

> But the most curious thing is to see Thérèse put her hand into her little pocket a hundred times a day to draw a bead on her chaplet every time she makes a sacrifice.

> These two children are inseparable and suffice to entertain themselves. The nurse gave Thérèse a cock and a hen of a small breed; at once the baby gave the cock to her sister. Every day after dinner, the latter goes to get her cock, catches him along with the hen all of a sudden; then the two of them come and sit in the corner by the fire; they amuse themselves like that for a very long time.

> One morning, Thérèse took it into her head to get out of her little bed to go and sleep with Céline; the maid was looking for her to dress her; at last she caught sight of her, and the little one said to her, hugging her sister and squeezing her very tight in her arms: "Leave me alone, my poor Louise, you see very well that we're like the two little white hens — we can't be separated!"

It is certainly true that I could not bear to be without Céline; I would rather leave the table before finishing my dessert than not follow her as soon as she got up. Turning round in my big child's chair, I would want to get down quickly, and then we would go and play together.

On Sundays, as I was too little to go to the services, Mama stayed to watch me. On those occasions I showed great wisdom, walking only on tiptoe; but as soon as I heard the door open, it was an explosion of unmatched joy; I rushed out to meet my pretty little sister and said to her: "Oh, Céline! give me some blessed bread, quick!" One day, she had none!... What was to be done? I could not do without it; I called that feast my Mass. A luminous idea crossed my mind: "You have no blessed bread — well then, make some!" She opened the cupboard, took the bread, cut a little piece, and reciting an Ave Maria over it in a solemn tone, presented it to me triumphantly. And I, making the sign of the cross, ate it with great devotion, finding it tasted exactly like blessed bread.

One day, Léonie, thinking herself no doubt too big to play with dolls, came to find the two of us with a basket filled with dresses, pretty pieces of cloth and other trimmings, on which, having laid her doll, she said to us: "Here, my little sisters, choose!" Céline looked and took a little ball of braid. After a moment's reflection, I reached out my hand in my turn, saying: "I choose everything!" and carried off basket and doll without further ceremony.

This trait of my childhood is like the summary of my whole life. Later, when perfection appeared to me, I understood that to become a saint one must suffer much, always seek what is most perfect, and forget oneself. I understood that in sanctity there are many degrees, that each soul is free to respond to Our Lord's advances, to do little or much for his love; in a word, to choose among the sacrifices he asks. Then, as in the days of my childhood, I cried out: "My God, I choose everything! I do not want to be a saint by halves; it does not frighten me to suffer for you; I fear only one thing, and that is to keep my own will. Take it, for I choose everything that you want!"

But I am forgetting myself, my beloved Mother; I must not speak to you yet of my youth — I am still with the little baby of three and four years old.

I remember a dream I had at that age, which engraved itself deeply in my memory:

I was going to walk alone in the garden, when suddenly I saw, near the arbor, two frightful little devils dancing on a barrel of lime with astonishing agility, despite the heavy irons they had on their feet. They first cast flaming eyes at me; then, as if seized with fear, I saw them hurl themselves in the blink of an eye to the bottom of the barrel, come out again through some unknown opening, run and hide at last in the linen room, which opened at ground level onto the garden. Finding them so cowardly, I wanted to know what they were going to do; and overcoming my first fright, I went up to the window... The poor little devils were there, running over the tables and not knowing how to flee my gaze. From time to time they came closer, peered through the panes with a worried look; then, seeing that I was still there, they started running again like desperate things.

No doubt this dream has nothing extraordinary about it; I believe, however, that the good God made use of it to prove to me that a soul in a state of grace has nothing to fear from demons, who are cowards, capable of fleeing before a child's gaze.

O my Mother, how happy I was at that age! Not only was I beginning to enjoy life; virtue itself had charms for me. It seems to me I was in the same dispositions as today, having already great control over all my actions. So I had taken the habit of never complaining when something of mine was taken away; or else, when I was accused unjustly, I preferred to keep silent rather than excuse myself. There was no merit of mine in that; I did it naturally.

Ah! how quickly they passed, those sunny years of my little childhood, and what a sweet and gentle imprint they left in my soul! I remember with happiness the Sunday walks on which my good mother always accompanied us. I still feel the deep and poetic impressions that arose in my heart at the sight of wheat fields spangled with poppies, cornflowers and daisies. Already I loved distant views, space, tall trees; in a word, all of beautiful nature ravished me and transported my soul to the heavens.

Often during those long walks we met poor people, and little Thérèse was always charged with bringing them their alms, which made her very happy. Often, too, my good father, finding the road a little long for his little queen, brought her back home, much to her displeasure! Then, to console her, Céline filled her pretty little basket with daisies and gave them to her on her return.

Oh! Truly, everything smiled at me on earth. I found flowers beneath my every step, and my happy nature also helped to make my life pleasant; but a new period was about to open. Destined to be so soon the fiancée of Jesus, it was necessary for me to suffer from my childhood. Just as the flowers of spring begin to germinate under the snow and unfold at the sun's first rays, so the little flower whose memories I write has had to pass through the winter of trial, and let its tender calyx be filled with the dew of tears...



Chapter II

Death of her mother. — Les Buissonnets. — A father's love. First confession. The winter evenings. — A prophetic vision.

All the details of my mother's illness are still present to my heart. I remember above all the last weeks she spent on earth. Céline and I were like poor little exiles! Every morning Madame X* came to fetch us, and we passed the day at her house. Once, we had not had time to say our prayer before leaving, and my little sister said to me softly on the way: "Should we confess that we haven't said our prayer?" — "Oh! yes," I answered her. Then, very shyly, she confided her secret to that lady, who said to us at once: "Well then, my little girls, you are going to say it"; then, leaving us in a large room, she went away. Céline looked at me in amazement; I was no less amazed, and I cried out: "Ah! It isn't like Mama! She always made us say our prayer with her."

During the day, despite the distractions they tried to give us, the thought of our darling mother kept returning to us. I remember my sister, having received a fine apricot, leaned toward me and said: "We won't eat it — I'll give it to Mama." Alas! Our beloved mother was already too ill to eat the fruits of the earth; she was to satisfy her hunger now only in heaven with the glory of God, and drink with Jesus the mysterious wine he spoke of at his last Supper, promising to share it with us in the kingdom of his Father.

The touching ceremony of Extreme Unction impressed itself on my soul. I still see the place where they made me kneel; I still hear my poor father's sobs.

My mother left this world on August 28, 1877, in her forty-sixth year. The day after her death, my good father took me in his arms: "Come," he said to me, "kiss your dear little mother one last time." And I, without uttering a single word, drew my lips near my darling mother's frozen forehead.

I do not remember having wept much. I spoke to no one of the deep feelings that filled my heart; I looked and listened in silence. I saw many things they would have liked to hide from me: at one moment I found myself alone, facing the coffin, standing upright in the corridor; I stopped for a long time to look at it. I had never seen one, yet I understood! I was so little then that I had to raise my head to see the whole of it, and it seemed to me very large, very sad...

Fifteen years later, I found myself before another coffin, that of our holy Mother Geneviève; and I thought I was back in the days of my childhood! All my memories thronged in a crowd into my memory. It was truly the very same little Thérèse who was looking; but she had grown, and the coffin seemed small to her; she did not raise her head to look at it; she raised it now only to contemplate heaven, which seemed very joyful to her, for trial had ripened and strengthened her soul in such a way that nothing here below could sadden her anymore.

On the day the holy Church blessed my mother's mortal remains, the good God did not leave me entirely an orphan; he gave me another mother, and let me choose her freely. We were all five together, looking at one another sadly. Seeing us like that, our maid was moved with compassion and, turning toward Céline and toward me, said: "Poor little ones, you have no mother anymore!" Then Céline threw herself into Marie's arms, crying out: "Well then, it is you who will be Mama!" I, always used to following Céline, ought to have imitated her in so just an action; but I thought Pauline would perhaps be hurt and feel left out, having no little girl; so I looked at her tenderly, and hiding my little head against her heart, I said in my turn: "For me, it is Pauline who will be Mama!"

As I wrote above, it is from that time that I had to enter the second period of my existence, the most painful, especially from the entrance into Carmel of her whom I had chosen as my second mother. This period extends from the age of four and a half to my fourteenth year, when I recovered my childlike character, while understanding more and more the seriousness of life.

I must tell you, my revered Mother, that as soon as Mama died, my happy character changed completely. I, so lively, so expansive, became timid and gentle, sensitive to excess; a look was often enough to make me melt into tears. No one had to pay attention to me; I could not bear the company of strangers and only recovered my gaiety in the intimacy of the family. There I continued to be surrounded by the greatest delicacy. My father's heart, already so loving, seemed enriched with a truly motherly love, and I felt my sisters become for me the most tender, most unselfish of mothers. Ah! If the good God had not lavished his beneficent rays on his little flower, she could never have become acclimated to the earth. Still too weak to bear the rains and storms, she needed warmth, a gentle dew, and spring breezes; these kindnesses were not lacking to her, even under the snow of trial.


* * *

Soon my father decided to leave Alençon and come to live in Lisieux, so as to be closer to my uncle, my mother's brother. He made this sacrifice in order to entrust my sisters, still young, to my dear aunt's guidance, so that she would lead them in their new mission and serve us in some way as a mother. I felt no sorrow in leaving my native town; children love change and whatever is out of the ordinary; so it was with pleasure that I came to Lisieux. I remember the journey, arriving in the evening at my uncle's house; I still see my little cousins, Jeanne and Marie, waiting for us on the threshold of the house with my aunt. Oh! How touched I was by the affection our dear relatives showed us!

The next day they took us to our new dwelling — I mean Les Buissonnets, a secluded quarter situated very close to the beautiful promenade called the "Garden of the Star." The house my father rented seemed charming to me: a belvedere from which the view stretched far away, the English garden in front of the house, and behind the house another large kitchen garden. All that, for my young imagination, was happy novelty. Indeed, that smiling dwelling became the theater of very sweet joys, of unforgettable family scenes. Elsewhere, as I said above, I was exiled; I wept; I felt I no longer had a mother! There, my little heart blossomed, and I smiled at life again.

On waking, I found my sisters' caresses, and then at their sides I said my prayer. Next I took my reading lesson with Pauline; I remember that the word cieux [heavens] was the first I was able to read alone. As soon as my class was over, I went up to the belvedere, my father's usual residence. Ah! How happy I was when I had good marks to announce to him!

Every afternoon I went with him for a little walk, to visit the Blessed Sacrament, one day in one church, the next in another. That is how I entered the chapel of Carmel for the first time. "You see, my little queen," Papa said to me, "behind that great grille there are holy religious who always pray to the good God." I was far from thinking that nine years later I would be among them; that there, in that blessed Carmel, I would receive such great graces!

After the walk, I went back home and did my homework; then the rest of the time I hopped about in the garden around my good father. I did not know how to play with dolls; my greatest pleasure was preparing herbal teas with seeds and tree bark. When my infusions took on a fine hue, I quickly offered them to Papa in a pretty little cup that really made you want to savor its contents. That tender father at once left his work and then, smiling, pretended to drink.

I also loved growing flowers; I amused myself by setting up little altars in a hollow that was happily located in the middle of my garden wall. When everything was ready, I ran to Papa, who worked himself into ecstasies, to please me, before my marvelous altars, admiring what I considered a masterpiece! I would never finish if I wanted to recount a thousand traits of this kind whose memory I have kept. Ah! How shall I tell all the tenderness my incomparable father lavished on his little queen?

They were beautiful days for me, those when my dear king — as I loved to call him — took me fishing with him. Sometimes I tried to fish myself with my little line; more often I preferred to sit apart on the flowered grass. Then my thoughts became very deep; and without knowing what it was to meditate, my soul plunged into real prayer. I listened to distant sounds...

The afternoon passed quickly; soon we had to return to Les Buissonnets; but before packing up, I took the little snack brought in my small basket. Alas! The beautiful slice of bread and jam prepared by my sisters had changed in appearance. Instead of its bright color, I saw only a slight pink tint, all faded and shrunken in. Then the earth seemed sadder to me still, and I understood that only in heaven would joy be without a cloud.

Speaking of clouds, I remember one day the beautiful blue sky of the countryside became covered over; soon the storm began to thunder violently, accompanied by dazzling lightning. I turned to the right and to the left so as not to miss any of that majestic spectacle; at last I saw the lightning strike a neighboring meadow, and far from feeling the least fright, I was delighted; it seemed to me that the good God was very close to me! My dear father, less content than his queen, came to draw her from her delight; already the grass and the tall daisies, taller than I, sparkled with precious stones, and we had to cross several meadows to reach the road. So he took me in his arms, despite his bundle of fishing lines, and from there I looked down at the beautiful diamonds, almost sorry not to be covered and flooded with them.


* * *

I seem to have forgotten to say that during my daily walks, in Lisieux as in Alençon, I often carried alms to the unfortunate. One day we saw a poor old man dragging himself painfully along on crutches. I went up to give him my little coin; he fixed a long, sad gaze on me, then, shaking his head with a sorrowful smile, refused my alms. I cannot say what went on in my heart. I would have liked to console him, to relieve him; instead of that, I had perhaps just humiliated him and caused him pain!

No doubt he guessed my thought, for I soon saw him turn away and smile at me from a distance. At that moment my good father had just bought me a cake; I badly wanted to run and give it to the old man, telling myself: "He didn't want money, but surely a cake would please him." Then some kind of fear held me back; my heart was so heavy I could scarcely hide my tears. At last I recalled having heard it said that on the day of first communion one obtains all the graces one asks for: that thought consoled me at once. Although I was only six years old at the time, I said to myself: "On the day of my first communion I will pray for my poor man," and five years later I faithfully kept my resolution. I have always believed that my childish prayer for that suffering member of Our Lord was blessed and rewarded.

As I grew, I loved the good God more and more, and I gave him my heart very often, using the formula my mother had taught me; I strove to please Jesus in all my actions and paid great attention never to offend him. However, one day I committed a fault well worth reporting here; it gives me great reason to humble myself, and I believe I had perfect contrition for it.

It was in the month of May 1878. My sisters, thinking me too little to go to the May devotions every evening, I stayed with the maid, and I made my devotions with her before my own little altar, which I arranged in my own way. Everything was so small — candlesticks, flowerpots, etc. — that two taper-matches sufficed to light it perfectly. Sometimes Victoire, to economize my supply of matches, surprised me with two real candle-ends; but that was rare.

One evening, just as we were about to begin praying, I said to her: "Would you start the Remember, O most gracious Virgin, while I light them?" She pretended to begin, then looked at me, laughing loudly. I, seeing my precious matches burning away quickly, begged her once more to say the Memorare very quickly. Silence again! Laughter again! Then, at the height of my indignation, I got up, and leaving behind my usual gentleness, stamped my foot hard, crying out loud: "Victoire, you're mean!" The poor girl no longer wanted to laugh; she looked at me, dumb with astonishment, and showed me — too late — the surprise of her two candle-ends hidden under her apron. After weeping with anger, alas! I shed tears of contrition; I was altogether ashamed and desolate, and I took a firm resolution never to do it again.

A short time afterward, I went to confession. Such a sweet memory for me! Pauline said to me: "My little Thérèse, it is not to a man, but to the good God himself that you are going to confess your sins." I became so convinced of it that I seriously asked her whether I should not tell M. l'abbé D* that I loved him with all my heart, since it was to the good God that I would be speaking in his person.

Well instructed in all that I had to do, I entered the confessional, and turning to face the priest directly, the better to see him, I confessed and received his blessing with a great spirit of faith — my sister having assured me that at that solemn moment the tears of the little Jesus would purify my soul. I remember the exhortation addressed to me: it invited me above all to devotion to the Blessed Virgin; and I promised myself to redouble my tenderness for her who already held so large a place in my heart.

At last, I passed my little chaplet through to have it blessed, and I came out of the confessional so happy and so light that I had never felt so much joy. It was evening. Arriving under a street lamp, I stopped, and taking from my pocket the newly blessed chaplet, I turned it over and over in every direction. "What are you looking at, my little Thérèse?" Pauline asked me. "But I'm looking at how a blessed chaplet is made!" That naive answer greatly amused my sisters. As for me, I remained a long time penetrated by the grace I had received. From then on, I wanted to go to confession at the great feasts, and that confession, I can say it, filled my whole little interior with gladness.

The feasts!... Ah! What perfumed memories that simple word recalls to me!... The feasts!... I loved them so much! My sisters knew so well how to explain the mysteries hidden in each one of them to me! Yes, those days of earth became for me days of heaven. I especially loved the processions of the Blessed Sacrament. What joy to scatter flowers beneath the good God's steps! But before letting them fall there, I threw them high up, and I was never so happy as when I saw my scattered roses touch the sacred monstrance.

The feasts! Ah! If the great feasts were rare, each week brought one very dear to my heart: Sunday. What a radiant day! It was the good God's feast, the feast of rest. First, the whole family set out for High Mass; and I remember that at the sermon — our chapel being far from the pulpit — we had to go down and find seats in the nave, which was not very easy. But for little Thérèse and her father, everyone hurried to offer them chairs. My uncle was delighted to see the two of us arrive; he called me his little sunbeam, and said that seeing that venerable patriarch leading his little daughter by the hand was a picture that delighted him.

I did not trouble much about being looked at; I cared only about listening attentively to the priest. A sermon on the Passion of Our Lord was the first I understood and that touched me deeply; I was then five and a half, and from then on I was able to grasp and savor the meaning of all the instructions.

When Saint Thérèse was mentioned, my father leaned down and said to me softly: "Listen well, my little queen — they're speaking of your patroness." I listened well, indeed; but I confess I more often looked at Papa than at the preacher. His beautiful face said so many things to me! Sometimes his eyes filled with tears that he struggled in vain to hold back. Listening to the eternal truths, he seemed already no longer to dwell on earth; his soul seemed plunged in another world. Alas! His course was far, very far from its term: long and sorrowful years had yet to flow by before the beautiful heaven opened to his eyes and the Lord, with his divine hand, wiped away the bitter tears of his faithful servant.

I return to my Sunday. That joyful feast, which passed so quickly, had its tinge of melancholy too: my happiness was unmixed until Vespers; but from that evening office, a feeling of sadness invaded my soul. I thought that the next day we would have to begin life again, work, learn lessons, and my heart felt the exile of earth. I sighed after the rest of heaven, the Sunday without sunset of the true homeland!

Before returning to Les Buissonnets, my aunt invited us, one after the other, to spend the evening at her house: I was very happy when my turn came. I listened with extreme pleasure to everything my uncle said; his serious conversations interested me enormously; he certainly did not suspect the attention I paid them. However, my joy was mixed with fright when he sat me on just one of his knees, singing Bluebeard in a thunderous voice!

Around eight o'clock, my father came to fetch me. Then I remember looking at the stars with inexpressible delight... In the deep firmament there was above all a group of golden pearls (the belt of Orion) that I noticed with delight, finding in it the shape of a T — and I said on the way to my dear father: "Look, Papa, my name is written in heaven!" Then, not wanting to see anything more of this ugly earth, I asked him to lead me; and without looking where I put my feet, I held my little head high in the air, never tiring of contemplating the starry azure.


* * *

What shall I say of the winter evenings at Les Buissonnets? After the game of checkers, my sisters read the Liturgical Year; then a few pages of an interesting and instructive book at the same time. During this time I took my place on my father's knees; and when the reading was over, he sang, in his beautiful voice, melodious refrains as if to put me to sleep. Then I leaned my head on his heart, and he rocked me gently...

At last we went upstairs to say our prayers; and there again, I had my little place beside my king, and I had only to look at him to know how the saints pray...

Afterward, Pauline put me to bed; and after that I invariably said to her: "Was I good today? — Is the good God pleased with me? — Are the little angels going to fly around me?..." The answer was always yes; otherwise I would have spent the whole night weeping. After this interrogation, my sisters kissed me, and little Thérèse stayed alone in the darkness.

I count it a real grace to have been trained from childhood to overcome my fears. Sometimes Pauline sent me alone in the evening to fetch something in a distant room; she would not allow any refusal, and it was necessary for me, for I would have become very fearful; whereas now it is quite difficult to frighten me. I wonder how my little mother was able to raise me with so much love without spoiling me, for she let no imperfection of mine pass: she never reproached me without cause, but neither did she — I knew it well — ever go back on a thing decided.

This dear sister received my most intimate confidences; she enlightened all my doubts. One day I told her my surprise that the good God does not give an equal glory in heaven to all the elect; I was afraid that all would not be happy. Then she sent me to fetch Papa's big glass and set it beside my little thimble; then, filling them both with water, she asked me which seemed the fuller. I told her I saw them as full, the one as the other, and that it was impossible to pour more water into them than they could hold. Pauline then made me understand that in heaven the last of the elect would not envy the happiness of the first. That is how, putting the most sublime secrets within my reach, she gave my soul the food it needed.


* * *

With what joy I saw each year the prize-giving arrive! Although I competed all alone, justice, as always, was nonetheless kept; I had only the absolutely deserved rewards. My heart beat very hard as I listened to my sentence, receiving from my father's hands, before the whole assembled family, the prizes and the crowns. It was for me like an image of the judgment!

Alas! Seeing Papa so radiant, I did not foresee the great trials that awaited him. One day, however, the good God showed me in an extraordinary vision the living image of that coming sorrow.

My father was away on a journey and was not to return for some time; it may have been two or three o'clock in the afternoon. The sun shone brightly and all nature seemed to be in festivity. I found myself alone at a window looking onto the kitchen garden, my mind full of cheerful thoughts, when I saw, in front of the wash-house, facing me, a man dressed exactly like Papa, with the same tall build and the same walk, but in addition very bent and aged. I say aged to depict the general look of his person; for I did not see his face, his head being covered with a thick veil. He was coming slowly, with a regular step, along my little garden. At once a feeling of supernatural dread seized me, and I called out loudly in a trembling voice: "Papa! Papa!..." But the mysterious personage did not seem to hear me; he continued his march without even turning aside, and so made his way toward a group of firs that divided the main path of the garden. I expected to see him reappear on the other side of the tall trees; but the prophetic vision had vanished!

All that lasted only an instant — an instant which engraved itself so deeply in my memory that today still, after so many years, the recollection of it is as present to me as the vision itself.

My sisters were together in a neighboring room. Hearing me call Papa, they felt themselves an impression of fear. Hiding her emotion, Marie ran to me: "Why are you calling Papa like that, when he's in Alençon?" I told what I had just seen, and to reassure me, they told me that the maid, no doubt wanting to frighten me, had hidden her head with her apron.

But Victoire, when questioned, insisted she had not left her kitchen; besides, the truth could not be darkened in my mind: I had seen a man, and that man resembled Papa absolutely. So we all went behind the mass of trees, and having found nothing, my sisters told me to think of it no more. Think of it no more! Ah! That was not in my power. Very often my imagination represented that mysterious vision to me. Very often I sought to lift the veil that hid its meaning from me, and I kept in the depths of my heart the intimate conviction that one day it would be entirely revealed to me.

And you know everything, my beloved Mother! You know it now: it was truly my father whom the good God had shown me, advancing bent with age, and bearing on his venerable face, on his white head, the sign of his great trial. As the adorable Face of Jesus was veiled during his Passion, so the face of his faithful servant was to be veiled in the days of his humiliation, so as to shine with greater brightness in the heavens. Ah! How I admire God's way with us, showing us that precious cross in advance, as a father shows his children a glimpse of the glorious future he is preparing for them and delights, in his love, to consider himself the priceless riches that will be their inheritance!

But a reflection comes to my mind: "Why did the good God give this light to a child who, if she had understood it, would have died of grief?" Why?... That is one of those impenetrable mysteries we shall understand only in heaven, and it will be the subject of our eternal admiration! My God, how good you are! How you proportion trials to our strength! I had not even the courage at that time to think, without dread, that Papa could die. One day he had climbed to the top of a ladder, and as I was standing there very close, he said to me: "Move away, my little queen, for if I fall, I shall crush you." At once I felt an interior revolt, and drawing closer still to the ladder, I thought: "At least, if Papa falls, I won't have the pain of seeing him die; I shall die with him."

No, I cannot say how much I loved my father! Everything in him caused me admiration. When he explained his thoughts to me about very serious things — as if I were a grown girl — I naively said to him: "Of course, Papa, if you spoke like that to the great men of the government, they would take you and make you king, and then France would be happier than she has ever been; but you, you would be unhappy, since that is the lot of all kings; and then you wouldn't be my king anymore, all mine — so I prefer that they don't know you."


* * *

Around the age of six or seven, I saw the sea for the first time. That spectacle made a deep impression on me; I could not take my eyes off it. Its majesty, the roaring of its waves, everything spoke to my soul of the greatness and power of the good God. I remember that on the beach a gentleman and a lady looked at me for a long time, and asking Papa whether I belonged to him, they said I was a very pretty little girl. At once my father signaled to them not to pay me any compliment. It gave me pleasure to hear that, for I did not think myself pretty; my sisters took such care never to use any language capable of making me lose my childlike simplicity and candor! Also, since I believed only them, I attached no great importance to the admiring words and glances of those people, and thought of them no more.

That evening, at the hour when the sun seems to bathe in the immensity of the waves, leaving before it a luminous furrow, I went and sat with Pauline on a deserted rock. I contemplated for a long time that furrow of gold, which she told me was the image of grace illumining here below the path of faithful souls. Then I pictured my heart in the middle of the furrow, like a light little boat with a graceful white sail, and I took the resolution never to move it away from the gaze of Jesus, so that it could sail in peace and swiftly toward the shore of heaven.



Chapter III

The boarding school. — A painful separation. A strange illness. A visible smile of the Queen of Heaven.

I was eight and a half when Léonie left boarding school and I replaced her at the Abbey of the Benedictines of Lisieux. I was placed in a class of pupils all older than I; one of them, fourteen years old, was not very intelligent, but she knew how to impose herself on the boarders. Seeing me so young, almost always first in compositions, and cherished by all the religious, she became jealous of me and made me pay in a thousand ways for my little successes. With my timid and delicate nature, I did not know how to defend myself and contented myself with weeping without saying anything. Céline, as well as my elder sisters, was ignorant of my sorrow; but I had not virtue enough to rise above such miseries, and my poor little heart suffered much.

Every evening, happily, I came home to my father's house; then my soul blossomed; I jumped onto my father's knees, told him the marks I had been given, and his kiss made me forget all my pains. With what joy I announced the result of my first composition! I had the maximum, and as my reward I received a pretty little white coin, which I placed in my moneybox for the poor and which was destined to receive a new companion nearly every Thursday. Ah! I had a real need of these little treats; it was very useful for the little flower to plunge her tender roots often into the beloved chosen soil of the family, since she found nowhere else the sap necessary for her subsistence.

Every Thursday we had a holiday; but I no longer recognized Pauline's holidays, which I spent in large part in the belvedere with Papa. Not knowing how to play like other children, I did not feel myself an agreeable companion; nevertheless I did my best to imitate the others, without ever succeeding.


* * *

After Céline, who was for me, so to speak, indispensable, I sought above all my little cousin Marie, because she left me free to choose games to my taste. We were already deeply united in heart and will, as if the good God had made us foresee that one day, in Carmel, we would embrace the same religious life.

Very often — the scene took place at my uncle's — Marie and Thérèse became two very penitent anchorites, possessing only a poor hut, a small field of wheat, and a garden in which to grow a few vegetables. Their life flowed in continual contemplation; that is to say, one replaced the other at prayer when they had to occupy themselves with the active life. Everything was done with perfect understanding, silence, and perfectly religious manners. If we went for a walk, our game continued even in the street: the two hermits recited the chaplet, using their fingers, so as not to show their devotion to the indiscreet public. However, one day the hermit Thérèse forgot herself: having received a cake for her snack, she made, before eating it, a big sign of the cross; and several profane people of the world did not refrain from smiling.

Our union of wills sometimes went beyond bounds. One evening, coming back from the Abbey, we wanted to imitate the modesty of the solitaries. I said to Marie: "Lead me — I'm going to shut my eyes." — "I want to shut mine too," she answered me; and each did her own will.

We were walking on a sidewalk, so we did not have to fear carriages. But after an agreeable walk of a few minutes, during which the two scatterbrains savored the delights of walking without seeing, they both fell onto some crates set at the door of a shop and knocked them over as they fell. At once the shopkeeper came out in a rage to pick up his merchandise; but the volunteer blind girls had picked themselves up all alone and walked off at a quick step, eyes wide open and ears too, to hear the just reproaches of Jeanne, who seemed as angry as the shopkeeper.


* * *

I have said nothing yet about my new relations with Céline. In Lisieux, the roles had changed: she had become the little imp filled with mischief, and Thérèse a very gentle little girl, but tearful to excess! So she needed a defender, and who can say with what intrepidity my dear little sister took charge of that office? We often gave each other little presents, which, on either side, caused an unparalleled happiness. Ah! At that age we were not blasé; our soul, in all its freshness, blossomed like a spring flower, happy to receive the morning dew; the same light breeze swayed our corollas. Yes, our joys were shared in common: I felt it well on the so-beautiful day of my dear Céline's first communion!

I was seven years old then, and did not yet attend the Abbey. How sweet the memory of her preparation is to me! Every evening, during the last weeks, my sisters spoke to her of the great action she was about to perform; I listened, eager to prepare myself too, and when they told me to withdraw because I was still too little, my heart was very heavy; I thought that four years was not too long to prepare to receive the good God.

One evening I heard these words addressed to my happy little sister: "From your first communion on, a wholly new life will have to begin." At once I took the resolution not to wait for that time myself, but to begin a new life with Céline.

During her preparatory retreat, she stayed at the Abbey as a full boarder, and her absence seemed very long to me. At last her beautiful day arrived. What a delicious impression it left in my heart! It was like the prelude of my own first communion! Ah! How many graces I received that day! I consider it one of the most beautiful of my life.


* * *

I have gone back a little to recall that ineffable memory. Now I must speak of the painful separation that broke my heart, when Jesus took from me my so tenderly loved little mother. I had once told her that I wanted to go away with her into a distant desert; she answered me then that her desire was mine, but she would wait until I was big enough to leave. That unrealizable promise, little Thérèse had taken it seriously, and what was her pain when she heard her dear Pauline speaking with Marie of her approaching entrance into Carmel! I did not know Carmel; but I understood that she would leave me to enter a convent; I understood she would not wait for me!

How could I tell the anguish of my heart? In one instant, life appeared to me in all its reality: filled with sufferings and continual separations, and I shed very bitter tears. I did not yet know the joy of sacrifice; I was weak, so weak, that I count it a great grace to have been able to bear, without dying, a trial apparently far beyond my strength.

I shall always remember with what tenderness my little mother consoled me. She explained the life of the cloister to me; and behold, one evening, turning over all alone in my heart the picture she had traced of it for me, I felt that Carmel was the desert where the good God also wanted to hide me. I felt it with such force that there was not the slightest doubt in my mind; it was not a child's dream that lets itself be carried away, but the certainty of a divine call. That impression, which I cannot render, left me in great peace.

The next day, I confided my desires to Pauline, who, looking on them as the will of heaven, promised to take me soon to Carmel to see the Mother Prioress, to whom I could tell my secret.

A Sunday was chosen for that solemn visit. My embarrassment was great when I learned that my cousin Marie, still young enough to see the Carmelites, was to accompany me. However, I had to find the means of staying alone; and this is what came into my head: I told Marie that, having the privilege of seeing the Reverend Mother, we ought to be very good, very polite, and to that end to confide our secrets to her; so, one after the other, we would have to go out for a moment. Despite her reluctance to confide secrets she did not have, Marie took me at my word; and so I was able to stay alone with you, my dear Mother. Having heard my great confidences and believing in my vocation, you nevertheless told me that postulants of nine years old were not received, and that I would have to wait until I was sixteen. I had to resign myself, despite my keen desire to enter with Pauline and to make my first communion on the day of her taking of the Habit.


* * *

At last October 2 arrived! A day of tears and blessings, when Jesus gathered the first of his flowers, the chosen flower who was to be, a few years later, the Mother of her sisters. While my beloved father, accompanied by my uncle and Marie, climbed the mountain of Carmel to offer his first sacrifice, my aunt took me to Mass with my sisters and cousins. We were melting into tears, so much so that as we entered the church people looked at us with astonishment; but that did not keep me from weeping. I asked myself how the sun could still shine on the earth!

Perhaps you will think, my revered Mother, that I am exaggerating my pain a little. I realize indeed that this departure ought not to have grieved me to that extent; but I must admit, my soul was far from mature, and I had to pass through many crucibles before reaching the shore of peace, before tasting the delicious fruits of total abandonment and perfect love.

On the afternoon of that October 2, 1882, I saw my dear Pauline, become Sister Agnes of Jesus, behind the grilles of Carmel. Oh! How I suffered in that parlor! Since I am writing the story of my soul, I must tell everything, it seems to me! Well then, I confess I counted as nothing the first sufferings of separation compared with those that followed. I, who was used to speaking heart to heart with my little mother, obtained with great difficulty two or three minutes at the end of the family parlors; of course, I spent them shedding tears and went away with my heart torn.

I did not understand that it would have been impossible to give each of us half an hour often, and that the longest moments had to be reserved for my little father and Marie; I did not understand, and I said in the depths of my heart: "Pauline is lost to me!" My mind developed in such an astonishing way amid suffering that I was not long in falling gravely ill.


* * *

The illness that struck me certainly came from the jealousy of the demon, who, furious at that first entrance into Carmel, wanted to avenge himself on me for the very great wrong my family was to do him in the future. But he did not know that the Queen of Heaven was faithfully watching over her little flower, that she was smiling on her from on high and preparing to make the tempest cease at the very moment when its delicate and fragile stem was to break forever.

At the end of that year 1882, I was seized with a continual but bearable headache, which did not keep me from pursuing my studies; this lasted until the feast of Easter 1883. At that time, my father having gone to Paris with my elder sisters, he entrusted Céline and me to my uncle and aunt.

One evening when I was alone with my uncle, he spoke to me of my mother, of past memories, with a tenderness that touched me deeply and made me burst into sobs. Seeing me in that state, he said he would not speak to me anymore about my dear little mother. But I understood that far from doing me harm, it was good for me to weep a little. Indeed, my heart was overflowing from being so filled with bitterness. My aunt, at that time, was a mother to me, the most affectionate one could be; and my uncle likewise heaped all sorts of kindnesses on me.

The good God had decided otherwise. That very evening, my headache became extremely violent, and I was seized with a strange trembling that lasted all night. My aunt, like a true mother, did not leave me for an instant; she surrounded me during that illness with the most tender solicitude, lavished on me the most devoted, most delicate care.

One may guess the sorrow of my poor father, when on his return from Paris he saw me fallen into that desperate state. He soon believed I was going to die; but Our Lord could have answered him: "This illness is not unto death; it is sent so that God may be glorified." Yes, the good God was glorified in this trial! He was glorified by the admirable resignation of my father, and by that of my sisters — of Marie especially. How she suffered because of me! How great is my gratitude toward that dear sister! Her heart dictated to her what I needed, and truly a mother's heart is far more powerful than the science of the most skillful doctors.


* * *

However, Sister Agnes of Jesus's taking of the Habit was approaching, and they avoided speaking of it before me for fear of hurting me, thinking I would not be able to go. In the depths of my heart, I firmly believed that the good God would grant me the consolation of seeing my dear Pauline again on that day. Yes, I knew well that that feast would be without clouds; I knew that Jesus would not try his fiancée by my absence — she who had already suffered so much from her little daughter's illness. Indeed, I was able to embrace my dear mother, sit on her knees, hide under her veil and receive her sweet caresses; I was able to contemplate her, so ravishing in her white array! Truly it was a beautiful day in the midst of my dark trial; but that day, or rather that hour, passed quickly, and soon I had to get into the carriage that took me far from Carmel!

On arriving at Les Buissonnets, they put me to bed, although I felt no fatigue; but the next day I was seized violently again, and the illness became so grave that, by human reckoning, I was never to recover.

I do not know how to describe so strange an illness: I said things I did not think, I did others as though forced in spite of myself; almost always I seemed to be in delirium, and yet I am sure I was not deprived for a single instant of the use of my reason. Often I remained in a faint for hours, in such a faint that it would have been impossible for me to make the slightest movement. Nevertheless, in the midst of that extraordinary torpor, I heard distinctly what was said around me, even in a low voice; I still remember it.

And what frights the demon inspired in me! I was absolutely afraid of everything: my bed seemed to me surrounded by frightful precipices; certain nails fixed into the wall of the room took in my eyes the terrifying image of big black charred fingers, and made me utter cries of terror. One day, while Papa watched me in silence, his hat, which he held in his hand, suddenly transformed itself into some horrible shape I know not what, and I showed such great fright that that poor father left, sobbing.

But if the good God allowed the demon to approach me exteriorly, he also sent me visible angels to console and strengthen me. Marie did not leave me; she never showed any annoyance, despite all the trouble I gave her, for I could not bear for her to go away from me. During meals, when Victoire watched me, I did not stop calling with tears: "Marie! Marie!" When she wanted to go out, it had to be to go to Mass or to see Pauline; only then did I say nothing.

And Léonie! And my little Céline! What did they not do for me! On Sundays they came and shut themselves up for whole hours with a poor child who looked like an idiot. Ah! My dear little sisters, how I made you suffer!

My uncle and my aunt were also full of affection for me. My aunt came every day to see me and brought me a thousand little treats. I could not say how much my tenderness for those dear relatives increased during that illness. I understood better than ever what my father often said to us: "Always remember, my children, that your uncle and aunt have toward you an uncommon devotion." In the days of his old age he experienced it himself; and now, how he must protect and bless those who lavished such devoted care on him!

In the moments when the suffering was less keen, I put my joy into braiding crowns of daisies and forget-me-nots for the Virgin Mary. We were then in the beautiful month of May; all nature was adorned with spring flowers; only the little flower languished and seemed forever withered! Nevertheless she had a sun beside her, and that sun was the miraculous statue of the Queen of Heaven. Often, very often, the little flower turned its corolla toward that blessed Star.

One day I saw my father enter my room; he seemed deeply moved, and coming up to Marie, he gave her several gold pieces with an expression of great sadness, asking her to write to Paris to request a novena of Masses at the shrine of Our Lady of Victory, so as to obtain the cure of his poor little queen. Ah! How touched I was to see his faith and his love! How I would have liked to get up and tell him I was cured! Alas! My desires could not make a miracle, and a very great one was needed to give me back my life! Yes, a great miracle was needed, and that miracle Our Lady of Victory worked it entirely.

One Sunday during the novena, Marie went out into the garden, leaving me with Léonie, who was reading near the window. After a few minutes, I began to call almost under my breath: "Marie! Marie!" Léonie, used to hearing me moan like that all the time, paid no attention; then I called out loud, and Marie came back to me. I saw her enter perfectly; but alas! For the first time, I did not recognize her. I searched all around me; I plunged an anxious gaze into the garden, and began to call again: "Marie! Marie!"

It was an unspeakable suffering, that forced, inexplicable struggle, and Marie suffered perhaps even more than her poor Thérèse! At last, after vain efforts to make herself recognized, she turned to Léonie, said a word to her in a low voice, and disappeared, pale and trembling.

My little Léonie soon carried me close to the window; then I saw in the garden, without recognizing her still, Marie, who walked gently, holding out her arms to me, smiling at me, and calling me in her most tender voice: "Thérèse, my little Thérèse!" That last attempt not having succeeded any better, my dear sister knelt weeping at the foot of my bed, and turning to the Blessed Virgin, implored her with the fervor of a mother who asks, who wants, the life of her child. Léonie and Céline imitated her, and it was a cry of faith that forced the door of heaven.

Finding no help on earth, and on the point of dying of grief, I too had turned toward my Mother in heaven, praying her with all my heart to have pity on me at last.

Suddenly the statue became animated! The Virgin Mary became beautiful, so beautiful that I shall never find an expression to render that divine beauty. Her face breathed a sweetness, a goodness, an ineffable tenderness; but what penetrated me to the depths of my soul was her ravishing smile! Then all my pains vanished; two big tears sprang from my eyelids and flowed silently...

Ah! They were tears of celestial and unmixed joy! The Blessed Virgin came close to me! She smiled at me... How happy I am! I thought; but I won't tell anyone, for my happiness would disappear. Then, without any effort, I lowered my eyes, and I recognized my dear Marie! She was looking at me with love; she seemed deeply moved, and seemed to suspect the great favor I had just received.

Ah! It was to her, to her touching prayer, that I owed that inexpressible grace of the smile of the Blessed Virgin! Seeing my gaze fixed on the statue, she had said to herself: "Thérèse is cured!" Yes, the little flower was about to be reborn to life; a luminous ray from her sweet sun had warmed her and delivered her forever from her cruel enemy! "The dark winter had just ended, the rains had ceased," and the Virgin Mary's flower strengthened in such a way that, five years later, she bloomed on the fertile mountain of Carmel.

As I have said, Marie was persuaded that the Blessed Virgin, in giving me back my health, had granted me some hidden grace; so, when I was alone with her, I could not resist her questions, so tender, so pressing. Astonished to see my secret discovered without my having said a single word, I confided it to her entirely. Alas! I had not been mistaken — my happiness was going to disappear and change into bitterness. For four years, the memory of that ineffable grace became for me a real pain of soul; and I was only to recover my happiness at the feet of Our Lady of Victory, in her blessed shrine. There it was returned to me in all its fullness; I shall speak later of that second grace.


* * *

Here is how my joy changed into sadness:

Marie, after having heard the naive and sincere account of my grace, asked my permission to tell it all at Carmel; I could not refuse. At my first visit to that blessed Carmel, I was filled with joy at seeing my little Pauline in the habit of the Blessed Virgin. What sweet moments for us both! There were so many things to tell each other! We had suffered so much! For my part, I could hardly speak, my heart was too full...

You were there, my beloved Mother, and with how many marks of affection did you not shower me? I saw still other religious, and you must remember that they questioned me about the miracle of my cure: some asked me whether the Blessed Virgin carried the Child Jesus; others, whether the angels accompanied her, etc. All those questions troubled me and hurt me; I could only answer one thing: "The Blessed Virgin seemed very beautiful to me; I saw her come toward me and smile at me."

Perceiving that the Carmelites were imagining something entirely different, I fancied I had lied. Ah! If I had kept my secret, I would also have kept my happiness. But the Virgin Mary permitted this torment for the good of my soul; without it, perhaps vanity would have slipped into my heart; whereas, humiliation becoming my lot, I could not look at myself without a feeling of profound horror. My God, you alone know what I suffered!



Chapter IV

First Communion. — Confirmation. — Lights and shadows. — A new separation. Gracious deliverance from her interior pains.

In telling of that visit to Carmel, I remember the first one that took place after Pauline's entrance. On the morning of that happy day, I asked myself what name would be given to me later. I knew there was a Sister Thérèse of Jesus; however, my beautiful name of Thérèse could not be taken from me. All of a sudden, I thought of the little Jesus, whom I loved so much, and I said to myself: "Oh! How happy I would be to be called Thérèse of the Child Jesus!" I took good care, though, my revered Mother, not to express this desire to you; and behold, you said to me in the middle of the conversation: "When you come among us, my dear little daughter, you will be called Thérèse of the Child Jesus!" My joy was great; and that happy meeting of thoughts seemed to me a delicateness of my beloved little Jesus.


* * *

I have not yet spoken of my love for pictures and for reading; and yet I owe to the beautiful pictures Pauline showed me one of the sweetest joys and the strongest impressions that ever stirred me to the practice of virtue. I forgot the hours looking at them. For example, "the little flower of the divine Prisoner" said so many things to me that I remained plunged in a sort of ecstasy; I offered myself to Jesus to be his little flower; I wanted to console him, to draw near me too, very close to the tabernacle, to be looked at, tended, and gathered by him.

As I did not know how to play, I would have spent my life reading. Happily, I had visible angels to guide me, who chose me books within the reach of my age, capable of entertaining me while nourishing my mind and my heart. I was only to take a very limited time for that chosen distraction, and that was often the subject of great sacrifices: as soon as the hour had passed, I made it a duty to stop at once, even in the middle of the most interesting passage.

As for the impression those readings produced, I must confess that, reading certain chivalrous tales, I did not always understand the hard facts of life. And so, admiring the patriotic deeds of French heroines, particularly of the Venerable Joan of Arc, I felt a great desire to imitate them. I then received a grace that I have always considered one of the greatest of my life; for at that age I was not favored with lights from on high as I am today.

Jesus made me understand that true glory, the only glory, is the one that will last forever; that to attain it, it is not necessary to accomplish dazzling works, but rather to hide from the eyes of others and from oneself, so that the left hand does not know what the right hand is doing. Thinking then that I was born for glory, and seeking the means to attain it, it was interiorly revealed to me that my glory would never appear to mortal eyes, but would consist in becoming a saint.

That desire might seem rash if one considers how imperfect I was, and how much I still am after so many years spent in religion; nevertheless I still feel the same bold confidence of becoming a great saint. I do not count on my merits, having none; but I hope in Him who is Virtue, Sanctity itself. It is he alone who, contenting himself with my feeble efforts, will raise me to himself, cover me with his merits, and make me a saint. I did not think then that one had to suffer much to arrive at sanctity; the good God was not long in unveiling that secret to me by the trials recounted above.


* * *

Now I take up my account at the point where I had left it.

Three months after my cure, my father took me on a pleasant journey; there I began to know the world. Everything was joy and happiness around me; I was celebrated, pampered, admired; in a word, for fifteen days my life was strewn only with flowers. Wisdom has good reason to say that "the witchcraft of trifles seduces even a mind far removed from evil." At ten years old, the heart easily lets itself be dazzled; and I confess that existence had charms for me. Alas! How well the world knows how to ally the joys of earth with the service of God! How little it thinks of death!

And yet death has come to visit a great number of the people I knew then — young, rich, and happy! I like to return in thought to the enchanting places where they lived, to ask myself where they are, what comes back to them today from the castles and parks where I saw them enjoying the comforts of life. And I think that "all is vanity on earth, except loving God and serving him alone."

Perhaps Jesus wanted me to know the world before his first visit to my soul, so as to let me choose more surely the way I was to promise him to follow.


* * *

My first communion will always remain for me a cloudless memory. It seems to me I could not have been better disposed. Do you remember, Mother, the delightful little book you gave me three months before the great day? That gracious means prepared me in a steady and swift way. If for a long time I had been thinking of my first communion, I nonetheless had to give my heart a new impulse and fill it with new flowers, as was marked in the precious manuscript. Each day, then, I made a great number of sacrifices and acts of love that turned into so many flowers; now they were violets, another time roses; then cornflowers, daisies, forget-me-nots — in a word, all the flowers of nature were to form in me the cradle of Jesus.

Last of all, I had Marie, who replaced Pauline for me.

Every evening I stayed a long time near her, eager to listen to her words; how many beautiful things she said to me! It seems to me that all her heart — so great, so generous — passed into me. As the warriors of old taught their children the trade of arms, so she taught me the combat of life, rousing my ardor and showing me the glorious palm. She also spoke to me of the immortal riches it is so easy to gather up each day, of the misfortune of trampling them underfoot when, so to speak, one has only to bend down to collect them.

How eloquent that dear sister was! I would have liked not to be the only one hearing her deep teachings; in my naivety I believed the greatest sinners would have been converted by listening to her, and that, leaving their perishable riches there, they would have sought only those of heaven.

At that time it would have been very sweet for me to make mental prayer; but Marie, finding me pious enough, permitted me only my vocal prayers. One day, at the Abbey, one of my teachers asked me what my occupations were on holidays, when I stayed at Les Buissonnets. I answered timidly: "Madame, I very often go and hide in a little empty space in my room, which I can easily close off with my bed-curtains, and there I think..." — "But what do you think about?" the good religious asked me, laughing. — "I think about the good God, about the swiftness of life, about eternity; in a word, I think!" That remark was not wasted, and later my teacher liked to remind me of the time when I thought, asking me whether I still thought... I understand today that I was then making true mental prayer, in which the divine Master was gently instructing my heart.


* * *

The three months of preparation for my first communion passed quickly; soon I had to enter retreat, and during that time become a senior boarder. Ah! What a blessed retreat! I do not think one could taste such joy outside religious communities: the number of children being small, it is all the easier to occupy oneself with each one. Yes, I write it with filial gratitude: our Abbey teachers lavished truly maternal care on us then. I do not know for what motive, but I perceived well that they watched over me even more than over my companions.

Each evening, the principal teacher came with her little lantern and gently opened my bed-curtains and placed a tender kiss on my forehead. She showed me so much affection that, touched by her goodness, I said to her one evening: "O Madame, I love you very much, so I'm going to confide a great secret to you." Drawing out mysteriously the precious little book from Carmel, hidden under my pillow, I showed it to her with eyes shining with joy. She opened it very delicately, leafed through it with attention, and made me notice how privileged I was. Several times, indeed, during my retreat, I experienced how few children, deprived of their mother as I was, are pampered as much as I was at that age.

I listened with great attention to the instructions given by M. l'abbé Domin, and I carefully made summaries of them. As for my own thoughts, I would write none of them down, saying I would remember them well; which turned out to be true.

With what happiness I attended all the offices, like the religious! I made myself noticed among my little companions by a large crucifix given by my dear Léonie; I wore it in my belt in the manner of the missionaries, and people believed I wanted to imitate my Carmelite sister that way. It was toward her, indeed, that my thoughts and my heart often flew! I knew she was in retreat too; not, it is true, so that Jesus would give himself to her, but to give herself entirely to Jesus, and that on the very day of my first communion. That solitude spent in anticipation was thus doubly dear to me.

At last the beautiful day of all the days of life dawned for me! What ineffable memories the least details of those heavenly hours left in my soul! The joyful waking at dawn, the respectful and tender kisses of the teachers and the older companions, the dressing room filled with snowy flakes, in which each child saw herself clothed in her turn; above all, the entrance into the chapel and the singing of the morning canticle:

O holy altar, surrounded by angels!

But I do not want to, and could not, tell everything... There are things that lose their perfume as soon as they are exposed to the air; there are intimate thoughts that cannot be translated into the language of earth without losing at once their profound and heavenly meaning!

Ah! How sweet was Jesus's first kiss to my soul! Yes, it was a kiss of love! I felt myself loved, and I also said: "I love you; I give myself to you forever!" Jesus made no request of me; he claimed no sacrifice. For a long time already he and little Thérèse had looked at each other and understood one another... That day, their meeting could no longer be called a simple look, but a fusion. We were no longer two: Thérèse had disappeared like the drop of water that loses itself in the midst of the ocean; Jesus remained alone; he was the Master, the King! Had Thérèse not asked him to take away her liberty? That liberty frightened her; she felt so weak, so fragile, that she wanted to be united forever to the divine Strength.

And behold, her joy became so great, so deep, that she could not contain it. Soon delicious tears flooded her, to the great astonishment of her companions who later said to one another: "Why did she cry? Did she have some anxiety of conscience?" — "No, it was rather not having her mother or her Carmelite sister, whom she loves so much, near her!" And no one understood that all the joy of heaven, coming into a heart, cannot be borne by that exiled, weak, mortal heart without its shedding tears...

How could the absence of my mother have grieved me on the day of my first communion? Since heaven dwelt in my soul: in receiving Jesus's visit, I also received that of my darling mother... Nor did I weep over Pauline's absence; we were more united than ever! No, I repeat it, joy alone — ineffable, deep — filled my heart.

In the afternoon I pronounced, in the name of my companions, the act of Consecration to the Blessed Virgin. My teachers no doubt chose me because I had been deprived very young of my mother on earth. Ah! I put all my heart into consecrating myself to the Virgin Mary, into asking her to watch over me! It seems to me she looked at her little flower with love and smiled at her again. I remembered her visible smile, which had formerly cured and delivered me; I knew well what I owed her! She herself, on the morning of that May 8, had she not come to deposit in the chalice of my soul her Jesus, the Flower of the fields and the Lily of the valleys?

At evening of that beautiful day, Papa, taking his little queen's hand, made his way toward Carmel; and I saw my Pauline become the spouse of Jesus: I saw her with her veil white like mine, and her crown of roses. My joy was without bitterness; I hoped to rejoin her soon and wait at her side for heaven...

I was not insensible to the family feast prepared at Les Buissonnets. The pretty watch my father gave me pleased me greatly; and yet my happiness was tranquil; nothing could trouble my intimate peace. Finally, night ended that beautiful day; for the most radiant days are followed by darkness: only the day of the first, eternal communion of the homeland will be without evening!


* * *

The next day was covered in my eyes with a certain veil of melancholy. The beautiful dresses, the gifts I had received did not fill my heart! Jesus alone henceforth could satisfy me, and I sighed only after the blessed moment when I would receive him a second time. I made that second communion on the feast of the Ascension, and I had the happiness of kneeling at the holy Table between my father and my beloved Marie. My tears flowed again with ineffable sweetness; I remembered and repeated to myself unceasingly the words of Saint Paul: "It is no longer I who live; it is Jesus who lives in me!" From that second visit of Our Lord, I aspired only to receive him. Alas! The feasts then seemed very far apart to me!...

On the eve of those happy days, Marie prepared me as she had done for my first communion. Once, I remember, she spoke to me of suffering, telling me that instead of making me walk by that way, the good God, no doubt, would always carry me like a little child. Those words came back to my mind after my communion the following day, and my heart was inflamed with a keen desire for suffering, with the intimate certainty that a great number of crosses were reserved for me. Then my soul was flooded with consolations such as I have not had since to the same degree.

Then my soul was flooded with such consolations that I have had none like them in all my life. Suffering became my attraction; I found charms in it that ravished me, without, though, knowing them well yet.

I felt another great desire: to love only the good God, to find joy in him alone. Often, during my thanksgivings, I repeated that passage of the Imitation: "O Jesus! Ineffable sweetness, change for me into bitterness all the consolations of earth." Those words issued from my lips without effort; I pronounced them like a child who repeats, without understanding much, what a friendly person suggests to him. Later I will tell you, Mother, how Our Lord delighted to realize my desire; how he was always, he alone, my ineffable sweetness. If I spoke of it to you now, I would have to anticipate my life as a young girl; and I have many details still to give you about my life as a child.


* * *

A short time after my first communion, I entered retreat again for my confirmation. I had prepared myself with great care for the visit of the Holy Spirit; I could not understand that one would not pay great attention to the reception of that sacrament of love. The ceremony not having taken place on the marked day, I had the consolation of seeing my solitude prolonged a little. Ah! How joyful my soul was! Like the Apostles, I waited happily for the promised Comforter; I rejoiced to soon be a perfect Christian, and to have upon my forehead, eternally engraved, the mysterious cross of that ineffable sacrament.

I did not feel the impetuous wind of the first Pentecost, but rather that gentle breeze whose murmur the prophet Elijah heard on Mount Horeb. That day, I received the strength to suffer — strength I very much needed, for the martyrdom of my soul was to begin soon after.


* * *

Those delightful, unforgettable feasts past, I had to take up my life as a boarder again. I succeeded well in my studies and easily retained the sense of things; I only had extreme difficulty learning word for word. Nevertheless, in catechism, my efforts were crowned with success. The chaplain called me his little doctor, no doubt because of my name of Thérèse.

During recreation, I very often amused myself by contemplating from afar the joyful games of my companions, giving myself over to serious reflections. That was my favorite distraction. I also invented a game that pleased me greatly: I carefully sought out the poor little birds found dead under the large trees, and I buried them honorably, all in the same cemetery, in the shade of the same patch of grass. At other times I told stories, and often older pupils mixed among my audience; but soon our wise teacher forbade me to continue my trade as an orator, wanting to see us run and not discourse.

At that time I chose as friends two little girls of my age; but how narrow the heart of creatures is! One of them was obliged to return to her family for some months; during her absence I was careful not to forget her, and I showed great joy at seeing her again. Alas! I obtained only an indifferent look! My friendship was not understood; I felt it keenly, and from then on I no longer begged for so inconstant an affection. Still, the good God has given me so faithful a heart that, when it has loved, it loves always; so I continue to pray for that companion, and I love her still.

Seeing several pupils attach themselves particularly to one of the teachers, I wanted to imitate them, but could not succeed. O happy failure! What great evils you spared me! How much I thank the Lord for having made me find only bitterness in the friendships of earth! With a heart like mine, I would have let myself be taken and my wings clipped; then how could I have "flown and rested"? How can a heart given over to human affection unite itself intimately to God? I feel that it is not possible. I have seen so many souls, seduced by that false light, hurl themselves into it like poor butterflies and burn their wings, then come back wounded to Jesus, the divine fire that burns without consuming!

Ah! I know it — Our Lord knew me too weak to expose me to the temptation; no doubt I would have burned myself entirely in the deceiving light of creatures; but it never shone in my eyes. There, where strong souls meet joy and detach themselves from it out of fidelity, I met only affliction. Where then is my merit in not having given myself over to those fragile attachments, when I was preserved from them only by a sweet effect of God's mercy? Without him, I acknowledge it, I could have fallen as low as Saint Mary Magdalene; and the profound word of the divine Master to Simon the Pharisee resounds in my soul with great sweetness. Yes, I know it, "he to whom less is forgiven, loves less." But I also know that Jesus forgave me more than he forgave Saint Mary Magdalene. Ah! How I would like to be able to express what I feel! Here at least is an example that will translate my thought a little:

I suppose that the son of a skillful doctor meets on his path a stone that makes him fall and break a limb. His father comes promptly, lifts him up with love, treats his wounds, using for that purpose all the resources of his art; and soon his son, completely cured, shows him his gratitude. No doubt that child is right to love so good a father; but here is another supposition:
