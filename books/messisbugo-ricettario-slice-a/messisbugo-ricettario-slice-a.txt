## Dedication

**Cristoforo di Messisbugo (d. 1548)** — who he was: steward
(*scalco*) of the House of Este at Ferrara, and a cook of the
Italian Renaissance. The verifiable record (Wikipedia, "Cristoforo
di Messisbugo") gives it plain: from 1524 to 1548 he served at the
courts of Alfonso I d'Este and his son Ercole II at Ferrara, where
he ordered many lavish banquets; greatly esteemed as a master of
ceremonies, he was made count palatine on 20 January 1533 by the
Holy Roman Emperor Charles V.

The circumstances of his death: he died in 1548, and his book —
*Banchetti, compositioni di vivande e apparecchio generale* — was
published posthumously at Ferrara in 1549. He is buried in the
church of the monastery of Sant'Antonio in Polesine. A *Libro novo*
attributed to him, printed at Venice in 1564 long after his death,
is largely a repetition of his *Banchetti* recipes.

Why his work is vital — proved, not praised: the record gives two
hard facts. First, the first known reference in Italy to the
preparation of Beluga sturgeon caviar — from the Po, where the
great sturgeon abounded in his century — is in Messisbugo's books:
he described serving it and preserving it. Second, the *Banchetti*
is one of the first cookbooks of early modern cookery in Italy
(Goodreads' redaction of the 2023 Kindle selection), addressed to
those who prepare princely feasts, giving not only the 323 recipes
but the menus of his official Este banquets, and treating
logistics, decor, and kitchen equipment besides. Some of the dishes
he described survive today in the Ferrara country. A master who
wrote down how a Renaissance court actually ate — and whose caviar
passage is the first of its kind in his country — needs no praise;
the facts are the monument.

Its relation to this rescue: the Boneyard rescues the working
knowledge of dead masters, and a master cook's working knowledge
is among the most perishable of all — it dies with the kitchen.
Messisbugo's recipes were already being repeated without him by
1564; the series sets out to give the master himself back his
voice, recipe by recipe, in plain English a working cook can read.
This slice — the pasta book's table, its introduction, and its run
of dishes for fat days and lean — is the first serving.

Where a reader goes next: the 1549 Ferrara edition itself, in the
Internet Archive's digitization (identifier `bub_gb_9yA8AAAAcAAJ`)
— the source of this rendering; the Wikipedia article "Cristoforo
di Messisbugo" for the life in brief; and Charles A. Potter's 2023
Kindle selection, *Cooking Renaissance Italian Food: 51 Redacted
Recipes from the 1549 Banchetti*, for a modern cook's redaction of
51 of the 323 recipes.

## How to read this book

Messisbugo writes for a steward's kitchen. His words are kept in
Italian in parentheses at first use:

- *libbra* — the pound (about 12 ounces in Ferrara's measure);
  *oncia* — the ounce; *meza* — half.
- *pignatta, tiella, orna* — pot, shallow pan, big earthenware jar.
- *testo* — the domed cover set over a pan with coals on top: the
  period's oven-lid.
- *buttiero* — butter; *zucchero* — sugar; *mele* — honey (not
  apples); *sabba/saba* — boiled grape must; *agrefto* — verjuice,
  the sour juice of unripe grapes; *acqua rosata* — rose water.
- *da grasso / da magro* — "of fat" / "of lean": feast-day food
  (meat, butter, eggs) and fast-day food (fish, oil, vegetables).
- *imbandire* — to dish up, to set on the board.

Bracketed notes in the form [Editor's note] are the editor's, not
Messisbugo's. Where the scan defeats the reading, the English
marks it [obscured in the scan] rather than guessing.

## Note on the Italian text

The source is the raw OCR of a 1549 printing, and it shows its age:
the long *s* prints as *f*, *u* and *v* interchange, and the
digitization mangles letters throughout (*zucchero* as *Zuccharo*,
*pevere* as *Peuere*). The translation was made by reading through
the noise to the Italian beneath; uncertain readings are flagged,
never smoothed over. Two transmission scars are left visible: the
table's page-references print as a column of *cor.* (for *carta*,
leaf) with the numbers mostly lost to the scan, and the slice ends
where the scan's duplicate gathering begins — the dangling *A FARE*
at the slice's end is the cut edge, not a recipe. A duplicate-scan
check of the whole OCR file (every 40-word window hashed) found no
repeated passage at the positions reported for this copy, so the
slice is translated once, straight through.

## The master's introduction to the recipes

Having in the preceding account described many different banquets,
in which there came many dishes composed variously of diverse
things — to give clear notice of the making of every great [feast]
— it is still necessary that I now show how all the aforesaid
dishes are to be composed, and many others that will be added to
them: which in all, I believe, will be no fewer than three hundred
and thirty, all in their order. By which, without any doubt, one
may fittingly and honorably serve every great banquet of whatever
prince one wishes — always adding its opportune [dishes, and the
seasonings (?) — obscured in the scan], which must then be varied
according as the quantity of what one wishes to make requires.
Where it is to be known that if there were any gentleman [who
ordered the banquets — obscured] he could make [them] with less of
sugars [and with half the (?) — obscured] things — in which,
however, all must [keep — obscured] that there always be the
seasoning in every [dish]. And [there is — obscured] also [for]
anyone [who would — obscured] in the compositions [and it is
needful — obscured] that it can also be made with [less — obscured;
the sense: the dishes can be made more cheaply].

[White tarts (?), or white savors (?), with the others — obscured]
in their own color will be stained. All the others without sugar in
every way can be made; yet it is true that, as in the [genoese
(?) — obscured], in the [supped (?) — obscured], in the double
puff-pastes, and the puffed [pastries], in many other dishes the
[rich seasoning — obscured] is more fitting. Yet whoever wished to
limit the expense could use honey [for sugar — obscured] in the
compositions — putting, however, on top of all the sugar where it
is fitting; although many require first the honey, then the sugar,
as in [the recipes — obscured] will clearly be made manifest. Of
which first [I shall begin to treat — the sentence breaks off in
the scan].

[Editor's note — in plain words: The banquets are described; now
the recipes — all three hundred and thirty of them, in order, so
any prince's feast can be served from this book. And for the
cost-conscious steward: honey can stand in for sugar inside the
dish, so long as sugar goes on top where it shows — though many
dishes want honey first and sugar after, as the recipes will show.]

## The recipes

### To preserve eels (*cevali*), and sea-bream (*orate*), for Lent, or another time

Take the eels from mid-September to mid-November, and wash them
well whole, without scaling or opening them; then cook them on the
embers over the grill, so they are not too cooked. Lay them on a
board with salt and fennel, and leave them so for a night. Have a
jar (*orna*) where you will lay them one upon another — putting,
however, between them salt and fennel. Pour over them strong
vinegar, enough to cover them. When you want to serve them, warm
them in the pan. [A passage follows, too obscured in the scan to
render: it concerns boiling vinegar — "two pounds" can be read —
and half [a measure] of honey, to be left to cool before [use];
then the remark that eels are of diverse kinds, but for this
purpose [certain — obscured] are the best.]

The sea-bream are to be opened a little, so you take out the guts,
then throw a little salt on them. Then fry them, and let them cool.
When they are fried, then [treat them — obscured] in the same way
as is said above of the eels.

*The end.* [So the print marks the close of the preserving section.]

[Editor's note — in plain words: Autumn eels, grilled whole and
laid down in vinegar with salt and fennel for Lent; fried
sea-bream kept the same way. The vinegar-and-honey pickle that
follows is lost to the scan where it matters most.]

### [To preserve quinces — and peaches; the title lost in the scan]

[The recipe's heading did not survive the digitization; the
identification is inferred from the closing note, which speaks of
peaches (*persiche*). What follows is translated as printed.]

[Take the quinces — obscured] until they are done. Then take them
[out — obscured] and lay them out on a board, putting another
board on top with weights to press them; leave them so, pressed,
for a day. Then take a pot and put them in with enough *saba* to
cover the things, and give them a good boil in the *saba* — yet so
they do not fall apart — and put them neatly in a jar, and pour
over them the said *saba* they boiled in, and leave them so for
twenty days. Then take another pot, and take them out of the said
jar, and put them in with honey and *saba*, cinnamon, pepper, and
ginger, and saffron, according to the quantity you want to make —
and [sage (?) — obscured] — and boil them again a little with the
aforesaid things. Then return them to the aforesaid jar with the
said flavor, and they are done. And if the expense does not trouble
you, boil them this last time in honey alone. — Note that the
peaches are not to be pressed.

[Editor's note — in plain words: Quinces pressed under boards,
boiled in grape-must, rested twenty days, then boiled again with
honey, must, and spices — or in honey alone if money is no object.
Peaches get the same keeping, but are never pressed.]

### To make a Hebrew-style dish (*vivanda alla hebraica*), of meat

Take three pounds of veal from the fleshy part, well trimmed of
skins and sinews, and mince it finely. Have a handful of oily
herbs, and raisins, and a little of every spice, and mix everything
together. Then take the yolks of four hard eggs — the yolk only,
whole. Then take half the aforesaid composition in the form of
half-balls [the print adds *da uento* — obscure], and put the four
aforesaid yolks in the middle of them; then close them together in
the form of a whole ball, and set it to cook in good broth with
minced oily herbs, and a little nutmeg, and saffron enough to give
it color. Then, when cooked, serve one or two per dish, according
to the size you make them, with the said broth — with slices of
soaked bread, if you wish.

[Editor's note — in plain words: A great minced-veal ball with four
whole hard yolks hidden in its heart, poached in herb broth with
nutmeg and saffron, served in its broth over soaked bread.]

### [Butter vermicelli, pressed through tin]

[The recipe's heading is lost to the scan; what survives begins
mid-method.] ...many times, and the last washing shall be in rose
water with [obscured]. Then have a tube of tin — or of hollow wood
— or a [pierced vessel — obscured] that has a flat bottom with
[small] holes [obscured]; fill it [with the butter — understood],
and have a wooden [plunger] that goes well-bored in the said tube,
and drive it into the said tube; and the butter will come out
through the holes in the bottom, and they will be vermicelli —
which you will go dishing on plates, giving them sugar on top.

[Editor's note — in plain words: Washed butter — the last washing
in rose water — forced through a pierced tin tube with a wooden
plunger, coming out in worms like vermicelli, sugared and dished.
The ancestor of the potato ricer, four and a half centuries early.]

### To make a cheese in the Castilian fashion (*formaggio alla catellana*)

Take lean meat, as large or small as you want it, and cut it small
and square like dice; then put it to boil in water, and when it is
nearly cooked take the meat out and put it to drain — and see that
it is well rid of the water. Then put it in a pot with milk, and
boil it; and when it is well done, drain it from the milk as much
as you can. Then take a white cloth and put it [in it — obscured],
and squeeze it very well, and then put it in press with a good
weight on top. It is done. If you want to give it shape with the
[mold (?) — obscured], it is up to you.

[Editor's note — in plain words: No cheese at all — diced meat
boiled, boiled again in milk, wrung in a cloth, and pressed under
a weight into a "cheese" in the Spanish style: a headcheese,
dressed for the board.]

### To make preserves of melon rinds, or *scocie*, or squashes, or turnips

Take your quantity of the aforesaid things, as you see fit; peel
them, and put them to soak in vinegar with salt for fifteen or
twenty days. Then take them out of the vinegar and put them in a
pot with water, and give them a good boil; then take them out and
throw them into another pot of fresh water [and — the recipe
breaks off in the scan].

[Editor's note — in plain words: The pickle begins like all
keeping pickles — salt-vinegar for a fortnight, then blanching.
The finishing — the second half of the method — did not survive
the scan. *Scocie* is as printed; likely another gourd.]

### *Mantigilia* [butter churned in a kid-skin]

You must have a kid-skin, flayed whole, which with various washings
be well cleaned — with spicery in the water, so it takes a good
smell. When the skin is ready, take milk that is not [skimmed (?) —
obscured] and put it in the said skin through the hole of the
[neck (?) — obscured], and then tie and close the said hole; and
you must work the said milk well in the said skin, always treading
it and [shaking it — obscured]. If it is very hot weather, make it
at night in a cool place. Goat's milk is the best. When you have
worked it an hour, untie one of the kid's feet and let the
*mantigilia* come out; it will be like butter. Put it in a pot with
fresh water, making dishes of it with sugar on top. — For this
dish it is very hard to make a great quantity for a great banquet;
make everyone understand that this is *manteca* [butter — the
print's word is as given]. Take [a measure of] milk, and two
thirds (?) of butter, and put it in the said skin, and work and
beat it until the butter whitens and takes the smell from the
spices through the skin; then make it come out through the foot,
and put it in water [and — obscured]. Make [the dishes (?) —
obscured], for it is all of one [kind (?) — obscured]; and when
you have dressed it, put sugar on top.

[Editor's note — in plain words: A whole kid-skin, washed sweet
with spices, is the churn: milk worked in it for an hour — by night
in hot weather — and the butter drawn off through a leg, washed in
fresh water, sugared, and dished. Goat's milk best. For a crowd,
stretch it: work butter itself in the skin till it takes the
spicery through the leather.]

### Dairy dishes of several kinds (*latticini di più sorti*)

#### Drawn from milk (*cavi di latte*) [clotted cream]

Take good milk, according to the quantity you want to make — [a
measure — obscured] will make eight or ten [dishes] — and put it in
a cauldron on a clear fire that takes no smoke, and keep stirring
[it] until it comes to a boil. Then have ready the [pans (?) —
obscured]; put the said milk in, and let it cool; and when it is
cold, it will make a *tellina* [a skin, a clot], which will be
[the clotted cream — understood], which you will take off from
above, and go putting it in little white dishes. Then return the
milk to the fire; do the same three times — for then nothing more
will come out. If one *tellina* is not enough in the dish, put two
or three, as needed. When you send them to table, put sugar on top.
And note that the milk must not be from a cow that is fresh of
milk for [nine (?) days — obscured], nor must the milk be skimmed.

[Editor's note — in plain words: Clotted cream, Ferrara 1549:
scald unskimmed milk over a smokeless fire, cool it, lift the
crust, repeat thrice, sugar it, and dish it in little white
plates.]

#### Honey-milk (*latte melle*)

Take the cream of the milk, according to the quantity you want to
make — a flask will make eight or ten dishes. Put it in a [churn
(?) — obscured] that has a [pierced (?) — obscured] covered lid
with holes (?); have a staff with a round at the end, like those
with which butter is made, and work it [obscured]. Let it stand so
a little, so the *latte melle* is born on top; and with a [ladle
(?) — obscured] go taking it out and putting it in a pot in a cool
place, little by little; and so keep working the staff several
times, keeping the aforesaid order as it is born. Then, when you
want to send it to table, dish it in little plates, giving it sugar
on top.

[Editor's note — in plain words: Cream worked with a butter-staff
till the "honey-milk" rises — the sweet first working — lifted off
as it forms, kept cool, and sugared for the table.]

### To make onions in the pan (*cipolle in tiella*)

Take your onions; peel them, then boil them in water. When they
are cooked, drain them from the water; then flour them and put them
in a *tiella* with good oil. Fry them, putting a *testo* on top, so
they come well colored; then dish them, putting a little [savor (?)
— obscured] on top. And instead of oil you may fry in fresh
butter; they will be better.

### To make fried squashes (*zucche fritte*)

Take tender squashes, not too large; when you have scraped them,
cut them in thin slices and lay them out, throwing salt on them so
they take a little salt. Then fry them in oil or butter, as you
wish — flouring them first, however. When they are fried, dish
them, putting on top [fennel (?) — obscured] and *agresto*.

### To make fresh green beans in the pan (*fasoletti freschi in tegole*)

Take the pods of the beans when they are tender; top them. Then
put them to cook in boiling water, and at once they will cook.
When they are cooked, put them to drain, with salt on top; then fry
them in oil or butter. Putting them in the pan, put a little
vinegar, pepper, and [obscured].

### Egg *ritortelli*, called fried eggs (*ritortelli d'uova, chiamati uova fritte*)

Take four (?) eggs; beat them with salt and a little water beaten
in, and throw them in a small pan to cook with hot butter — so the
bottom of the pan is greased — and [make] the frittata very thin.
As it begins to set, have good grated cheese and a little pepper
mixed with the cheese, and throw it on top; and in the middle of
the frittata lay a sprig of tender rosemary, and with the spatula
turn the said frittata in the guise of a *cialdone* [a rolled
wafer]; then dish it on plates with sugar on top — and put no
fewer than three per plate.

[Editor's note — in plain words: A paper-thin frittata, cheesed
and peppered, laid with rosemary down its middle, rolled like a
wafer, sugared, three to a plate.]

### To make six *grostate*

Take six small [dishes (?) — obscured] and put in the bottom, per
plate, one ounce of butter and a third of an ounce (?) of sugar,
spread. Then have thirty thin square slices of toasted bread,
fried in a pound of butter; put five slices per plate. Then have
half a pound of rich cheese, taken in slices (?), and put it on
the bread — that is, [so much] per plate — and put on top one
ounce of sugar and two [ounces (?)] of butter; give it a good
heating with the *testo*. When cooked, you may give another half
ounce of sugar per plate.

The said *grostate* you may make without the bread slices, keeping
the rest as above; also you may fry the cheese in slices in the
pan with fresh butter — and, dished, put sugar on top.

[Editor's note — in plain words: Toasts fried in butter, laid with
rich cheese, sugared top and bottom, browned under the testo lid:
the Renaissance cheese toast, six to the making — or the cheese
alone, fried and sugared.]

### To make a *butirata*

Take a *tiella* or a silver plate, and put in the bottom two ounces
of fresh butter and an ounce and a half of well-dissolved sugar
[and — the recipe breaks off in the scan].

### To make forty-eight egg *ritortelli* Florentine, full and empty (*ritortelli d'uova alla fiorentina, pieni et vuoti*)

Take the pan with a little fresh butter; and when it is hot, have
a beaten egg; make a thin little frittata and turn it into a dish.
Then have sugar and ground cinnamon, and raisins, and lay them
lightly on the frittatina. Then sprinkle it with juice of orange
or *agresto*. Then roll it in the guise of a *cialdone*, and so
make four per dish; cover them with another dish, on the cinders,
until you want to send them to table. When you want to send them
to table, put on top sugar and cinnamon and juice of oranges. And
to make [them] for ten dishes there will go in all forty eggs, and
of butter two and a half pounds, and of sugar a pound and a half,
and of raisins a pound, of cinnamon half [an ounce (?)]; and
inside and on top; and thirty oranges, or *agresto* — and putting
on top sugar and cinnamon, or juice of oranges, or *agresto*.

[Editor's note — in plain words: Forty-eight little rolled
frittatas for ten dishes — some filled (sugar, cinnamon, raisins,
orange juice), some empty — kept warm under a cover on the coals
till serving. The shopping list is given to the ounce.]

### To make a double frittata, filled (*frittada doppia, piena*)

Make a frittata of one egg in the aforesaid manner; put first in
the bottom of the dish a little sugar and cinnamon, butter, and
orange juice. Then put the said frittatina in the dish and load it
lightly in the aforesaid manner; then make another and put it on
the first; on top put sugar and cinnamon and orange juice. And you
may so make to the number of four, one on the other, filling them
in the aforesaid manner; put it on the hot cinders, covered, till
you want to send it to table — putting then on top sugar and
cinnamon and orange juice. And to make ten of these frittate there
will go the same stuff as went to make the forty *ritortelli*
aforesaid.

### To make a dish of stuffed hard eggs (*uova dure piene*)

Take ten (?) fresh eggs and cook them in their shells so they
become hard; peel them, split them in half lengthwise, and take
the yolks out neatly. Then take four of the said yolks, and a good
piece of hard grated cheese, half an ounce of raisins, up to
fifteen (?) pine nuts, oily herbs, a little ground cinnamon and
pepper, and a little saffron to give color; mince everything well
together with two ounces of sugar and a little rose water. Then
take the white of the eggs and fill the holes where the yolks were
with this composition; put them in a *tiella* with no fire below,
and give them a heating on top with the *testo*; dish them. Then
take three slices of crumb of bread soaked in *saba* or *agresto*,
and mince them with a little raisins, an egg yolk, sugar, and
cinnamon; pass them through the sieve (?) tempered with *saba* or
*agresto*, and bring to a boil; then put it on the said savor. And
if you don't want this expense, fry the eggs, when you have cut
them, in oil; and, dished, put on top sugar, cinnamon, and
*agresto*.

[Editor's note — in plain words: Deviled eggs, 1549: the yolks
pounded with cheese, raisins, pine nuts, herbs, cinnamon, pepper,
saffron, sugar, and rose water, piped back into the whites,
browned under the testo lid, and sauced with a bread-thickened
saba — or, cheaply, fried and sugared.]

### To make a dish of eggs French-style (*uova alla francese*)

Take a silver plate or a little *tiella*, and put half a pound of
butter in. Then have ten fresh eggs, and breaking them one by one
into the said vessel, give them gently the fire below and above
with the *testo*. Then put on top sugar and cinnamon, and send
them to table. This dish will be most honorable and substantial —
besides the yolks alone [so the print; the sense: "apart from the
yolks themselves," i.e., even before counting the yolks].

[Editor's note — in plain words: Ten eggs broken into half a pound
of butter in a silver dish, fired above and below, sugared and
cinnamonned: the rich man's fried eggs.]

### Sturgeon meatballs (*polpette di sturione*), for ten dishes

Take the flesh of the sturgeon and make it in slices, as are made
those of meat, a finger thick; beat them very well with the back
of your knife. Then sprinkle salt on them. Then have oily herbs
minced very finely, with two or three egg yolks, half a pound of
well-cleaned raisins, ginger more than anything, and pepper, and
ground cloves, saffron, and cinnamon; season them as seems good to
you, and put with them also half a pound of butter; incorporate
everything together in a vessel. Then spread the said *battuto* on
your meatballs; roll them as the others are done, and put them on
the spit to cook. When they are cooked, put them in a pot with half
a pound of melted butter and *agresto* and two ounces of sugar;
put them [thus — obscured] in dishes; throw on top [obscured]. So
you may make of other fishes, but [without the bone (?) —
obscured].

[Editor's note — in plain words: Sturgeon — the same fish whose
roe gives the caviar of Messisbugo's famous passage — beaten flat,
spread with a spiced forcemeat (ginger foremost), rolled, spit-
roasted, and finished in butter, verjuice, and sugar. For ten
dishes; other firm fishes may serve.]

### *Tomaselle*, for lean days (*tomaselle da magro*)

Take six ounces of [pike (?) — obscured] or [obscured] of pike,
cleaned of the bones, and mince them very well. [Take] six ounces
of raisins, six ounces of sugar, eight egg yolks, two ounces of
ground cinnamon, thirteen (?) ounces of good [fresh (?) —
obscured] cheese, five ounces of salt, and little saffron;
incorporate everything well minced, as is done for the others;
flour them very well. Then fry them in fresh butter; they want to
be somewhat done. And if you made the pastry (*sfoglia*) a little
thick, it would not be out of place. Dough (*impastata*) made with
white flour, rose water, butter, egg yolks, sugar.

[Editor's note — in plain words: Lean-day fish cakes — minced pike
bound with raisins, sugar, yolks, cinnamon, cheese, and saffron,
floured and fried in butter — with the master's aside that a
little pastry jacket wouldn't hurt: flour, rose water, butter,
yolks, sugar.]

[A FARE — *so the slice ends, on a dangling recipe-heading: the
cut edge of the scan's next gathering, not translated.*]

## Glossary of measures and pantry words

- *agresto/agrefto* — verjuice: the sour juice of unripe grapes.
- *battuto* — the minced seasoning-mixture; forcemeat.
- *buttiero* — butter.
- *cialdone* — a large thin rolled wafer; the model for rolling.
- *fiasco* — flask; a liquid measure.
- *libbra* — pound (about 12 ounces, Ferrara); *oncia* — ounce;
  *meza* — half.
- *mele* — honey (in this book; not apples).
- *orna* — a big earthenware storage jar.
- *pignatta* — a cooking pot; *tiella/tegghia* — a shallow pan;
  *testo* — the domed lid with coals on top.
- *sabba/saba* — grape must boiled down to a syrup.
- *acqua rosata* — rose water.
- *da grasso / da magro* — for fat days (meat, butter, eggs) / for
  lean days (fish, oil, vegetables).
- *imbandire* — to dish up for the table.
