Dedication and introduction



The man

Ulrich Jasper Seetzen was born on 30 January 1767 at Jever, in German Frisia, and died in Yemen in September 1811. He studied medicine at Göttingen, but his real bent was toward natural history, geography, languages, and the patient gathering of facts. In 1802 he accepted a government post at Jever; in the same season of his life he gave himself to exploration.

He travelled down the Danube, through Constantinople and Asia Minor, and reached Aleppo, where he remained from November 1803 to April 1805 studying Arabic. That study was not ornament. It was equipment. He meant to go where Europeans seldom went, to listen before he judged, and to write down what he saw while the dust of the road was still on the page.



The circumstances

Seetzen's great journal belongs to the years when the map of Syria, Palestine, Transjordan, Arabia Petraea, and Egypt was still being corrected by men who walked the ground. He made the circuit of the Dead Sea in the winter of 1806–1807. Later he crossed Arabia, reached Mecca in October 1809 as the pilgrim Musa al-Hakim, and pushed on into Yemen.

In September 1811 he left Mocha hoping to reach Muscat. Two days later he was found dead. The standard account says he was allegedly poisoned by his guides on orders from the imam of Sanaa. That word allegedly must stay where it belongs until the evidence is pinned harder. What is not in doubt is the loss: a trained observer died on the road, many of his papers and collections never reached Europe, and the full diary waited more than forty years before Kruse and his fellow editors brought the Reisen into print at Berlin in 1854–1859.



Why this witness matters

These pages matter because Seetzen does not merely perform adventure. He tests stories. A guide swears he has seen asphalt welling from the rock; Seetzen cross-examines the tale and finds it hollow. Local men describe fruit that looks fair and turns to ash; Seetzen opens the fruit, names the plant, and separates wonder from botany. He is devout enough to notice a cave of David and exact enough to ask whether the cave is large enough for the scene assigned to it.

That is why the diary still deserves readers. It is an early scientific traveler's record from country that has been described ever since by soldiers, pilgrims, archaeologists, tourists, and partisans. Seetzen goes earlier than most of them, on foot and on muleback, with a notebook.



Why it belongs on this shelf

Robert Tilford's Rare & Obscure Books series exists for workers whose books were left unfinished, unpublished, scattered, or stranded in a language most readers cannot use. Seetzen fits that rescue pattern in travel dress: killed far from home, his collections partly lost, his great work posthumous, and his English afterlife reduced for generations to the slim 1810 Brief Account of the Countries Adjoining the Lake of Tiberias, the Jordan, and the Dead Sea — a useful pamphlet, but not the diaries.

This volume gives back the witness, not just the legend. It does not claim to be the first English Seetzen, and it does not call the 1810 pamphlet a translation of the 1854 Reisen. It says only what the work can bear: here is a portion of the diary, newly Englished from the German, with the dates kept in order and the old place-names tied to the names a modern reader can find.



Signposts for the reader

For the life, the old Encyclopaedia Britannica article on Seetzen remains a public-domain backbone. Edward Robinson, writing in 1841, called him “judicious, enterprising and indefatigable” — praise from a later traveller who knew the country and the cost of getting it right. Inside the Reisen itself, Kruse's editorial work is both a help and a warning: the diaries reached print through other men's hands, after the author's death, and must be read with that in mind.



Place-names in this installment

Seetzen writes Ain Dschíddy for the spring now usually written Ein Gedi, the Engedi of the Hebrew Bible. His todtes See is the Dead Sea. El Pherdéis is the cone of Herodium south of Bethlehem. Mûdscheb is the Arnon, the modern Wadi Mujib. Kárrak is Karak; el Belka is the Balqa plateau east of the Dead Sea; Gór el Száphiá is Ghor es-Safi at the sea's southern end; Dschibbal Üsdúmm is the salt mountain of Usdum. Where his spelling carries evidence, the first use gives the modern form with Seetzen's form beside it.

Journey to the west side of the Dead Sea and to Jericho

From vol. 2 of the 1854 Reisen, printed pp. 217–236. This first installment ends at Ein Gedi, after Seetzen's inquiry into the so-called apples of Sodom. The journey northward along the shore, and the later crossing to the east side, remain for the next slices.



15 December 1806

I have already said that a man of Bethlehem offered to accompany me round the Dead Sea. At first his demands seemed too high, and I broke off the negotiation with him. But no other road was left to me, so I resolved to yield to necessity and treat with him again. Soon after my arrival in Bethlehem I agreed with him that, with three Bedouin, he would guide me round the sea for a fixed sum, and that he would also supply two horses. Because I knew that few provisions, or none, would be had on the whole road, I had already laid in a store in Jerusalem of biscuit, olives, and the like, and here I increased it with flour, oil, and grape syrup — dibbs. I also had barley bought for the horses. Our departure was set for 12 December.

On the day before, the air was still cloudy, though clearer than for several days, and I hoped for good travelling weather. To guard against losing my money on the road, I handed it over to the vice-procurator.

Bodrus — that was the Catholic's name — had assured me in Jerusalem that he had seen the place on the shore of the Dead Sea where the asphalt wells up. The place, he said, was on the east side, about opposite Ein Gedi (Ain Dschíddy); there one constantly saw the asphalt trickling like oil out of a rock and, as it ran down, gradually forming a thick firm crust. But he had told an untruth. Now, when he knew that it would soon be discovered, he confessed that he had never been at the place he called Ain el-Hommar, “the asphalt spring.” Besides, he added, it was impossible for anyone to come near enough to examine it, because it rose at the foot of an immense steep wall of rock, and only birds or good swimmers could get there; yet with a telescope, from the west shore of the sea, people had really seen the liquid asphalt dripping out.

That was to replace one untruth with another still more absurd; for at that place the two shores may be four or five hours apart. Several Bethlehemites agreed with him in the report of the asphalt spring, but none of them was an eyewitness. I saw clearly enough that no reliance whatever could be placed on their statements.

On 11 December my three Bedouin of the Ta'amira (Taámer) tribe, which stays southeast of Bethlehem and in its neighbourhood, came and excused themselves: they could not set out the next day, because it was the first day of the Beiram festival, which as Muslims they had to keep solemnly. In the afternoon one of them came to me and explained that if I insisted on travelling to the eastern shore of the Dead Sea, he and his two companions would have to withdraw, because there was blood between them and a Bedouin tribe on the east side. But when he heard that I should look about for other companions, and when he considered that he did not want to lose the good pay, he consented again — on condition that we should make the stretch from the mouth of Wadi el-Hossa to the mouth of Wadi el-Karrak by night, so as to escape in the dark the danger they would certainly run if they had the misfortune to meet their enemies there. I granted him that condition.

Nevertheless they gave up the plan altogether, as I soon learned from Bodrus. To replace them, on 12 December he sent a messenger to the Shahalin (Scháhalín), who were supposed to be on friendly terms with the Arabs of the east side, and on the following day he hoped to receive their answer.

My intended journey had meanwhile become known in Bethlehem, and since Bedouin come here daily on visits, they too had heard of it. I had gone a little way from the monastery to gather plants. A Bedouin overtook me. It struck him that I was collecting mosses, and he asked me whether I was the man who wanted to make a journey round the Dead Sea. “I hope so,” I said. “The Arabs will murder you!” he went on. “The Arabs do not murder,” I answered; “I know them; I have already been in es-Salt and Karak.” “Look! Look!” he said to a couple of Muslims from Bethlehem who came up to us — “Ya mal'un! Ya khanzir!” [“You cursed one! You pig!” — insults, the 1854 note says, with which Muslims often address Christians] — pointing at me. I pretended not to hear it, and he went off, angry with me.

On 13 December Bodrus confessed to me that he had not sent to the Bedouin of the Shahalin tribe but to the Reshaide (Rscheide), and that he expected them that day or the next. In the evening the messenger came back with the unwelcome news that neither the Shahalin nor the Reshaide were inclined for this journey, because they did not know all the Bedouin tribes on the far side, and because there were now many dawars — clusters of tents — on the eastern shore, since at this season it is warmer and richer in grass than the higher mountains over which they wander in the hot part of the year, when hardly a Bedouin shows himself at the sea.

I was extremely annoyed at the unexpected obstacles set against my undertaking, and yet firmly resolved to carry it out. I was already willing to travel to Karak, to try whether men could be found there who would consent to accompany me. But Christmas was near, and I wished to be present at the feast. I therefore thought it better this time to travel only along the west side of the Dead Sea, which was bound up with fewer difficulties, and to put off the visit to the east side until after the feast. I soon came to an agreement with Bodrus about this, and on 15 December at half past eight we began our journey.

Instead of two horses, as our bargain had been, he brought me two mules. I kept silent about it, so as not to be delayed again, and because the difference was of little importance to me. A Bethlehemite, the owner of one mule, accompanied us; besides, two Bedouin of the Ta'amira soon joined us on the road, having been hired for this. The weather was very good.

Ein Gedi, which we decided to visit first, lies southeast of Bethlehem, about nine or ten hours away, on the shore of the Dead Sea. At first the road led over very stony mountains and valleys, though we passed some orchards and vineyards enclosed by stone walls, which owed their existence to the industry of the Bethlehemites.

Soon after, we overtook some Bedouin of the Reshaide, who stay in this mountain country west of Ein Gedi. They were driving an ass loaded with oil. They had heard of my journey, but did not know exactly which road we meant to take. Since my people wanted to hide it from them, they pretended that we meant to go to Hebron and from there to the south end of the Dead Sea. But they had not foreseen that we had to take the same road to reach Ein Gedi as these people did, and their untruth therefore did not stay hidden long. An old Reshaide soon came to a violent exchange of words with one of my Bedouin, named Khalil, a young sturdy fellow. The old man asserted that he knew beforehand it would be impossible for us to travel northward from Ein Gedi along the shore of the Dead Sea; Khalil contradicted him. It almost came to blows, and Khalil drew his khanjar before the others hurried up and pulled them apart. “By God!” Khalil said to me afterward, “if they had not separated us, I would have struck him down with my khanjar!” But these were the words of a braggart, as I later came to know him.

The Bethlehemite, Bodrus's companion, was so frightened by this scene that he resolved to return with his mule, because, as he said, he feared he would lose it on the road. Its load was therefore put on my mule, and I now saw the two horses promised me changed into a single mule. The Reshaide Bedouin then became our companions.

About half past ten we came to some Ta'amira Bedouin who were ploughing a few small fields. The Ta'amira live in tents, but originally they were peasants who inhabited a village at the foot of el-Fureidis (el Pherdéis), Beit Ta'mir (Bêt el Taámer), where the remains of a church and other things are still said to be found. They probably left it because of the tyranny of the government, since with tents they could flee more easily when necessary than when they had fixed dwellings. No doubt in several regions the Bedouin have increased at the expense of the peasants for the same reason.

The Ta'amira still carry on a good deal of farming. They dress like the other peasants, in that they do not wear the usual head-cloth of the true Bedouin, the keffiyeh, and they are generally more muscular and stronger than the latter. They also differ from them in their features, which resemble those of the other Syrian peasants. About thirty-five years ago, with the help of the Bethlehemites, they took a considerable piece of land from the Bedouin of the Htem tribe, who were forced to give it up and make peace.

Soon afterward we had, close beside us to the north, the striking conical mountain peak known here as el-Fureidis, on which are still found the ruins of an old fortress said once to have been defended most bravely for many years by the Knights of St. John. The Ta'amira make much of being descendants of these knights. That might in earnest be so, although those knights, if I remember rightly, had to vow perpetual chastity; one need only recall the known manner of life of the Knights of Malta in our own age. Yet I suspect that their tradition does not rest on historical facts carefully handed down orally from father to son, and still less on a written document, since no one of this tribe can read or write. Rather, the European monks probably gave rise to it — just as they presumably moved the Bedouin of the Obbediye tribe to regard themselves as descendants of the English.

El-Fureidis is a good two hours from Bethlehem. At some distance south of here they showed me Kasr el-Leimun, a ruined place where I noticed nothing but a few insignificant ruins. South of Fureidis likewise lies the ruined place Khureitun (Chreitûn), beside the wadi of the same name, in which a great labyrinthine cave is said to be found. The Bethlehemites call it el-Ma'sha, and it is three hours from Bethlehem. Franciscan monks visit it once every two or three years and call it the Labyrinth. It is said to be far larger than another cave north of el-Fureidis, two hours from Bethlehem, which the monks call David's Cave, but the Bethlehemites Magharet Sha'ul, “Saul's Cave,” or Umm et-Tala'a. It bears the last name because one is supposed to enjoy an extensive view from it, since it lies on a mountain.

The first great cave is without doubt the same that Pococke and Arvieux visited; the other is the one described by Nau and Troilo. Since the great cave of el-Ma'sha is near Ein Gedi, in whose inaccessible rock gorges David, afterward king of the Jews, stayed as a fugitive, it is highly probable that the famous scene in which he proved his unshakable loyalty to his king so strikingly took place in that cave — and by no means in the so-called David's Cave, because, all circumstances taken together, that one is surely too small for it. I was assured that the monks make use of a ball of twine so as not to lose themselves in the windings of the cave of Khureitun. [The 1854 note adds that rainy weather in that year hindered the monks and Seetzen from visiting this cave.]

Although the sharply pointed form of Fureidis might have led one to expect a basalt mountain, it consisted, like all the other mountains of this region, of limestone.

As we continued our journey without stopping there, we came upon some other Reshaide Bedouin who were busy watering their sheep and goats, drawing the water up from a well in leather vessels. They used a kind of song to mark the beat, as our sailors do when they weigh anchor or hoist sails.

The Reshaide are usually of small stature, lean, and of a brownish-yellow colour — all the marks of most Bedouin.

The mountains here showed no trace of cultivation and were covered with a quantity of flint and hornstone. These desert mountains seem to have served Bedouin as a dwelling place already several thousand years ago. At least I find it probable that the sheepfolds mentioned at one place in the history of Saul and David (1 Samuel 24:4) were either a dawar or a flock belonging to Bedouin. I suspect that this passage is not quite correctly rendered in Luther's translation. In this tract, compared with other regions of Palestine, one meets extremely few traces of former settlements, which makes it probable that fixed dwellings never existed here.

In the afternoon we reached a dawar of twelve tents, inhabited by Reshaide Bedouin. We turned in there and were entertained by them with coffee, fresh bread, and oil. They would have slaughtered a lamb for us, but Bodrus and I were still keeping the Christmas fast, in which we might eat neither meat nor butter, eggs, or milk dishes.

We stayed there about an hour and a half. Bodrus hired a couple of armed men from these people to accompany us farther, because those who had been in our company until then stayed here. We had hardly gone half an hour from the dawar, however, when they fell into a dispute with Bodrus over the agreed wage and turned back again. Only one of them at last let himself be persuaded to go with us; but soon afterward there was another exchange of words with him too, and Bodrus saw himself forced to grant his demand, because otherwise we would not have been safe in this region. My company now consisted, besides Bodrus, of the two Ta'amira and this Reshaide.

To the left of the road they showed me a place in a wadi where there is supposed to be a cave larger than David's Cave.

The ground became ever barer, more gravelly, more stony, and more unfruitful. Nevertheless there is no lack of game here; we saw five wild boars spring up before us, and farther on a gazelle, whose musk pouch has a pleasant smell.

Only about an hour and a half after sunset did we turn into a cave, where we stayed the night. It was roomy enough for us to tie our mules beside us, which was necessary for safety. A fire was made at once, and I was already waiting for coffee to be prepared and fresh bread baked, when they announced to me that there was no water here and that they had forgotten to fill the skin at the Bedouin's. That was an annoying circumstance. Fortunately I had brought some bread with me from the monastery, which pleased my Bedouin very much, because it was baked in the European manner and was therefore something new to them. Soon after our arrival it began to rain, and during the night there was lightning. We slept very quietly, however, lying round the fire, though after midnight we suffered somewhat from the cold.



16 December 1806

We left the cave the next morning at six o'clock. The country now became extraordinarily mountainous and was torn by a multitude of ravines. The nearer we came to the Dead Sea, the barer and wilder it grew. The rubble of hornstone and flint continued and was often blackish in colour. Some stones heaped together marked the grave of a Bedouin who must have stood in uncommon respect, for my three Bedouin approached it reverently and kissed a small round stone that lay on top.

In these unfruitful mountains I saw many holes in the earth. My Arabs assured me that they were inhabited by the jerboa (Dscherbúe). I did not convince myself of this by my own eyesight; their assurance was the answer to a question I put to them: what was in these holes? “Ground mice (far el-ard),” they said. “Are these not jerboas, which the Bedouin eat?” I asked further. “Of course! Of course!” they cried, showing their astonishment that I had heard of the jerboa. Concerning this animal I could never learn with complete certainty whether it is found on the west side of the Dead Sea or not.

On the mountains, among the few shrubs, grew two called Ruggel and Oddip, from which a kind of soda is burned — inferior, however, to the soda burned from some other shrubs that grow below on the sea shore. Oddip is a kind of Salicornia, or glasswort, with a whitish ash-grey bark.

After an hour and a half we reached the edge of the wild mountains that enclose the Dead Sea on both sides and that here formed immense high perpendicular walls of rock, in which an extremely troublesome rock path wound downward — a path that a man unacquainted with the region would hardly be able to find.

From the top of the rocky crest I enjoyed a wide and interesting view over the Dead Sea and its surroundings. Directly before us, beneath our feet, at the foot of the giant walls lay Ein Gedi, whose springs were marked by a group of trees and bushes, their green contrasting in the strangest way with the wild desolation round about. To our left they showed me a still more troublesome rock path leading down to it. A beautiful dark blue adorned the enormous mirror of the sea. Its edges were set with a silver hem of waves breaking on the strand, and similar silver streaks spread over its surface like garlands of white-blossoming flowers. Beyond the sea rose the steep mountains of the land of el-Belka and Karak, and a flat notch between them, almost straight opposite us, marked the mouth of the deep wild valley in which the Mudscheb, or Arnon [the modern Wadi Mujib], flows.

North of that, they showed me the mighty rock wall called Tur el-Hommara, at whose foot the asphalt was supposed to well out. Farther south of the Mudscheb we recognised Wadi el-Karrak; Karak itself, which is usually seen from here, was hidden from us by cloudy air. Still farther south we thought we noticed a considerable island. My people assured me that its nature was not known, because for want of boats no one could reach it. But my later observations, made on the spot itself, will prove that we were mistaken about this island, and that my people were in complete ignorance concerning it. Beyond that appeared Ghor es-Safi, and very distinctly the salt mountain Jebel Usdum (Dschibbal Üsdúmm), at the south end of the sea, about a day and a half's journey from Ein Gedi. Since the north end of the sea, which we could not see because of some projecting mountains, lies at an equal distance from here, one sees from this that Ein Gedi lies in the middle of the sea — though the road southward may be found somewhat longer than the road northward. Ein Gedi must therefore be drawn on the map of Palestine by Professor Paulus at the place where the brook is marked by which the town of Ziph lies, south of Mount Ziph — that is, straight east of Hebron.

Ein Gedi has kept its ancient name. I have no hesitation in taking the Engedi of the Hebrews for the present Ein Gedi, which in the Egyptian dialect of Arabic must be pronounced Ain Giddi. The Hebrew En is one with the Arabic Ain: for example, Ennon in Arabic is Ain Nun. En means a spring, as Ain does. This being granted, it is clear that the position of Engedi is wrongly given on the map mentioned, where it is drawn at the north end of the sea, north of Masada.

At Ein Gedi there is a small plain that draws a little toward the south; its breadth is five to ten minutes. Near Ein Gedi it is cultivated by the Reshaide, who have appropriated it. They grow a little wheat and durra there, and a quantity of cucumbers, which they carry to Jerusalem, where they are much sought after because they ripen a couple of weeks earlier than the cucumbers round Jerusalem.

Among the wild rock walls of the whole west side of the Dead Sea, and on the neighbouring mountains, besides gazelles and wild boars, many ibexes and wubber — hyraxes (Hyrax syriacus L.?) — keep, and, by the assurance of my Arabs, also panthers, hyenas, foxes, and other game. On this occasion I may be permitted to share some remarks on a couple of passages in the Hebrew writings.

Everyone will without doubt remember the place where David as a boy boasts before his king that he once slew a lion and a bear that had taken a sheep from him (1 Samuel 17:34), a heroic deed that a later poet expresses in this way (Sirach 47:3): “With lions he dealt as if he played with kids; and with bears, as if he sported with lambs.” But since now in all Palestine no lions, and in this region no bears either, are met with, it seems to me very probable that they were never met with here, because the physical character of Palestine seems to have changed little or not at all in the roughly three and a half thousand years that have since passed. I therefore suspect that it would be more fitting for the locality where this event happened to translate “panther” and “hyena” instead of lion and bear, since panthers and hyenas really still exist here and still hunt the flocks. As to the bear, this conjecture would be found still more probable — if the same holds in Hebrew as in Arabic — when one knows how alike el-dubb (the bear) and el-doba (the hyena) are in pronunciation. Instead of the hyena one might also assume a wolf, which is found in several regions of Palestine, and probably here too, and whose name, dib, also has much resemblance to the name of the bear.

In the interesting history of this same remarkable man, another passage tells (1 Samuel 24:2–3) that David withdrew from his king's pursuit into the stronghold of Engedi, on the rocks of the wild goats. This passage again is incorrectly translated; for everyone knows that there are no chamois in all Palestine. But since these wild rocks of Engedi are inhabited by ibexes, whose dwelling places always belong to the wildest on earth, and since David's place of refuge is characterised most aptly by that addition, I find it highly probable that instead of chamois one should say ibexes. The same holds for another passage in one of the finest hymns of this royal poet (Psalm 104:18), where it says: “The high mountains are a refuge for the wild goats, and the rocks for the conies.” I would translate it thus: “To the high mountain crest the ibexes flee, and the rock-dassie seeks its safety in the cleft of the rock.” [Seetzen's note thanks his friend Dr. Marpurg, a physician he met in Cairo, for the two Hebrew words in question.]

It might seem probable to some that gazelles, too, might be meant instead of the chamois, since they also stay on high mountains. But since they are also found, and indeed more frequently, on plains and in valleys, naming them would not be characteristic of a refuge; whereas ibexes always live among these rocky crests. Rabbits are not present at all in these mountain regions, and it is wholly contrary to their nature to dwell in rock clefts. Those, however, are precisely the constant dwelling and hiding place of the wubber — the hyrax — whose manner of life David must have known exactly, because he stayed so long in this wild solitude. I am no connoisseur of the original text, however, and our Orientalists will have to decide whether the two Hebrew words may be translated in this way.

Although I had dismounted, my mule still found it very hard to climb down the rock path, and we were several times afraid that it would break its neck. One can easily imagine how troublesome must be the transport of the salt that the Reshaide and other Bedouin and peasants fetch from the shore, and of the other products for which they use asses. At eight o'clock we came to the smaller spring of Ein Gedi, which lies at the foot of the rock mountains. Although the water was clear, I did not find it refreshingly cool; to my feeling it was a little lukewarm. But the morning cold and the cold wind on the mountains may have contributed to that, by accustoming my hand to a greater cold. Not far from its source this spring joins a larger one that rises a little to the north in a rocky valley and, after a short course, pours itself into the sea.

The bank of the smaller spring was bordered with reeds and some trees and bushes, which showed a luxuriant growth. One sees from this that the shore of the sea would be in no way lacking in fertility if only spring water and sufficient level ground were present everywhere; and I am convinced that in the almost tropical heat said to rule here in summer one would then find the most flourishing vegetation everywhere. The plain of Ein Gedi, of which only a very small part is now cultivated, could easily be cultivated along its whole length if the Bedouin were only a little inclined to help nature. Since both springs rise fairly high, they could with little trouble be led so that they watered the whole plain. But it is already a rarity when these people cultivate small patches on the most fruitful soil; how then could one expect them to think of artificial arrangements for making land fruitful? A little below the spring one still sees a ruined, walled water basin, as proof that in former times the plain of Ein Gedi was better used.

The sziddr (Rhamnus zizyphus L.?) grew here frequently, in part as a tree. I further found some wild fig and pomegranate trees, phistuk el-ban, szeal (a mimosa tree), oscher (Asclepias gigantea L.), and a kind of great bushy prickly nightshade (Solanum sanctum L.), which the Bedouin called szacharan or szeckaran.

Ein Gedi is known as the only place where the much-famed apples of Sodom are found. Bodrus and several other Bethlehemites had assured me that here one found not only apples but also pears, pomegranates, figs, quinces, lemons, and so on, all of which were said to have a beautiful appearance outwardly but to contain nothing inside except a light dust or ash, and no flesh at all. A merchant of Jaffa, who is Imperial-Royal vice-consul there, had had a basketful fetched for him by a Bethlehemite some years ago and brought to Jaffa; and everyone who had seen them in Bethlehem assured me that they had the properties just stated. One can easily imagine how such definite news stretched my curiosity to the utmost, and that my first inquiry in Ein Gedi concerned these wonder-fruits.

Hamdan, the Reshaide, to whose tribe Ein Gedi belongs, and who visited it several times every year, was the only one who could give me news about this, since my other people had never been here. Because the Reshaide sometimes bring these fruits to Bethlehem and make a small, though insignificant, profit by selling them, Hamdan also had a private interest in making the existence of the apples of Sodom better known. Immediately after our arrival at the spring, therefore, he showed me, among the reeds and bushes, a shrub with large leaves on which we still found a few fruits that he passed off as the apples — of which, however, only a shrivelled skin remained. Right beside it he showed me a small prickly shrub that bore uncommonly beautiful citron-yellow fruits, much like lemons, which he also passed off as real lemons; but on opening them one found inside nothing but a quantity of seeds in a kind of decayed slime or pulp. Other apples of Sodom Hamdan did not know. He assured me, it is true, that the pomegranates and figs here also had the same property; but that was certainly an untruth, which I could not prove to him with regard to the pomegranates because the pomegranate trees were now without fruit. With regard to the figs, however, I could: the single almost fully grown fig that I found had exactly the same inner character as all wild-growing figs in the whole world.

My readers will now very much wish me to make them better acquainted with these two kinds of apples of Sodom. So be it!

The shrub first named I recognised at first sight as the oscher, or giant milkweed (Asclepias gigantea L.), which I had already found in spring at Ghor es-Safi, at the south end of the Dead Sea. Of this remarkable plant, which is also met with in the East Indies, Arabia, Egypt, and even Jamaica, one finds a full description together with an illustration in Linnaeus's complete system of plants. Here this milkweed had only the size of a considerable shrub, whereas at Ghor es-Safi I had found it the size of a middling fig tree. Its branches are light green and full of sap, like a plant stem. They are extraordinarily thickly set with large, oblong-oval or inversely egg-shaped leathery leaves, which clasp the stem with their base. Two and two always stand opposite each other; each pair is only a finger's or an inch's breadth from the other, so that one sees almost nothing of the stem. They are four to five and a half inches long and, at the top where they are broadest, two and a half to three and a half inches broad. The small lobe on each side of the base that clasps the stem is rounded. At the top they have a small point. Their edge is untoothed. They have a dull sheen, are smooth and light green; the leaf-ribs are whitish, and from them veins run out in an oblique direction, fairly straight and in part opposite one another. Leaves, branches, stem, and fruits are full of a white milky sap, which wells out abundantly when they are broken off or cut into, but shows almost no sharpness on the tongue. The bark on stronger stems is extremely fissured and only a little firmer than elder pith. I found no flowers.

The most remarkable thing about this plant is the fruits, which sometimes reach almost the size of a small child's head. Here I found only three sitting together in a group, their stem less than an inch long; but they consisted merely of a shrivelled skin. At their base sat the likewise shrivelled flower calyx, which could easily be detached and was split five ways. Their tips were violet-coloured inside. One clearly saw that the skin had burst open on one side and the contained silk had flown away. Later I had the opportunity to see many fresh fruits. Outwardly they had a green colour. If one opens them, one finds inside a loose web of innumerable white threads crossing one another, which connect the outer skin with an oblong spindle-shaped body in which the seeds lie, scale-like, one upon another, fastened by shining silk threads to an inner axis. The skin enclosing the seeds has a yellowish colour inside. The silk is uncommonly light and flies away quickly when the fruits burst open. The Bedouin use it for the touch-holes [priming] of their flintlocks, since it catches fire very easily; sometimes their women also prepare from it, mixed with cotton, a kind of headband. The milk of the plant is said to be used in the Greek monastery at Jerusalem — whether to make women fruitful or unfruitful, I could not rightly make out. It is also said to be good against the white spots on the skin that constitute a kind of leprosy.

From this description my readers will clearly see that there is no talk here of cursed apples or pears at all, but of a wholly natural fruit of a particular plant, which in its kind is as perfect as any other fruit in the world.

Now to the other fruit, which was passed off as lemons. I found this plant, which from its flower I at once took for a kind of nightshade, not only here but afterward in great quantity at Jericho. It is a small shrub with prickly crooked branches of a yellowish-grey colour. The leaves are egg-shaped and sometimes scalloped and toothed at the sides. The leaf-stalk, only half an inch long, does not sit exactly in the middle of the base, so that the base runs down somewhat deeper on one side than on the other. The colour of the leaves is greyish green, underneath always lighter than above, and on both sides — but especially underneath — covered with a felt-like substance. The shrub bore flowers and fruits at the same time and in great quantity. The flowers are violet-blue and measure almost an inch across at the mouth. The perennial calyx is split five ways and, like the inch-long fruit-stalk, usually has crooked thorns. The fruits have the size of a middling plum, a beautiful citron-yellow colour, and are almost completely round. Their outer skin is smooth and thin. Inside it is a fleshy rind half a line thick, and in the inner cavity one meets a great quantity of small, almost kidney-shaped seeds of a brownish-yellow colour, in a slimy juice, around and within two fleshy bodies. Plant connoisseurs will very soon recognise from this description that this wonder-fruit is nothing other than the fruit of the holy nightshade (Solanum sanctum L.), which is probably found in the whole Jordan plain and perhaps in several regions of Palestine, and of which single specimens might also be met with in European greenhouses. The Bedouin call this plant szakaran; and since that word in Arabic means “drunken,” I suspect that they know from experience the poisonous effect of its fruits, which most kinds of nightshade display.

It is astonishing how the tale of the apples of Sodom could keep itself alive for thousands of years, when only a somewhat unprejudiced observation would have found it wholly unfounded. But how could one expect unprejudiced observers here, when they usually came as pious pilgrims, led and instructed by monks who were partly too ignorant, partly too self-interested, to fight prejudices that gave their land a more wonderful appearance and awakened a greater interest in it abroad? The whole tale seems to rest on a single passage in the older Hebrew writings, from which one sees how such a wonder-seed can grow on for millennia when it was contained in a writing that we were taught to regard as most venerable. This passage is found in the Wisdom of Solomon (10:7). The author speaks of the terrible catastrophe said to have struck the five cities in the Valley of Siddim, and says: “Their desolate land still smokes, a witness to wickedness; trees there never bear ripe fruit; and the salt pillar still reminds us of the doubter.”

Flavius Josephus seems first to have warmed up this fable. “One sees there, in the land of Sodom,” he says, “some remains of the five cities, and their cursed ash brings forth fruits which seem edible, but which fall into dust as soon as one barely touches them.” [Seetzen cites a French Josephus, Paris, 1689.] But however much this historian deserves our greatest praise as a presenter of historical events and as a painter of places, he shows himself every time a true simpleton when the talk is of things concerning physics and natural history.

The great rarity of the giant milkweed — which, so far as I know, is found nowhere in Palestine except around the edge of the Dead Sea — and the striking resemblance of the fruits of the holy nightshade to young lemons could perhaps, in highest antiquity, when natural knowledge was still in its childhood, have moved a moralist like Solomon, or one still earlier, to derive from them apparent grounds with which to prove the greatness of the crime committed by the inhabitants of this region. Of the remaining points of the passage quoted I shall speak in what follows.

Here the first installment stops: at Ein Gedi, printed p. 236 of vol. 2, with the so-called apples of Sodom examined and set down as plants, not portents. The diary continues northward along the western shore in the next slice.
