**by Regino of Prüm (d. 915), to Archbishop Rathbod of Trier**
A fresh English rendering, plain and complete

harmonica institutione* — the earliest surviving "please fix our
church music" letter: an abbot's report to his archbishop on the
confused psalm-singing of the diocese, and the course of musical

semitone, the true *musicus*, and the meaningless syllables,
translated sentence by sentence from the Latin of Martin Gerbert's
edition, vol. 1 (St. Blasien, 1784), pp. 230-247.

## Dedication

**Regino of Prüm (d. 915)** — who he was: a monk and abbot of the
Carolingian world, remembered as the chronicler Reginon. The
verifiable record (1911 *Encyclopaedia Britannica*, "Reginon") gives
his works: a *Chronicon* of the world's history to the year 906; a
collection of canons of church discipline drawn up at the request of
his friend and patron Radbod (Rathbod), archbishop of Trier; and to
that same archbishop this letter on music, the *Epistola de
harmonica institutione*, with its *Tonarius* — written, the
record says, "to improve the singing in the churches of the
diocese."

The circumstances of his death: he died in 915 and was buried in
the abbey of St. Maximin at Trier, his tomb being discovered there
in 1581. No more is asserted here than the record gives.

Why his work is vital — proved, not praised: the letter itself is
the proof. It is the earliest surviving first-hand account of a
church-music reform as it happened: an abbot who *heard* the
psalm-choirs of his diocese singing "with confused voices, through
dissonance of tone," saw his archbishop troubled by it, and sat down
with the antiphoner "from beginning to end" to put every antiphon,
introit, communion, and responsory into its proper tone. Around that
practical labor he built a complete short course of music — the
eight tones, the "bastard" (*nothae*) chants that wander between
tones, the music of the spheres after Pythagoras, Boethius, Cicero,
and Macrobius, the ethical power of music over morals (David and
Saul, Elisha, the drunken youths and the Phrygian mode), the
Pythagorean hammers, the fifteen strings with their Greek names
parsed in Latin, the hard problem of the semitone, and the famous
line that the true *musicus* is not the man who works with his hands
but the man who can give the reason. Eleven hundred years later,
choirmasters still fight the battle he describes in his first page.

Its relation to this rescue: the Boneyard rescues the working
knowledge of dead masters, and Regino's letter is working knowledge
with the dust of the choir-stalls still on it — not a philosopher's
system but a foreman's report: what was wrong, what he did about
it, and what every singer must know so it does not go wrong again.
It belongs in the series beside the mensural-notation row as the
row's elder: the letter that predates Franco of Cologne by three
and a half centuries and already asks the question the whole row
answers — how shall we sing together, and in tune?

Where a reader goes next: Martin Gerbert, *Scriptores ecclesiastici
de musica sacra potissimum*, vol. 1 (St. Blasien, 1784), pp. 230-247
— the printed home of the Latin letter (the *Tonarius* follows in
Coussemaker, vol. 2, pp. 1-73); the Thesaurus Musicarum Latinarum
corpus, entry REGHAR, for the searchable transcription; and the
1911 *Encyclopaedia Britannica* article "Reginon" for the life in
brief.

## How to read this book

Regino writes to a fellow churchman who sings. His world has no
staff notation as we know it; the "tones" (*toni*) are the eight
church modes, and the *differentiae* ("divisions") are the melodic
turns sung on the last syllable of a psalm verse. Technical words
are kept in Latin or Greek in parentheses at first use:

- *tonus* — both the church mode (one of the eight) and the musical
  interval of the whole tone; context tells which.
- *nothae* — "bastard" antiphons: chants that begin in one tone,
  wander through another, and end in a third.
- *musica naturalis / artificialis* — "natural music" (the music
  that sounds with no instrument: the heavens' motion, the human
  voice) and "artificial music" (music made by human art, on
  instruments).
- *diatessaron, diapente, diapason* — the fourth, the fifth, the
  octave.
- *epitritus, hemiolius, duplaris, triplaris, quadruplus, epogdous*
  — the number-ratios 4:3, 3:2, 2:1, 3:1, 4:1, 9:8.
- *musicus* — not "musician" in our sense but the knower: the man
  who can give the reason for what is sung.

Bracketed notes in the form [Editor's note] are the editor's, not
Regino's. The letter is divided by Regino's own numbered heads
(1-19, following Gerbert's synopsis); paragraphing within them is
the editor's.

## The Letter on the Institution of Harmony, sent to Rathbod, Archbishop of Trier, by Regino the priest

*To the most excellent lord Rathbod, Archbishop of the holy church
of Trier, Regino [sends] devoted service forever.*

### 1. Why the Tonarius joined to this letter was made

When, in the dioceses of your church, I had often heard the choir
of psalm-singers sounding the melody of the psalms with confused
voices, through dissonance of tone — and had often seen your
reverence troubled by such a matter — I took up the Antiphonary,
and turning it diligently through, from beginning to end in order,
I distributed the antiphons I found noted in it to their proper
tones, as I believe; and the divisions of the tones, that is, the
*differentiae*, which are customarily made on the last syllable in
the verse, so that the singing-together may come out seemly and
fitting — just as they were handed down to us by our elders, and
just as the experience of the harmonic discipline itself has shown
— I took care to insert in distinct orders. Some add other
divisions too, which we judge superfluous; but lest we be blamed by
superstitious musicians, we took care to note them in the margin,
below or above, leaving it to the judgment of the skilled singer
whether he wishes to deem them necessary or superfluous. And not
only did I distinguish the antiphons by their fitting tones, but
the introits for the Masses and the communions as well; and also
the responsories, which are sung in the night hours to the divine
praise — I labored with the utmost zeal to join them to the
consonant agreements of their tones.

[Editor's note — in plain words: The choirs were singing out of tune
with each other and the archbishop was upset. Regino went through
the whole chant book and put every chant in its right mode, writing
the psalm-verse endings in too — and noted the disputed extras in
the margin for the experts to argue over.]

### 2. The irregularities of the modes, or eight tones

The skilled singer must know that not every tonal consonance is
easily recognized in some antiphons. For there are certain
antiphons which we call *nothae* — that is, degenerate and not
legitimate — which begin in one tone, are in another in the middle,
and are finished in a third. Their dissonance and ambiguity we laid
open in their places in the breviary of the work that follows. But
so that the reason may shine clearer, we insert some here for
example: *Ante me non est formatus Deus*; *Ex quo facta est vox*;
*Ex Aegypto vocavi*; *Ad te Domine levavi animam meam*; *Sion
renovaberis*; *O mors ero mors tua*; *Vade iam et noli peccare*.
These aforesaid antiphons begin in the seventh tone, and some are
finished in the first, others in the fourth tone. Likewise the
antiphons *Ne reminiscaris* and *Tulit ergo paralyticus* begin in
the third tone but are finished in the fourth. So *Quare
detraxistis* and *Multa quidem et alia signa* begin in the third
tone but are finished in the sixth. *Qui odit animam suam* and *Et
respiciens* begin in the third but are finished in the eighth.
*Quod uni ex minimis meis* begins in the seventh but is finished in
the first — and many like these. Nor are antiphons alone mixed with
these ambiguities and doubtfulnesses of tones: some introits too
begin in one tone but are finished in another. *Deus in adiutorium
meum intende* begins in the eighth tone but is finished in the
seventh. Likewise *Accipite iocunditatem* and *Deus dum egrederetur*
begin in the eighth but are finished in the fourth. *Repleatur os
meum* and *Charitas Dei* begin in the third and are finished in the
fourth. *Iudica Domine nocentes me* begins in the fourth but is
finished in the seventh. *Victricem manum tuam* begins in the third
but is finished in the eighth. *Eduxit Dominus populum suum* begins
in the fourth but is finished in the eighth. *Ecce oculi Domini*
begins in the third but is finished in the fourth. *Loquetur
Dominus* begins in the third but is finished in the fourth. *Iustus
non conturbabitur* begins in the second but is finished in the
first. *Dicit Dominus, sermones* begins in the third, is finished
in the first — and the like. But this the prudent singer must
observe above all: that he always attend more to the *beginning*
of the antiphon, introit, or communion in the sonority of the tone
than to the end; and conversely in the responsories let him
consider more the end and close in the consonance of the tone than
the beginning.

[Editor's note — in plain words: Some chants are "bastards" — they
start in one mode and end in another. Regino lists them by name with
their wandering tones, and gives the working rule: judge an
antiphon by its opening, a responsory by its close.]

### 3. The eight tones, or musical modes

In natural music — that is, in the song (*cantilena*) that is
modulated in the divine praises — there are found four principal
tones, which are named by this Greek vocabulary: *authenticus
protus, authenticus deuterus, authenticus tritus, authenticus
tetrardus*. From their fountains flow four others, which are named:
*plaga proti, plaga deuteri, plaga triti, plaga tetrardi*. For from
*authenticus protus* is born or derived *plaga proti*; so from the
other three the remaining three take their beginning — they are, so
to speak, their members. They can be interpreted thus: *authenticus
protus*, that is, the first authority — understand, in natural
music; *authenticus deuterus*, that is, the second authority;
*authenticus tritus*, that is, the third authority; *authenticus
tetrardus*, that is, the fourth authority. *Plaga proti*, that is,
part of the first tone; *plaga deuteri*, that is, authority of the
second tone [so the text; the pattern suggests "part of the second
tone"]; *plaga triti*, that is, part of the third tone; *plaga
tetrardi*, that is, part of the fourth tone. In these eight tones
not only all harmony of spiritual melody but also all natural song
is contained and comprehended.

[Editor's note — in plain words: Four "authentic" modes and their
four "plagal" partners — the eight church tones that hold every
sacred melody and, Regino says, every natural tune besides.]

### 4. What a tone is, and natural and artificial music

But many hear "tone" and perhaps do not know what a tone is, or why
it is called tone, or how far the tone of natural music differs
from the tone of artificial music. The tones of natural music are
four principal. The tones of artificial music are five, and two
semitones — which semitones, however, do not fill out a whole tone,
for they cannot be divided into equal parts: one is greater, the
other less, and therefore they admit no equal sections. These five
tones with the two semitones stand firm in the three consonances of
musical perfection: namely in diapente, diatessaron, and diapason.
Item: from the four principal tones of natural music four others
arise, and as streams flow from fountains and branches proceed from
a tree's root, so in a manner they draw their origin from them.
None of this happens in artificial music: no tone begets another
tone from itself, nor does one of them excel another in authority —
but all are divided among themselves by equal divisions, namely in
sesquioctave proportion, except the semitones. This too is to be
noted: that in natural music all eight tones are whole and perfect
— though they differ in authority among themselves — and admit no
semitone, no *diesis*, no *apotome*, no *tristemoria*, no
*tetrastemoria*; for into these parts the tone of artificial music
is divided.

From this dissonance it appears that the oft-named eight, called
tones, should be called not so much tones as *modes*, or
*differentiae*, or *tropes* of the consonances of musical
modulation — which, with beautiful variety of harmonic delight,
mixed of grave and acute sounds, as if sprinkled with flowers,
render the sweetness of melody mild and fitting. To this is added
that all skill of the musical discipline is measured and confined
by arithmetical rules: it is made in duple, quadruple, sesquialter,
sesquitertian, or sesquioctave proportions of numbers. These
dimensions, I think, can hardly be found in the aforesaid tones.
For all whole and perfect tones must differ among themselves by
equal dimensions — that is, by an eighth part of themselves. This
distance can be shown between the numbers eight and nine: nine
contains the whole eight within itself, and besides, its eighth
part — that is, one. Musicians call this difference the interval;
arithmeticians the sesquioctave proportion. The interval is the
distance of acute and grave sound.

A tone, then, either transcends another tone or is transcended by
another tone by an eighth part of itself. But what the ratio of the
tones or of the aforesaid consonances is, or how the musical
consonances are found, or how they are fitted to each other or
sundered from each other — we shall show more plainly below.
Meanwhile, let credence with middling understanding be given to the
aforesaid discussion; then faith will be undoubting, when what
seemed ambiguous is revealed by plain demonstration. And since
mention has been made of natural and artificial music, the less
skilled musician may ask what distance there is between natural and
artificial music. To which it must be answered: though every
modulation of harmonic institution is one and the same in the
sounds of the consonances, yet natural music is one thing,
artificial another. Natural music is what resounds with no musical
instrument, no touch of fingers, no human impulse or touch, but,
divinely breathed, with nature alone teaching, modulates sweet
modes — which happens either in the motion of heaven or in the
human voice. Some add a third: in the irrational creature, by sound
or voice.

[Editor's note — in plain words: The church's eight "tones" aren't
really tones at all — they're modes. A real tone (the interval) is
the 9:8 step, the difference between 8 and 9, and it belongs to
"artificial" music — the music of instruments and numbers — while
the modes belong to "natural" music: the heavens turning and the
human voice singing, with no instrument at all.]

### 5. Music in the motion of the heavenly bodies

That music is in the motion of heaven is detected by this argument
of the Pythagoreans. "How," they say, "can it be that so swift a
machine of heaven moves in a hushed and silent course? Though that
sound does not reach our ears, yet it can in no way be that so
exceedingly swift a motion lacks sound — especially since the
courses of the stars are joined with such fitting and agreement
that nothing can be understood so joined and connected. For some
are borne higher, some lower, and all roll with equal swiftness, so
that through disparate inequalities the ordered course of their
courses is led." Hence it is conjectured that in heaven's motion
there is an ordered order of modulation. For consonance, which
rules all musical modulation, cannot happen without sound; sound is
not rendered without some impulse or stroke; stroke does not happen
unless motion has preceded.

Motions are some swifter, some slower; and if the motion is slow
and sparse, it makes grave sounds — but if swift and dense, it must
render acute sounds. In an immobile thing no sound is ever made.
Hence musicians define sound thus: *sound is a percussion of air,
undissolved, all the way to the hearing*. From many motions, then,
both acuteness and gravity consist.

From this conjecture the astrologers — or musicians — say that
between the outermost sphere and the circles of the seven planets
all musical consonances are fulfilled. From Saturn to the celestial
sphere, they say, the acutest sound is made; from earth to the
lunar circle the gravest — though some think otherwise. In short,
from Saturn to the [outer] sphere they assert a graver sound is
made, from earth to the lunar circle an acuter — because what is
tighter and shorter must resonate acuter, as conversely what is
longer, graver. Between Saturn's circle and the Moon's, through the
whole amplitude of the planets, the variety of diverse tones and
all musical consonances are perfected. All this Martianus
figuratively feigned to have been in the grove of Apollo, in the
book he wrote *On the Marriage of Philology and Mercury* — Apollo
himself being the Sun, the moderator of celestial music. For, he
says, the loftier summits — that is, the higher branches — spread
out, that is, greatly extended, rang with acute sound, that is,
subtle and slender. Whatever was near and neighboring to earth —
the more inclined and lower branches, nearer to earth — the hoarse
gravity shook, that is, struck and beat back. But the middle parts
of the wood sang their joined spaces with double *succentus*.
*Concentus* is the united society of like voices; *succentus* is
varied sounds most agreeing with one another, as we see in the
organ. "With double," he says, "*succentus*, and sesquialter, and
sesquitertian." Here he touches three consonances: diapason,
diatessaron, and diapente. "Octaves without distinction" — that is,
without interval — "joined," that is, in consonances: here he
touches the tone. "Though *limmata* intervened," that is,
semitones: here he touches the two semitones. From all these the
sum of all music consists. But all this will be shown more plainly
in its place.

Nor must it be omitted that the strings — that is, the chords — are
compared to celestial music: *hypate meson* is attributed by
musicians to Saturn; *parypate* is like the Jovian circle; *lichanos
meson* they handed to Mars; the Sun obtained *mese*; *trite
synemmenon* Venus has; *paranete synemmenon* Mercury rules; *nete*
holds the pattern of the lunar circle. This according to Boethius.
Cicero makes the contrary order: in the *Dream of Scipio* he
asserts — and nature bears it — that the extremes sound gravely on
one side, acutely on the other. Hence the highest star-bearing
chariot of heaven, whose turning is swiftest, is moved with acute
and excited sound; the lowest, the lunar, with the gravest. For the
earth, ninth, remaining immobile, ever sticks in one seat. In this
place Cicero puts the earth as silence — that is, immobile. After
it, nearest to silence, he gives the Moon the gravest sound, so
that the Moon is *proslambanomenos*, the string that resounds most
gravely; Mercury *hypate hypaton*, Venus *parypate hypaton*, the
Sun *lichanos hypaton*, Mars *hypate meson*, Jupiter *parypate
meson*, Saturn *lichanos meson*, the celestial sphere *mese*. The
Moon compared with Mercury resounds a tone; the same Moon with Mars
creates the diapente consonance, with the Sun diatessaron, with the
celestial sphere diapason. Behold, you have in heaven's motion the
sum of all music. Let these few things suffice on celestial music.
If anyone desires to know more fully, let him read the second book
of Macrobius, the excellent philosopher, on the aforesaid *Dream of
Scipio*. This one thing we add: not only the gentile philosophers
but also the strenuous preachers of the Christian faith agree in
this celestial harmony.

[Editor's note — in plain words: The planets must make music,
because nothing that moves that fast and that regularly can move
silently. Regino walks the reader through Martianus's grove of
Apollo, Boethius's planet-strings, and Cicero's upside-down version
— and notes that Christian preachers agree with the pagan
philosophers on this one.]

### 6. Natural music, and its effects

These things passed over, we must cross to contemplating human
music. That music is naturally joined to all men, and all ages, and
every sex, no one who understands himself doubts. What age, or what
sex, is not delighted by musical songs? It is proper to humanity to
soothe the mind with sweet modes, to exasperate it with contrary
ones. Infants and youths, and even old men, are so naturally
implicated, by a certain spontaneous affection, in musical
modulations that there is no age at all without delight in sweet
song. Though there are some who cannot sing learnedly and sweetly
for others, yet for themselves they sing something unsweetly sweet.

Know moreover that the morals of men are known through music: the
wanton and petulant mind delights in more wanton modes — or,
hearing them often, is softened and effeminated. Conversely the
harder and fiercer mind, or the rougher, rejoices in rougher modes
or is stirred by rougher. For it cannot be that soft things are
joined to hard, or hard to soft, and delight in them; but likeness
of morals, as said, conciliates love and delight. For the rougher
peoples delight in harder modes; the gentle and peaceful in gentler.

In short, every habit of morals is so governed and ruled by songs
that both the advance to war and the retreat are sung — with the
trumpet's song exciting, and again calming, the mind's virtue. Song
gives sleep and takes it away; it brings cares and anxieties and
draws them back. It suggests anger, persuades clemency, and heals
diseases of bodies. It is common talk how often song has repressed
angry minds, how many wonders it has worked in afflictions of
bodies or souls. In short, as Cicero attests: when drunken youths,
incited by the flutes' song, were breaking the doors of a chaste
woman to violate her chastity, Pythagoras perceived that the youths'
minds were incited to lust by the sound of the Phrygian mode — for
at that hour he was watching the courses of the stars — and at once
running up advised the flute-girl to change the mode and play the
spondee. When she did, by the slowness of the modes and the gravity
of the playing, their raging petulance settled.

But what we bring from the gentiles' books we can affirm from our
own. When the evil spirit harassed King Saul, David, taking up the
psaltery, mitigated the fierceness of his breast with sweet and
suave modes. What shall we say of the prophet Elisha? When, asked
by the king, he perceived that the spirit of prophecy was not in him
at that hour, he ordered a psalter to be brought to him; and when
he psaltered before him, suddenly the spirit descended upon him,
and he prophesied.

Thus, as it stands certain that the minds of fighters in war are
kindled by the song of trumpets, so there is no doubt that a more
modest and sweeter mode of songs can temper the mind's disturbance.
From all these it appears perspicuously and indubitably that music
is so naturally joined to us that we cannot, even if we would, not
sing. Wherefore the mind's intention must be raised, so that what
is naturally implanted in us may also be comprehended by science.

Now remains to see the third kind of natural music, which we
mentioned above is in the irrational creature. Of this, then, such
is the philosophers' opinion. "No wonder," they say, "if music has
such dominion among men, when birds too — nightingales, swans, and
others — practice song as if by a discipline of the musical art;
whence Virgil says of swans: *dant per colla modos* — 'they give
measures through their necks' — and the rest. Some earthly or water
birds, invited by song, run willingly into nets and snares. Nor is
it less to be observed that the shepherd's pipe commands the
flocks' feeding, advancing, and resting. And in the sea the Sirens
are said to utter sweet songs." Rightly then is all that lives
captured by music, since the celestial soul, by which the universe
is animated, took its origin from music, as Plato and his followers
hold. These things on natural music we have briefly touched.

[Editor's note — in plain words: Music moves morals: wanton tunes
make wanton minds, rough tunes rough minds — so choose. Regino
proves it three ways: Pythagoras calming the drunken mob by
switching modes, David soothing Saul, Elisha calling down prophecy
with the psaltery. And the birds sing too — even the Sirens.]

### 7. Artificial music, briefly

Remains to see briefly the artificial. Artificial music is what is
devised and invented by human art and ingenuity, and it consists in
certain instruments. It is likewise divided into three kinds:
*tensibile, inflatile, percussibile*. *Tensibile* is made by the
stretching of strings, as in lyre, cithara, harp, and the like.
*Inflatile* is driven by breath or wind, as in tibiae, musae,
fistulae, organs, and the like. *Percussibile* is made in certain
hollow brazen vessels and other instruments, when they are struck
with a beat and a percussion, whence diverse sounds are made, as in
cymbals, drums, and the rest. Whoever desires to have knowledge of
this art must know that, though natural music far precedes
artificial, yet none can recognize the force of natural music except
through artificial. Therefore, though our discourse proceeded from
natural, it must end in artificial — so that through the visible
thing we may demonstrate the invisible.

[Editor's note — in plain words: Instruments come in three kinds:
stretched (strings), blown (winds), struck (bells and drums). You
can only understand heaven's music and the voice's music by way of
the music you can see and touch.]

### 8. Whence music is named

With these briefly foretasted, reason now demands that we
contemplate music itself more diligently. First to be said: whence
is it called music? Music is named from the *musa* — the pipe —
which the ancients deemed the instrument worthy to be set before
all musical instruments: either because, as they say, it was first
found by nature, or rather because in it all musical perfection is
contained. For in its upper holes all consonances and tones can be
shown; in the two lower, the greater and lesser semitone. [The
Latin's opening is compressed here — "a musis ... quod instrumentum"
— and the translation follows the sense: the pipe, *musa*, as the
name-source.] It is named from *musa* as *grammatica* from
*gramma*, and *rhetorica* from *rhetoreon* — that is, copiousness
of elocution — and *arithmetica* from *arithmos*, that is, number,
and *geometria* from *ge*, that is, earth, and *astrologia* from
*astron*, that is, star.

[Editor's note — in plain words: "Music" is named for the pipe the
way "grammar" is named for the letter — and the pipe earns it,
because every interval can be fingered on its holes.]

### 9. What voice is, what sound is

But before we discern the parts of musical institution, first let
us say something of voice and sound. Though every voice is sound,
yet not every sound can rightly be called voice. The creature that
is breathed with vital spirit renders voice; what is driven by
beat, stroke, or breath gives sound. Every voice is either
*syneches* — that is, continuous — by which, speaking or reading a
lesson, we run through words continuously; or *diastematice* — that
is, suspended with interval — which we suspend in singing, in which
we serve not speeches but measures. Of this our discourse now
turns.

[Editor's note — in plain words: Voice needs lungs and life; sound
needs only a knock. Singing-voice is the "gappy" kind — it moves
by steps, not by talk.]

### 10. The consonances and the intervals

Know that from voice or tone consonance is procreated. And note
this: in those voices or sounds which discord with no inequality,
there is no consonance at all. Consonance is defined thus:
*consonance is the concord of dissimilar voices reduced into one*.
Otherwise: *consonance is the mixture of acute and grave sound,
falling sweetly and uniformly on the ears*. And conversely
*dissonance is the harsh and unpleasant percussion of two sounds
mixed together, coming to the ear*. Though the ear's sense judges
consonance, reason weighs it. For whenever two strings are
stretched, and one sounds graver, the other acuter, and struck
together they render a somehow mixed and sweet sound, and the two
voices as if joined into one coalesce — then happens what is called
consonance. But when, struck together, each strives against the
other, nor do they mix into a sweet sound, one composed of two, for
the ear — then it is what is called dissonance.

The numbers that create the consonances, or by which the
consonances are discerned, are only six: *epitritus, hemiolius,
duplaris, triplaris, quadruplus, epogdous*. *Epitritus* is when of
two numbers the greater has the whole lesser, and besides, its
third part — as 4 to 3; for in 4 are 3 and a third part of 3, that
is, 1. This number is called in arithmetic *sesquitertius*, and from
it is born the *symphonia*, that is, consonance, called in music
*diatessaron*.

*Hemiolius* is when of two numbers the greater has the whole
lesser, and besides, its half — as 3 to 2; for in 3 are 2 and their
middle part, that is, 1. This number is called *sesquialter* in
arithmetic, and from it comes the *symphonia* named *diapente*.
The *duplaris* number is when of two numbers the lesser is counted
twice in the greater — as 4 to 2; and from this *duplaris* is born
the *symphonia* named *diapason*.

*Triplaris* is when of two numbers the lesser is counted thrice in
the greater — as 3 to 1; and from this *triplaris* arises the
*symphonia* named *diapason with diapente*. The *quadruplaris*
number is when of two numbers the lesser is counted four times in
the greater — as 4 to 1; this number makes the *symphonia* called
*bis diapason*.

*Epogdous* is the number that has the lesser within itself, and
besides, its eighth part — as 9 to 8; for in 9 are 8, and besides,
their eighth part, that is, 1. Arithmeticians call this number
*sesquioctave*. This number begets the sound musicians called
*tone*. A sound less than a tone the ancients wished to call
*semitone* — but not so to be taken as if a half-tone were meant,
for neither do we take *semivowel* in letters for half a vowel.
Then a tone by its nature cannot be divided into two equal parts:
since it consists of the ninefold number, and 9 is never divided
equally, the tone refuses to be divided into two halves. But they
called *semitone* the sound less than a tone, which was found to be
distant by as small an interval as these two numbers are distant
from each other — that is, 243 and 256. This semitone some ancient
Pythagoreans named *diesis*; but later usage established that a
still smaller sound be named *diesis*. Plato called the semitone
*limma*.

These are the parts into which all music is resolved. There are,
then, five *symphoniae*, that is, consonances: diatessaron,
diapente, diapason, diapason with diapente, and bis diapason. All
musical consonances consist either in duple, triple, quadruple,
sesquialter, or sesquitertian proportion of numbers. What in numbers
is sesquitertian is in sounds diatessaron; what in numbers
sesquialter is in voices diapente; what in numbers duple is in
consonances diapason; triple is diapente and diapason; quadruple is
bis diapason.

Let the diligent reader know that consonances set upon consonances
make certain other consonances: diapente and diatessaron joined
create the diapason consonance; to this — that is, diapason — if
diapente is joined, comes the consonance named from both words:
diapason and diapente; to which if diatessaron is added, comes bis
diapason.

Know moreover that if diapason and diatessaron are joined, they
make no consonance: at once it falls into the superpartient genus
of inequality, keeping neither the order of multiples nor the
simplicity of the superparticular. How this may be proved is long
to insert here; whoever desires to know more fully, let him read
Boethius's second book *On the Institution of Harmony*. Let this
one thing briefly commemorated suffice: from unequal numbers the
concord of the consonances is made. The genera of unequal numbers
dissent among themselves in five modes in their inequality: either
one transcends the other by multiplicity, or by single parts, or by
several, or by multiplicity and a part, or by multiplicity and
parts. Whoever desires to consider these species of inequalities,
let him first read Boethius's first book of *Arithmetic*.

Note that of the five aforesaid genera of inequality only two are
taken for forming harmonic consonances: the *multiple* and the
*superparticular*. The *multiple* is the number that contains the
lesser twice or thrice or four times in itself; from this the
diapason consonances are formed. The *superparticular* is the number
that has the whole lesser in itself, and besides, its half, third,
or eighth part; from this are procreated the diapente and
diatessaron consonances, and also the tone. Hence it is fitting to
know that none can be made a perfect musician unless first fully
instituted in arithmetical rules.

With these premised for clarity, let us discuss the aforesaid
consonances through the tones. The diatessaron consonance consists
of two tones and a semitone, and comes from the *epitrite*, that
is, sesquitertian. The diapente consonance consists of three tones
and a semitone, and comes from the *hemiole* — that is, from
*semis* and tone, for the Greeks call *semis* "hemi," the whole
"holon." The diapason consonance consists of six tones and comes
from the *duplaris* number; for from diapente and diatessaron comes
the *symphonia* diapason. For it was shown above that diatessaron
consists of two tones and a semitone, diapente of three tones and a
semitone — that is, of five tones and two semitones. But diapason
and diapente consists of nine tones and a semitone, and comes from
the *triplaris* number. Bis diapason contains twelve tones and
comes from the quadruple. These we said of the tones according to
Macrobius's assertion; but Cassiodorus in secular letters affirms
there are fifteen tones, according to the number of strings placed
in the bis diapason consonance — which we shall show below, when we
begin to treat of the strings and their names.

[Editor's note — in plain words: Six ratios father every consonance:
4:3 the fourth, 3:2 the fifth, 2:1 the octave, 3:1 octave-plus-fifth,
4:1 double octave, 9:8 the whole tone. Stack them and they breed:
fifth plus fourth is an octave. And no one is a real musician who
hasn't done his arithmetic first.]

### 11. After Pythagoras's discovery

Know that the oft-named consonances were not invented by human
ingenuity but shown to Pythagoras by a divine nod. When the
philosopher, of burning ingenuity, long seething, inquired by what
reason he might firmly and constantly learn the momenta of the
consonances, it happened that, passing the smiths' workshops, he
heard hammers struck, from diverse sounds sounding one fittingness.
Astonished at what he sought, he approached the work, and long
considering judged that the strength of the strikers made the
diversity of tones. And that this might appear, he ordered the
hammers changed among themselves. When changed, the diversity of
sounds, receding from the men, followed the hammers. Then he turned
all care to examining their weights: and one was found the greater,
of twelve weights, another of nine, a third of eight, a fourth of
six. So the hammers of twelve and six weights sounded the diapason
consonance in the duple. The hammer of twelve to the hammer of
nine, and the hammer of eight to the hammer of six, in *epitrite* —
that is, sesquitertian — proportion, sang the diatessaron
consonance. Nine to eight in sesquioctave proportion resounded the
tone. So Pythagoras, returned home, weighed by varied examination
that in the aforesaid dimensions consisted the whole ratio of the
*symphoniae*: now fitting equal weights to strings and judging
their consonances by ear; now fitting in the length of reeds or
pipes the duple, the half, and the other proportions; often also
striking cymbals formed of diverse weights with a little hammer,
after the above-comprehended mode. From all these he gathered by
diverse experience the most whole faith, and was greatly delighted
to have found nothing diverse. And since in the now-often-noted
consonances, known by divine gift, consists the sum of all harmonic
discipline, they must be known to mind and ears. For in vain are
these gathered by reason and science, as the notable author Boethius
testifies, unless they have become most known by use and exercise.

[Editor's note — in plain words: The legend Regino retells:
Pythagoras heard smiths' hammers ringing in tune, weighed them —
12, 9, 8, 6 — and found the octave, fourth, fifth, and tone in the
weights. Then he checked it on strings, pipes, and cymbals until
ear and reason agreed.]

### 12. On the seven liberal disciplines

There are seven liberal disciplines, of which three — grammar,
rhetoric, dialectic — are gathered by reason and natural sense, not
seen by the eye or shown by the finger, since their force is in
speech, and speech — that is, human locution — can be heard, not
seen. The other four — arithmetic, geometry, music, astrology —
are in no way perceived clearly by the mind unless seen by the eye,
shown by the finger.

[Editor's note — in plain words: Three arts you learn by ear and
reason; four — counting, measuring, music, star-lore — you must
see and touch to truly know.]

### 13. The names of the strings, or intervals

Wherefore it seems to us useful and most necessary to discourse a
little first on the strings and their names, and to set under each
string its tone — so that when we begin to treat of the aforesaid
consonances more openly and plainly, we may send the sober singer
to the aforesaid strings, there to see with the eye and touch with
the finger what he perceives by ear from our word and writing. The
invention of the strings, or how the number of them was added by
divers at diverse times, we pass over for brevity's sake. Let the
nerves, then, be disposed in order, in which the bis diapason
consonance is perfected and shown, in this mode:

*Proslambanomenos*, and *Prosmelodos*. *Hypate hypaton*.
*Parypate hypaton*. *Lichanos hypaton*. *Hypate meson*. *Parypate
meson*. *Lichanos meson*. *Mese*. *Paramese*. *Trite diezeugmenon*.
*Paranete diezeugmenon*. *Nete diezeugmenon*. *Trite hyperboleon*.
*Paranete hyperboleon*. *Nete hyperboleon*.

The names of the above-written strings, since they are Greek, need
interpretation. *Proslambanomenos* is interpreted "acquired" or
"added": since *mese* was not in the middle place so as to join
both diapasons, but approached more to the hypatae, therefore this
one string was added above the *hypatae hypaton*. Whence by some it
is called *prosmelodos* — that is, joined or added to the melody.
*Hypate hypaton* — that is, principal of principals, understand "of
strings": for this was always first, before *proslambanomenos* was
added, and it was the string resounding the gravest sound — whence
some thus interpret: the *hypatae hypaton* were called as if
"greatest of great," or "gravest of grave." *Parypate hypaton* is
called the third string, as if placed beside the hypate, that is,
gathered; it can be interpreted "subprincipal of principals."
*Lichanos hypaton* is so called because *lichanos* in Greek is the
index finger — the Greek calls it *lichanon* from licking — and
just as in singing, at that string which was third from the hypate,
the index finger, which is *lichanos*, was found, therefore it too
was called *lichanos*.

[Editor's note — in plain words: Fifteen strings, lowest to highest,
each with its Greek name Englished: the "added-to-melody,"
"principal of principals," the "index-finger" string, and so on.]

### 14. The tetrachords

This tetrachord, since it is formed of longer and thicker strings
and renders a graver sound, is called *principal*. These *hypate
meson* follows — that is, principal of the middle strings,
understand "of strings." *Parypate meson* — that is, subprincipal
of the middles, or placed beside the principal of the middles.
*Lichanos meson* — that is, third of the middles. Then *mese* is
placed in the eighth place: *mese* is interpreted "middle,"
because it is always middle between seven and seven, or because it
is the end of the preceding diapason and the beginning of the
following, and is taken for two strings. In short, though the
eighth string resounds the diapason consonance with the first, not
for this cause are sixteen strings found in bis diapason, but only
fifteen — because *mese*, as we said, supplies the place of the
sixteenth string. *Paramese* — that is, placed beside the middle.
*Trite diezeugmenon*: *trite*, that is, third from *mese*;
*diezeugmenon*, that is, of the disjunct — understand "strings":
for when two tetrachords are joined with one middle nerve
intervening, this is called in Greek *synemmenon*, that is,
conjoined. Hence also *synaphe* is said — that is, conjunction —
whenever one middle term continues and joins two tetrachords. On
the contrary *diezeuxis* is called what can be said disjunction,
whenever two tetrachords are separated and differ from each other
by a full tone. *Paranete diezeugmenon* is so called because it is
placed beside *nete* — that is, the last of the disjunct. This
indeed is followed by *nete diezeugmenon* — that is, the lower or
last of the disjunct. *Trite hyperboleon* — that is, third of the
excellent, understand "strings." *Paranete hyperboleon* — that is,
joined to *nete*, that is, placed before the last of the excellent.
*Nete hyperboleon* is interpreted last of the excellent. In short,
these three last and newest strings render a more acute and slender
sound than the other strings; whence also they are called
excellent.

[Editor's note — in plain words: The fifteen strings fall into
tetrachords (four-string groups): the low "principals," the
"middles," the "conjoined," the "disjunct," and the "excellent"
high ones — with *mese*, the middle string, doing double duty so
fifteen strings cover two octaves.]

### 15. And the consonances it contains

Now let us see how in these fifteen strings all consonances are
found. *Proslambanomenos* is distant a whole tone from the second
string, which is *hypate hypaton*. The same *proslambanomenos* is
eighth from *mese* and resounds with it — that is, with *mese* —
the diapason consonance. And the same *proslambanomenos* resounds
with the fourth string — that is, *lichanos hypaton* — the
diatessaron consonance; which *lichanos hypaton* resounds with
*mese* the diapente *symphonia*, and is the fifth string from it.
Again *mese* is distant a tone from *paramese*; likewise the same
*mese* makes with *nete diezeugmenon*, which is the fifth string
from *mese*, the diapente consonance — which *symphonia* is called
diapason and diapente. The aforesaid *nete diezeugmenon* makes with
*nete hyperboleon*, the fourth string, the diatessaron consonance.
Again *proslambanomenos* with *nete hyperboleon* — that is, the
first string with the fifteenth — resounds the bis diapason
consonance.

Behold, you have in fifteen strings all tones and all consonances.
To one looking more diligently into the subtlety of musical
discipline, no more than five tetrachords are found: *hypaton,
meson, synemmenon, diezeugmenon, hyperboleon*. They can be
interpreted thus: take *hypatae* as principals, *mesae* as middles,
*synemmenae* as conjoined, *diezeugmenae* as disjunct,
*hyperboleae* as excellent. All which is too prolix to show in this
place: for we have not proposed to expound in this letter the
fullness of all harmonic institution, since its perplexed subtlety
the notable author Boethius could scarcely expound in five books.
We note only this: that the aforesaid five tetrachords are extended
in twenty-eight strings.

[Editor's note — in plain words: Every consonance lives somewhere
among the fifteen strings — Regino walks them off one by one — and
the five tetrachords are really all there is. (Even Boethius needed
five books for the whole of it; a letter must stop somewhere.)]

### 16. The names of the consonances, and their genesis

With these premised on the strings and their names, it is now time
to discourse a little more plainly and openly on the names of the
consonances and their voices, for the less understanding. Though we
have above commemorated for the most part all that pertains to
music, yet since the names are Greek they require an expositor.
*Diatessaron* can be said in Latin "of four" — not for the reason
that it consists of four numbers or parts, since it is made of the
*hemiole*, that is, of the half and the whole, but because it is
made of four sounds or voices distant from each other by a fixed
space. So *diatessaron* is a consonance of four voices, of three
intervals. What an interval is was shown above: the distance of
acute and grave sound. When then the greater voice contains the
whole lesser voice in itself — or the greater sound the whole
lesser sound — and besides, the third part of the lesser voice or
tone, it creates the diatessaron consonance; and it is always made
in the fourth string. In short, from *proslambanomenos* to
*lichanos hypaton* resounds diatessaron, as shown above.

*Diapente* is interpreted "of five" — *dia* in Greek is the
preposition "of/from," *pente* five. *Diapente* is so called because
it is perfected of five voices or sounds; it is of five voices, of
four intervals. When then the greater voice precedes the whole
lesser voice in all its quantity, and transcends besides the half
of the surpassed voice — whether in acuteness or gravity — this
*symphonia* is called *diapente*; and it is always made in the
fifth string. For *lichanos hypaton* to *mese* resounds the
diapente consonance.

*Diapason* can be interpreted "of all," for it is made of all
semitones, tones, and consonances: for diapason consists of
diapente and diatessaron. When then the greater voice surpasses the
lesser voice by double its quantity — whether in extension of
acuteness or remission of gravity — such a *symphonia* is called
*diapason*; and it is always made in the eighth string. For
*proslambanomenos* to *mese* resounds the diapason consonance, and
it is more principal and honorable than the other consonances.
Moreover the diapason-and-diapente consonance is formed when the
greater voice or sound transcends the lesser voice or sound in
acuteness or gravity by triple its quantity; and it is always made
in the twelfth string. In short, *proslambanomenos* to *nete
diezeugmenon* resounds the diapason-and-diapente consonance. The
*bisdiapason* consonance is said when the greater voice or sound
exceeds the lesser by quadruple quantity in acuteness or gravity;
and it is always made in the fifteenth string. For
*proslambanomenos* to *nete hyperboleon* resounds the bis diapason
consonance.

[Editor's note — in plain words: The names parsed: "of four" (the
fourth — four voices, three steps), "of five" (the fifth), "of all"
(the octave — it contains everything). The bigger the leap, the
farther the string: fourth on the fourth string, fifth on the
fifth, octave on the eighth, octave-plus-fifth on the twelfth,
double octave on the fifteenth.]

### 17. Of the tone, likewise, and of the lesser intervals

But *tone* is called when the greater voice or tone holds the whole
lesser voice or sound and besides its eighth part; and it is always
made in the second string. For *proslambanomenos* to *hypate
hypaton* resounds tone. And this is to be known: what is called
*epogdous* in arithmetic is called tone in music. *Epogdous* is
said as if *epiogdous* — that is, superoctave. The tone is divided
into two semitones — not that the semitones are altogether
half-means, but that "half" is wont to be said of what does not
reach the full measure. And that this may most easily be proved:
let the sesquioctave proportion be 8 and 9. Between these no middle
number naturally comes. Let us multiply them by two: twice 8 makes
16, twice 9 makes 18. Between 16 and 18 one natural number is
found, that is, 17. Let these three numbers be disposed in order:
16, 17, 18. So 16 and 18 compared keep the sesquioctave proportion,
and therefore make a tone. But this proportion the number 17, which
is middle between them, does not part into equals: compared to 16
it has in itself a tone — 16 — and besides its sixteenth part, that
is, 1. Again if 17 and 18 are compared, in 18 are found 17 and
besides its seventeenth part, that is, 1. So it does not surpass
the lesser and is surpassed by the greater by the same parts: the
lesser part is a seventeenth, the greater a sixteenth. But both
parts are called semitones, one greater, the other less. In what
numbers these semitones are found or shown we could here abundantly
and multiply insert, and annotate the diverse opinions of diverse
authors — which for two causes we omit: lest the epistolary
discourse pass into the magnitude of a book, and that be objected
to us which Horace objected: "when the wheel was running, an
amphora began to be made — why does a pitcher come out?"; and lest
we create more difficulty, and dull the readers' senses rather than
inform them with evident documents. Though the semitone is the
smallest part of harmonic institution, yet of all difficult things
it is most difficult, and needs laborious exposition. In a matter
naturally obscure, he who in expounding pours on more than
necessary adds darkness, does not remove density. Let this one
thing suffice to know: that half a semitone is called *diesis*, and
it is a fourth part of the tone; likewise *tetrastemoria* is a
fourth part of the tone, *tritemoria* is called a third part. The
greater part of the tone — that is, the greater semitone — is
called by the Greeks *apotome*, by us it can be called *decisio*
(a cutting-off). For it is natural that whenever something is cut
so as not to be divided into equal parts, by as much as the lesser
part is less than half, by so much the greater part exceeds the
half. If then to the semitone the *apotome* is added, the greater
semitone is made.

So the whole tone consists of *apotome* and semitone. Moreover the
semitone differs from the *apotome* by a *comma*. And therefore
*apotome* is nothing else than the lesser semitone and a comma. If
then one takes two lesser semitones from the tone, a comma remains.
The comma is the last sound subject to hearing. Philolaus the
Pythagorean includes the lesser spaces of tones in such
definitions: "*Diesis*," he says, "is the space by which the
sesquitertian proportion is greater than two tones. *Comma* is the
space by which the sesquioctave proportion is greater than two
*dieses* — that is, two lesser semitones. *Schisma* is half the
comma; *diaschisma* half the *diesis* — that is, of the lesser
semitone."

From which is gathered: though the tone is divided principally
into the lesser semitone and *apotome*, it is also divided into two
semitones and a comma — whence it is divided into two
*diaschismata* and a comma. The whole half of the tone, which is
the semitone, consists of two *diaschismata* — which is one lesser
semitone — and a *schisma*, which is half the comma. Though the
whole tone is joined of two lesser semitones and a comma, if one
wishes to divide it wholly, let him make one lesser semitone and
half the comma. But one lesser semitone is divided into two
*schismata*; half the comma is one *schisma*. Rightly then it is
said that the whole half-tone can be parted into two *diaschismata*
and one *schisma*. Whence the whole semitone seems to differ from
the lesser semitones by one *schisma*; the *apotome* differs from
the lesser semitone by two *schismata* — for it differs by a comma;
but two *schismata* make one comma.

All this we set down to show the difficulty of the semitones,
which can be expressed and shown more by proportions of numbers
than by variety of sounds. If anyone is a curious investigator of
such profundity and perplexed subtlety, let him read the third book
of the oft-named Boethius *On the Institution of Harmony*, and
there perhaps not only will his curiosity be satisfied, but he will
also be able to test his own ingenuity: for if he is found fit to
grasp these, let him know nothing in the seven liberal disciplines
will be difficult for him.

Omitting then what few intellects grasp, let us say what many
senses can gather. Know that though there are eight tones
completing the diapason consonance, yet there are not more than
seven dissimilar sounds, distant from each other by a tone — since
the eighth is the same as the first in the double, the ninth the
same as the second in the double, and so in the rest. Virgil,
conscious of this, says: *Est mihi disparibus septem compacta
cicutis fistula* — "My pipe is compacted of seven unequal reeds."
For from seven unequal reeds the pipe is made, since from seven
dissimilar voices or sounds the whole consonance is perfected.
Whence also some wish only seven tones.

But that Martianus introduces nine Muses singing diverse songs in
the nuptials of Philology pertains to celestial harmony, which is
discerned to have nine orders: for the seven planets, the eighth
celestial sphere, and the ninth earth. But eight resound all the
consonances; the ninth — that is, earth — perpetually keeps
silence, since it is immobile. Hence it is that when the eight
Muses were borne aloft in the celestial circles, the ninth, Thalia,
remained on earth. For when Urania had entered the celestial
sphere's circle, Polymnia Saturn's, Euterpe Jupiter's, Erato Mars's,
Melpomene the Sun's, Terpsichore Venus's, Calliope Mercury's, Clio
the Moon's, to resound sweet modes there — Thalia alone, left
behind in the very udder of the flowering field, that is, in the
earth's circle, sat down: because her bearer, that is, carrier, the
swan, impatient of the burden and also of flying up — that is,
since it would not bear her and fly up, that is, fly upward —
sought the fosterling pools, that is, nutritive and dear to it.
Thalia is interpreted as if *theton lia* — that is, "placing
sprouts" — which fits the earth beautifully, which sprouts green
grass and brings forth seed. Some say the nine Muses are so called
because the offices of the human voice are nine: for all speech is
formed by these nine things. First by the stroke of four teeth, the
repercussion of two lips, the plectrum of the tongue, the hollow of
the throat, the breathing of the lungs. For if any of these be
lacking, the voice will not be perfect.

[Editor's note — in plain words: The tone (9:8) won't split even —
16:17:18 proves it — so the semitone comes in two unequal sizes,
and below them lie the *diesis*, *comma*, and *schisma*, the
smallest sounds ears can catch. Regino begs off the full thicket
("he who pours on more than necessary adds darkness") and lands
where singers live: seven different notes to the octave — Virgil's
seven-reed pipe — and nine Muses because the heavens have nine
orders and the voice has nine tools.]

### 18. The difference between the practical and the theoretical musician

But behold, while we try to show the tones, we have wandered
farther through the vast and most profound forest of musical
institution, which is wrapped in such obscurity of darkness as to
seem withdrawn from human knowledge. For though there are very few
who weigh its force and nature by certain reason, yet what they
understand of it they cannot show clearly by work of hands. Again,
though there are many who work it with fingers or bring it forth
with sound of voice, yet they understand its force and nature
least. In short, if you ask a *citharoedus* or *lyricus*, or any
other possessed of knowledge of musical instruments, to show the
consonances — the kinship of strings, how that string is joined to
another by a rated proportion of numbers — he will give you no
answer at all from these. He will confess only this: that he does
it so, as he received and learned from his master. Since then music
hides itself for the greatest part both from the knowing and the
unknowing, and lies as if covered by profound darkness — hence they
say the fable of Orpheus and Eurydice was feigned. For Orpheus
loved the nymph Eurydice, whom, soothing with the sound of the
cithara, he took as wife. Her Aristaeus the shepherd, loving,
pursued; she, fleeing, fell upon a serpent and died. When her
husband knew it, he tried to recall her from the underworld to the
upper with the sound of the cithara, but could not. Orpheus is said
as if *oreon phone* — that is, best voice. Eurydice is interpreted
profound judgment. Orpheus then wishes to recall Eurydice from the
underworld with the sound of the cithara, but does not prevail —
because human ingenuity tries to penetrate the depth of harmonic
subtlety and to judge and discern it by certain reasons and recall
it to light, that is, to science; but it, fleeing human cognition,
hides in the darkness of ignorance.

Meanwhile it is to be known that he is not called *musicus* who
works it only with hands, but he is truly *musicus* who knows
naturally how to dispute about music and to unfold its senses by
certain reasons. For every art and every discipline has naturally a
more honorable reason than the craft exercised by the artificer's
hand and work. For it is much greater to know what each does than
to do what one learns from another. For bodily craft as it were
serves in service; reason as mistress commands. So much more
glorious is the science of music in cognition of reason, as the
body is surpassed by the mind. Whence it comes that though reason
needs not the act of working, the works of hands are nothing unless
led by reason. Add that we may say: bodily artificers took their
names not from musical discipline but rather from the instruments
themselves. For *citharoedus* is named from *cithara*, *lyricus*
from *lyra*, *tibicina* from *tibia*, and the rest are named by the
words of their instruments. But *musicus* took his name not from
any instrument but from music itself.

He then is *Musicus* who, with reason weighed, has taken up the
science of singing not by servitude of work but by command of
speculation. Whoever is utterly ignorant of the force and reason of
harmonic institution vainly usurps the name of singer, though he
know how to sing excellently. For not he who reads the lesson but
he who expounds the lesson is called master. And though boys chant
the psalms' words from memory, yet they are strangers to their
science, since they know not how to penetrate their mystic senses.
So as it does not suffice for learned men in sight to behold
colors and forms unless they also investigate what their property
is, so it does not suffice to delight the mind with musical songs
unless it is also learned by what proportion of sounds or voices
they are joined among themselves.

These few things from many we have briefly touched, lest we give
the musical and skilled singers laughter at our unskillfulness. But
we do not at all propose these to be read by Walcaud, nor provoke
his mind to learn such things — for in vain is the lyre sung to
the ass. But knowing that you are an egregious and perfect singer,
with your excellent prudence and with other skilled singers our
discourse and the reason of disputation are held. The words of this
letter are spoken to you only, and to very few other musicians — if
ever you read them; the rest we so remove that those who cannot
grasp with intellect may seem unworthy even to read them.

[Editor's note — in plain words: The man who plays but can't say
why is no *musicus* — he's named for his instrument, like
"cithara-man." The *musicus* is named for music itself: he knows
the reason. Orpheus failing to lead Eurydice up from the dark is
the picture of reason trying, and failing, to drag harmony's depth
into the light. (And Regino, with a wink, exempts one Walcaud from
the readership: don't play the lyre for the ass.)]

### 19. The syllables Nonannoeane, and the rest

Moreover, lest we seem to have passed over untouched anything
pertaining to the undertaken business, we put at the end what
above, if memory had not failed, ought to have been commemorated in
its place. It is asked by some what those words signify by which we
discern the sonority of the tones in natural music — that is,
*Nonannoeane*, and *Noeais*, and *Noioeane*, and the like — and
whether their sense can be interpreted. To which it must be
answered that they receive no interpretation at all, nor do they
signify anything: but they were found by the Greeks for this only,
that through their diverse and dissimilar sounds the admirable
variety of the tones might be comprehended by ear and mind
together. But now the prolix discourse awaits its end.

May supernal providence deign to keep your Excellency safe through
many courses of years, to profit all His holy Church! Most reverend
Father.

Here ends the letter on the institution of harmony.

[Here begin the eight tones of the musical art, with their
*differentiae*, etc., etc. — the *Tonarius*, a separate work not
translated here.]

[Editor's note — in plain words: Those chant-syllables —
*Nonannoeane* and company — mean nothing at all. The Greeks made
them up so the ear and mind could tell the eight tones apart by
their sheer sound. End of letter.]

## Glossary

- *apotome* — the greater semitone; the "cutting-off" (*decisio*).
- *comma* — the smallest sound the ear can catch; the tone's
  leftover after two lesser semitones.
- *consonantia* — consonance: dissimilar voices brought into one
  concord.
- *diapason* — the octave ("of all").
- *diapente* — the fifth ("of five").
- *diatessaron* — the fourth ("of four").
- *diaschisma* — half a *diesis*.
- *diesis* — the least part of the tone; a quarter-tone.
- *differentia* — the melodic turn sung on a psalm-verse's last
  syllable, marking the tone.
- *discantus* — see the Aegidius volume in this row.
- *epogdous* — the 9:8 ratio; the whole tone in numbers.
- *limma* — Plato's name for the semitone.
- *mese* — the middle string, joining the two octaves.
- *musica artificialis* — music made by human art, on instruments.
- *musica naturalis* — music that sounds with no instrument: the
  heavens, the voice.
- *musicus* — the knower of music's reasons — not the mere player.
- *nothae* — "bastard" antiphons, wandering between tones.
- *schisma* — half a comma.
- *symphonia* — a consonance, in the Greek word.
- *tetrachordum* — four strings in order; the unit of the scale.
- *tonus* — the church mode; also the whole-tone interval.
