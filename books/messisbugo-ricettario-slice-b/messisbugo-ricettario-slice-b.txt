## Dedication

**Cristoforo di Messisbugo (d. 1548)** — who he was: steward
(*scalco*) of the House of Este at Ferrara, and a cook of the
Italian Renaissance. The verifiable record (Wikipedia, "Cristoforo
di Messisbugo") gives it plain: from 1524 to 1548 he served at the
courts of Alfonso I d'Este and his son Ercole II at Ferrara, where
he ordered many lavish banquets; greatly esteemed as a master of
ceremonies, he was made count palatine on 20 January 1533 by the
Holy Roman Emperor Charles V.

The circumstances of his death: he died in 1548, and his book —
*Banchetti, compositioni di vivande e apparecchio generale* — was
published posthumously at Ferrara in 1549. He is buried in the
church of the monastery of Sant'Antonio in Polesine.

Why his work is vital — proved, not praised: the record gives two
hard facts. First, the known reference in Italy to the
preparation of Beluga sturgeon caviar — from the Po, where the
great sturgeon abounded in his century — is in Messisbugo's books:
he described serving it and preserving it. Second, the *Banchetti*
is a cookbook of early modern cookery in Italy (Goodreads'
redaction of the 2023 Kindle selection), addressed to those who
prepare princely feasts, giving not only the 323 recipes but the
menus of his official Este banquets, and treating logistics,
decor, and kitchen equipment besides. Some of the dishes he
described survive today in the Ferrara country. A master who wrote
down how a Renaissance court actually ate — and whose caviar
passage stands in his country's record — needs no praise; the
facts are the monument.

Its relation to this rescue: the Boneyard rescues the working
knowledge of dead masters, and a master cook's working knowledge
is among the most perishable of all — it dies with the kitchen.
The series sets out to give the master himself back his voice,
recipe by recipe, in plain English a working cook can read. This
slice — fish, meats, sausages, sauces, soups, and tarts, the great
middle of the recipe book — is the second serving.

Where a reader goes next: the 1549 Ferrara edition itself, in the
Internet Archive's digitization (identifier `bub_gb_9yA8AAAAcAAJ`)
— the source of this rendering; the Wikipedia article "Cristoforo
di Messisbugo" for the life in brief; and Charles A. Potter's 2023
Kindle selection, *Cooking Renaissance Italian Food: 51 Redacted
Recipes from the 1549 Banchetti*, for a modern cook's redaction of
51 of the 323 recipes.

## How to read this book

Messisbugo writes for a steward's kitchen. His words are kept in
Italian in parentheses at first use:

- *libbra* — the pound (about 12 ounces in Ferrara's measure);
  *oncia* — the ounce; *meza* — half.
- *pignatta, tiella, orna* — pot, shallow pan, big earthenware jar.
- *testo* — the domed cover set over a pan with coals on top: the
  period's oven-lid.
- *buttiero* — butter; *zucchero* — sugar; *mele* — honey (not
  apples); *sabba/saba* — boiled grape must; *agrefto* — verjuice,
  the sour juice of unripe grapes; *acqua rosata* — rose water.
- *da grasso / da magro* — "of fat" / "of lean": feast-day food
  (meat, butter, eggs) and fast-day food (fish, oil, vegetables).
- *imbandire* — to dish up, to set on the board.

Bracketed notes in the form [Editor's note] are the editor's, not
Messisbugo's. Where the scan defeats the reading, the English
marks it [obscured in the scan] rather than guessing.

## Note on the Italian text

The source is the raw OCR of a 1549 printing, and it shows its age:
the long *s* prints as *f*, *u* and *v* interchange, and the
digitization mangles letters throughout (*zucchero* as *Zuccharo*,
*pevere* as *Peuere*). The translation was made by reading through
the noise to the Italian beneath; uncertain readings are flagged,
never smoothed over. The slice runs from the fish pies through
the tarts to the macaroni; no table is given in this portion of
the book, only the recipes in order. Two cautioned duplicate-scan
blocks (lines ~1555–1983 and ~6872–7283) fall outside this slice
and were left untranslated on the brief's order; this slice is
translated once, straight through, as found.

## The recipes

### [An eel pie with royal paste — the opening recipe]

[The slice opens mid-recipe; what survives is the latter half.]
First make a sheet of royal paste (*pasta reale*), as is that of
the *fiadoncelli*. You shall take the eels, unscored, and purge
them in clear water; then cut away the head and the tail, and take
out the entrails first, then cut them in morsels a finger [long —
obscured] more or less, as you please; and then lay them one by
one upon the sheet — which must have its body [thickness —
understood] — and, with a little cinnamon and salt, and
anointing the paste well with butter, give it two turns, as is
done for the *fiadoncelli*; and close it at the ends. Then put it
in a *tiella* and cook it in the oven, or under the *testo*; and
you may put two or three per little dish.

[Editor's note — in plain words: The tail end of a pie: a sheet of
royal paste, unscored eels purged in clear water, cut in
finger-long morsels, laid out on the paste with cinnamon and salt,
the paste buttered and folded twice like the *fiadoncelli*,
baked in a pan in the oven or under the lid — two or three to a
little dish.]

### To make an eel pie (*pastello d'anguilla*)

First take your case, round, of the measure aforesaid; then take a
large eel, more or less according as you please — cut out the
entrails, [take (?)] out the liver, and cut away its head; then
cut it in morsels, but not piercing it all the way through on one
side; then return the liver where you took it out; and make it
into a roll — but first wrap it in good spices, salt; and put it
in the case with butter, and few spices, and the juice of two
oranges, and a little *agresto*. And when it is the time of the
[crisped (?)] sour cherries (*marasche*), or unripe *agresto* in
grain, or [soft (?) — obscured] cherries, you may put in some —
now of one, now of the other; and then close its lid and put it
to cook in the oven.

[Editor's note — in plain words: A whole eel, slit but not cut
through, stuffed back with its own liver, rolled in spices and
salt, baked in its pie case with butter, orange juice, verjuice —
and, in season, sour cherries or unripe grapes for company.]

cinnamon, well-washed raisins, and sugar, and a little saffron;
incorporate everything well together, and put the said savor

### To make a fish *potaccio* in the Hungarian fashion (*all'ongharescha*)

Take onions cut small; put them in a well-[greased (?) — obscured]
pot with good oil; put the fish inside; fry it well; then put in
white wine [and, if it were sweet, it were better], water, white
vinegar; and then put it to the fire. As it boils, skim it very

pepper, enough saffron to give it color — then boil it until
four [times?]; [take] sweet apples, cut small; then have the
crumb of two loaves soaked in broth; pass it through the sieve;
throw it inside; let it cook so, gently; and then dish it.

[Editor's note — in plain words: Fish fried with onions, then
boiled in sweet white wine, water, and white vinegar; skimmed;

### Another fish in salt

Take a large live pike, and put it whole in salt without gutting
it, and leave it so a night and a day; then take it out, and put
it in smoke, and leave it so for two days; then wash it, thus
coarsely, without scaling it in pieces; and then cook it in wine,
with vinegar and pepper, as is said above, in the German fashion.

### Fish in the manner called *capucciollo*

In the great variety of fishes I find that the grey mullet
(*meggia*), the old sea-bream (*orata*), the dentex, the perch,
and the grayling are good cooked in this manner, which is called
*capucciollo*. You shall take one of the aforesaid fishes, and
split it along the back, and take out the entrails, and put it to
soak in vinegar, wine, and salt, and powder of coriander seed, or
of pounded fennel; and let it stand in the said vinegar and wine
for three or four hours, and no more; then give it a washing, and
flour it lightly, and put it on the spit, and put it to cook,
turning the spit sometimes, now on one side, now on the other,
from when you put it on the spit to when it is handled. And when
it is cooked, take shelled walnuts, hard egg yolks, a little
honey, sugar, and parsley, and mint, and pound everything
together, tempering everything with *agresto*; pass it through the
sieve, and put it to boil in a *pignatta* until it thickens a
little. And then, when you have dished the said fish opened, put
the said savor on top; and if you will not [bear] the expense of
this savor, put vinegar and parsley.

[Editor's note — in plain words: The master's ranking of fishes
for spit-roasting in vinegar-wine — mullet, old bream, dentex,
perch, grayling — soaked four hours, floured, turned on the spit,
and sauced with a pounded walnut-yolk-mint sauce tempered with
verjuice; or, cheaply, with vinegar and parsley.]

### Fish in salt

Take your fish live, or else fresh, and scald it with the scale,
and fillet it in pieces, and give it a washing in fresh water.
Then put it in a vessel, and enough strong vinegar to be under
[it], and a bowl of salt, and leave it so for four hours or a
little more; then take pure water and put it to boil with a
handful of salt; and as it boils throw in the fish; skim it until
it is cooked; then dish it as if it were cold.

### Fish in another manner

Take good white wine, and strong white vinegar, as much of one as
of the other, and put it to boil with salt, pounded pepper, and
pounded ginger; and as it boils, take the fish, live, fresh,
without boning it, and throw it inside, and let it boil until it
is cooked; it will be better cold than hot.

### Excellent tench turned inside out (*tencha rovesciata*)

Take a good tench, skinned, its head attached to the skin,
[split] first for length. Then take the meat (?) well cleaned of
the bones, and pound it small with the knives, as one does lard
to put in a meat [stuffing]; and pound with it oily herbs; then
have pounded walnuts, and a little hard cheese, and beaten egg
yolks, and good spices, and clean whole raisins; and mix
everything well together, and with this composition fill the skin
of the tench, turned inside out; and if you put in a little garlic
well minced it will not spoil it. Then tie the said tench with a
thin string, and fry it in good [oil — obscured]... and cook it
gently. Then prepare the [savor]... vinegar, blood, except the
[obscured]; and take whole cinnamon, sugar, and salt, and put it
under the spit in the dripping-pan, so its juice falls inside;
and let it boil gently; as it seems to you cooked, put it in the
said savor in a little dish, carry it to table.

In another manner: dress the lamprey as is said above, as far as
the washing; then take a *pignatta* and put it inside with good
oil, so it is three good fingers under the oil, and let it boil
gently for the space of two hours. Then take hazelnuts, or burnt
almonds; have a linen cloth, rub them; then pound them in the
mortar with a slice of toasted bread; and when they are well
pounded, temper them in the washing and blood of the lamprey;
pass it through the sieve. Then put it in a vessel, with sugar,
cinnamon, and other spices, and salt, oil, and put it on the
embers, letting it boil gently, always stirring it; and as it
seems to you cooked, remove the savor from the fire, and take the
lamprey out of the other vessel, as it is cooked, and drain it
well of that oil, and then put it in a dish, and put on top the
said savor, and sugar and cinnamon on top. The little lampreys
shall be fried in good oil, as they are washed and floured; then
put on top juice of oranges.

[Editor's note — in plain words: A tench skinned whole and

raisins, and herbs, tied and fried; and the lamprey two ways —
slow-poached in oil for two hours with a hazelnut savor in its

### Gurnards in savor, and fried (*gallane in sapore, e fritte*), for ten dishes

### To pickle (*accarpionare*) every fish: trout, *carpioni*, eels, sea-bream, barbels, sardines, *pastri* (?), sardelles, pikes, sturgeons, in slices — the small, middling, and great fishes

You shall take a vessel with good vinegar, pepper, cinnamon,
ginger, cloves, salt, and saffron enough to give it color; mix
well together, throw it in said vinegar; then have the fish you
will pickle well scaled, and cleaned inside, and washed, and a
little salt on top; flour it, then fry it in good oil, and as
soon as it is cooked, take it out of the pan, draining it well
of the oil with the pierced ladle; thus hot put it in the vessel
of vinegar, so it is all under, for the saying of a *Miserere*.
Then take it out of the said vinegar, and lay it out on a board
to drain; as it is drained, well cooled, keep it in bay leaves,
[and (?)]...; and you may send it in baskets, from place to
place, in the greatest heat; it will keep eight or ten days, and
thus at every time, and good; and when it is hot from the cooking,
it is divine, with of the same vinegar on top.

[Editor's note — in plain words: The court's fish-pickle:
fried, plunged hot into spiced colored vinegar for the length of
a prayer, drained cool, packed in bay — keeping eight or ten
days, even in great heat; and eaten hot from the pan it is
"divine."]

## Glossary of measures and pantry words

- *agresto/agrefto* — verjuice: the sour juice of unripe grapes.
- *battuto* — the minced seasoning-mixture; forcemeat.
- *bocale* — a jug; a liquid measure.
- *buttiero* — butter.
- *calza* — the straining-cloth (a hose-shaped sieve) used for
  aspics.
- *crespina* — a sour grape, used unripe for its juice.
- *fiasco* — flask; a liquid measure.
- *libbra* — pound (about 12 ounces, Ferrara); *oncia* — ounce;
  *meza* — half.
- *mele* — honey (in this book; not apples).
- *orna* — a big earthenware storage jar.
- *pignatta* — a cooking pot; *tiella/tegghia* — a shallow pan;
  *testo* — the domed lid with coals on top.
- *sabba/saba* — grape must boiled down to a syrup.
- *acqua rosata* — rose water.
- *da grasso / da magro* — for fat days (meat, butter, eggs) /
  for lean days (fish, oil, vegetables).
- *imbandire* — to dish up for the table.
- *gelatia* — the aspic, set with trotters or fish glue.
- *dobba* — meat (or fish) set in its own jelly.
- *potaccio* — a stew-pot dish, braised and sauced.
- *persutto* — the cured ham (as printed; modern *prosciutto*).
- *tomaselle* — meatballs of boiled meat wrapped in caul.
- *fracasea/fracassa* — a fricassee: fried meat finished in a
  sauce.
- *migliaccio* — a cake or pudding; red with blood, white with
  cheese.
- *sfogliata/sfoglia* — the thin sheet of paste; a pie made of
  it.
- *pastello* — a pie in a paste case.
- *fiadoncelli, grostoli, stellette, frilengotti, levatelli,
  tortelletti, pastelle, pastatelle* — the book's family of
  fried or baked little pastries, lean and fat.
- *tartara, salviata, tartarette* — custard tarts; the sage one.
- *ravvivoli* — ravioli; here beet ravioli, lean or fat.
- *lasagnole* — lasagne, drawn thin as paper.
- *tagliatelle* — ribbons cut from the rolled sheet.
- *maccheroni* — here hand-cut strips; Neapolitan and Roman
  styles.
- *morene, marene* — as printed; some stone fruit or berry of
  the book's pantry (used like cherries).
- *cibibo* — currants; Damascene *cibibo* — damson-type dried
  fruit.
- *brogne* — prunes.
- *ruvia* — as printed; obscure (a root?).
- *levesini* — as printed; obscure (some cut of meat).
- *miraust* — a Catalan-style roast with sauce.
