DEDICATION: MICHAEL MAIER

Who he was. Michael Maier (1568–1622) was a German physician and scholar who rose to be court physician to the Emperor Rudolf II in Prague — the strangest and most alchemy-haunted court in Europe. He was a true believer in the hidden art of chymistry, and he gave it the most beautiful dress it ever wore: his Atalanta Fugiens (1618) set fifty alchemical emblems to fifty musical fugues, so the science could be seen, read, and sung at once.

How he ended. He died in 1622, in the middle of his labor — worn out, it is said, by his writings and his wanderings in the cause of the Rosicrucian manifestos he defended. He left behind a shelf of Latin books that only the learned could ever open.

Why his work is vital. Maier did not ask anyone to take his word for anything. He proved, by piling text upon text — Orpheus, Homer, Virgil, the Greek chroniclers, the Arab and Latin chymists — that the gods and heroes of the pagans were never gods at all, but hieroglyphs: picture-writing in which a whole secret art of nature was locked away. His logic is the engine of this book. Where others saw only pretty fables, he showed the lock, the key, and the door, and dared the reader to turn it.

What this rescue means. Robert Tilford pulls the unfinished and the untranslated out of the boneyard and sets them before common readers in plain words. Maier wrote this book in Latin for princes and professors; four hundred years later no free English of it exists for the rest of us. This Book II — the voyage of Jason and the Golden Fleece, read as the master-key to the whole hidden art — is Maier's own front door to his great decipherment, now opened in the plain tongue he never used.

Where the reader goes next. Book I (the gods of Egypt) came before; Books III through VI (the golden family of the gods, the festivals and games, the labors, and the fall of Troy) come after. The key cut in this Book — that every golden thing in the old fables means one and the same hidden medicine — unlocks them all.

THE TRANSLATION

BOOK II. ON THE GOLDEN FLEECE, OR THE EXPEDITION OF THE GREEKS.

[Printed p. 58]

After the sacred rites of the Egyptians come those of the Greeks — for the Greeks took their religion from the Egyptians, as we shall show. Orpheus was the man who carried them across. He lived in the time of Psammetichus, king of Egypt — long after the days when Egypt's own rites were first founded, and some centuries before the first Olympiad was ever run. [The exact year-numbers in the old print are garbled in the scan; the order of time is what matters.]

Six men, above all, were the authors and founders of the Greek religion: Orpheus, Musaeus, Linus, Melampus, Homer — and a sixth whom the old writers join to them. They did not invent gods out of nothing. What they did was this: they took the hidden art — the chymical art that the Egyptians had wrapped in hieroglyphs — and they wrapped it again, in songs and stories, so that the common people would worship and the wise would understand.

For you must know their intention. It was never their purpose that men should believe Jupiter to be a king in the sky, or Venus a beautiful woman, or Hercules a strong man who once lived. Their purpose was to hide the arcana — the most secret things of nature and of art — under the names of gods and heroes, as under a veil. The persons of the fables are hieroglyphic persons: pictures that stand for things. Whoever reads them as mere history reads with his eyes shut; whoever reads them as emblems finds the art.

[pp. 59–62]

To unfold all these veils would take a great volume, and more labor than one man's life. So we have chosen only the chief and most famous of the allegorical fables and stories of gods and heroes, and we treat them here not in a sharp or flowery style, as others do, but in a common and plain one, fitted to the matter rather than to fine words.

And in this second book we treat the Expedition of the Greeks — the voyage of Jason after the Golden Fleece. In the third, the golden genealogy of the gods and goddesses, and of the heroes sprung from them. In the fourth, the festivals and sacred rites of Greece, and the contests and games instituted by the same authors for the same hidden purpose. In the fifth, the labors, and at the last the taking and destroying of Troy, with the persons and the things required for it. In the sixth, we shall give such judgment as we can on the rest. From these, once they are understood, a like judgment may be made about all the other allegories we have not expounded here, to which these belong.

Orpheus, then — as he was the first author of religion among the Greeks, so he was the first inventor and publisher of the allegory of the Golden Fleece brought from Colchis. He devised it with a singular subtlety, and he made it with exactly the same intention and end as the writers of our own later age have made so many of their books: namely, that there should be a double sense in it — one on the surface, and one hidden within. The surface sense follows the letter, or if it leaves the letter, it expounds common morals or the nature of things. The hidden sense looks to the inward working, and whispers to the wise the arcana removed from common knowledge, hiding them from those of weaker wit.

This author wrote many works in verse — he himself lists them in his Argonautica — all showing the highest keenness and learning. Among them is his book On Stones, and his Cave of Mercury, from which all good things are to be learned: he paints it so vividly that any country mouse or city mouse — that is, any man, learned or unlearned — may easily sniff out what he means by Mercury's cave. We shall first run through the story of his telling, and then add our own notes, with the agreements of nature and of the authors.

[pp. 63–67: The story of Jason]

Jason was the son of Aeson, the grandson of Cretheus, the great-grandson of Aeolus, and beyond that of Jupiter himself; his mother was Polymede (some say Alcimede), daughter of Autolycus. Pelias, the son of Neptune, held Iolcus after Cretheus, who had founded it, having driven out Aeson; and Jason was given to Chiron to be raised. Chiron — himself the son of Saturn and the nymph Philyra — trained him in the art of healing and in all things that pertain to a humane life. When Jason grew up, accomplished in learning and manners, he came to the court of Pelias. But the more he excelled in every virtue, the less could he earn Pelias' love — for Pelias never looked on him without suspicion.

So Pelias, to throw Jason into dangers and into death itself under an honorable pretext, laid on him this command: to bring back to him the celebrated Golden Fleece from among the Colchians, with a stoutness of heart befitting so great a youth, once the perils were overcome. Hard as the command seemed, and hard to do, it put courage into Jason — for it was not his own rashness but another's order, which it would be a sin not to obey, that laid the task upon him.

So, all things made ready for the journey, he went aboard with fifty companions — nearly all of them tracing their birth from the gods — in the ship Argo, built by the counsel of Pallas, with a talking mast of Dodonian oak, to sail for Colchis. First he touched at Lemnos, to win Vulcan's favor; then at Mysia(?), Cius, Iberia, Bebrycia, and the Syrtes of Libya, where the ship could not sail for the nature of the shallows, and they carried the Argo on their shoulders through Libya for twelve days, until on the thirteenth, finding the sea again, they launched her. Falling in later with Eurypylus, the son of Neptune, they received from him the stranger's gift he offered — a clod of earth, from which, cast into the water(?), Medea prophesied many things. Then they came to Phineus, the blind prophet plagued by the Harpies, who was freed by the sons of Boreas; and he, though blind in the eyes of the body, was keen in the eyes of the mind, and showed the Argonauts the whole course. First the Cyanean rocks had to be passed — the Symplegades, or clashing rocks, as some call them, from which much fire burst forth — and they tested the danger by sending a dove through. Leaving Bithynia far astern, they sailed past the island Thynias, the Mariandyni, Acherusia, the city of the Encheleans(?), Carambis, the Halys, the Iris, Themiscyra, Cappadocia, the Chalybes, and countless other places, and came to the river Phasis, which flows down through the land of Circe — and at last to the Golden Fleece.

When Jason was brought with his companions to Aeetes, the king of the Colchians, the son of the Sun, he was kindly received; and when Jason put his request, Aeetes answered that he would grant him the Fleece — set as a prize before all — if he could win it by the might of Mars and by art. But when the perils looked unconquerable, Medea herself — [here the old print breaks into corrupt Greek verses, and the scan is too garbled to recover her words with certainty] — gave him her aid and counsel, and by her drugs and devices the thing was done.

[pp. 68–69: Against the literal reading]

If you would prove them gods, I can say nothing against the letter of the tale; if men, they seem too long-lived for history. Shall we persuade men, not children, that there were bulls breathing fire, and that from the earth, springing up more swiftly than mushrooms or gourds grow, there came forth crops and battle-lines of armed men, who at the cast of a single stone fell upon one another and slew each other to the last man? What shall we say of the sleepless dragon, said to be the son of Typhon and Echidna, and of his sown teeth? What of Medea's drugs and counsels, and countless other things? Shall we make a history out of these? It is a fable — and a tasteless one at that — that these sheep had fleeces of gold, or citron-yellow, or gilded, and that Jason took such pains to win them.

Those who understood golden mines by it — among them Strabo in his Geography — came nearer the mark. Nearer still those who read it as a book or parchment holding the secret of making gold, as Suidas does. But for such things as these no man would brave so many and such varied perils; Medea's drugs would not be needed, nor Jason — whose very name sounds the healer's art — trained by Chiron in manual experience, to win it; any man would do. Nor would Aeson need Medea to give him back his youth.

It remains, then, to fit the whole matter, from beginning to end, to the allegorical interpretation. Not to morals, properly and in the first place — unless we want to go mad, or to accuse of madness those who would invent such great things for morals' sake; though in the second place we do not deny that, like all other things, it can be turned to morals too. Nor to the works of nature known to country folk, but to those proper to philosophers, and most secret: namely, the golden Medicine, that treats of the remedy for the soul and for the body, made from gold.

With us agree those who went before — as Aloysius Marlianus in the volume he put out under the title of the Golden Fleece, and those who gathered so many treatises on the chymical art into diverse volumes and published them in German under the same title of the work, and countless others whom we pass over for brevity's sake — and their writings are in all men's hands, so we shall not cite them here. But that the whole wandering of Jason, with its perils, deeds, persons, and circumstances, pertains to the Philosophical Medicine — and to nothing else but the highest chymical artifice, the most secret of secrets, carried from the Egyptians to the Greeks and described under this most lovely allegory — this we shall now demonstrate in particular.

[pp. 69–72: The true interpretation]

What Jason is, his name, his upbringing, his family, and his deeds sufficiently prove. For his name expresses the healer — from iasthai, to heal. It is not enough to say, as some do, that he was no surgeon and gave no medicines to the sick, and therefore to drag him over to morals. For though he was no physician or surgeon — since Jason himself never existed in the nature of things — he is imagined and supposed to have been one: all the names agree with the name. Brought up by Chiron, who trained him in medicine — as he also trained Hercules and Achilles, young men of one calling and one family, the one most fit to tame monsters, the other to be the hero of the Trojan war — from Chiron Jason learned manual experience; from Medea, perfected counsel and theory, necessary for the completing of the work.

Here a note worth marking occurs: that any manual handling, learned from Chiron, must go before the theory in the seeking out of this work — the perfected theory instilled by Medea. For perfect theory goes before perfect practice, and practice does not go before theory — but many errors go before perfect theory. For errors are as it were the masters and scourges in the art; and errors are not known except by trying the work again and again. And so it appears that Chiron's institution goes before Medea's most perfect counsels — and the labors of the artificer order and dispose the work itself.

His family is from the genealogy of the gods: his father Aeson, his mother Polymede (or Alcimede), his grandfather Cretheus, his great-grandfather Aeolus, and beyond, Jupiter. Take away Aeolus, and the line begins from no one; and Aeolus himself, if he ever was, had no teacher — for he never existed in the nature of things either, any more than Saturn did with the nymph Philyra. Knowledge is the mother, as the name Polymede(?)/Alcimede(?) tells. Such parents, then — and such a teacher, and such a wife — befitted Jason, the future golden artificer, the physician, the keenest searcher into the things of nature.

Now we come to weighing his deeds. Those fifty companions of his journey are chosen, nearly all, as sons of gods. Why? Perhaps because that age produced gods? I think not — but because it pleased the poet Orpheus to assign such parents to his Argonauts, lest they seem born of low place, but rather of a golden family. For when all are sons of Aeolus, or Jupiter, or Mercury, or other gods and goddesses and nymphs, who would not take them for true gods? Among the heathen it was a sin to deny the gods; among us it is permitted — and we add that neither were the forefathers of the Argonauts gods, nor men.

[pp. 72–76: The dragon, the bulls, the sown men]

The dragon that never sleeps must be killed — and not alone, but with his brother and his sister. [The old print is corrupt here; the sense seems to be that the dragon's whole kindred must die together.] He is to be approached with certain narcotic(?) things cast into his jaws, and so extinguished. He is venomous, and therefore most deadly; he breathes fire — hence he is to be feared; he threatens with sharp teeth, and of these he must be disarmed.

But Jason, the philosophical physician, received from Medea most present remedies: the images of the Moon and the Sun to be set together, an unguent that fears no fire, with which to anoint himself, and a lustral(?) water of the virgin, warding off and extinguishing all burning. Armed with these remedies, he slew the dragon and drew his teeth — and this is what truly happens in this medicine. For the seed of our future harvest must be had — and it is not yet the seed that, sown in its own proper earth, will give fruit. But this seed must be drawn by art out of the dragon, as the teeth are drawn. Once it is had, he went on to tame the bulls.

What is meant by the bulls, we have already warned under the hieroglyph of Apis. For this is the true subject of the golden medicine — and the dragon's teeth are now ready too. So the fierce, fire-breathing bulls were tamed and put under the yoke. In short: the seed prepared beforehand was handed to these bulls for the earth, and the earth was turned about them until it had had enough — then it was fit and disposed to bear fruit. And Jason used the same aids in subduing the oxen as in overcoming the dragon. But the chief remedy was that pentacle-like thing with the images of the Sun and the Moon, conferring by a certain hidden force and property against the might of such great monsters — and whoever can obtain it need not doubt of victory. How he may obtain it, the philosophers teach in their books, when they speak of the Sun and the Moon and their conjunction, in the natural dissolving of things, in the radical things of nature, or the reduction into the first matter — which, since they are everywhere at hand, we need not bring in here.

The seed now handed to the earth — behold, armed men spring up! What does this mean? Before the imperfect(?) [the print is corrupt], as we said before, [Isis(?)] gathered the divided limbs, the wife and sister of Osiris; here the father is forced to gather the limbs of the son. The reason is the same in both places — for all these are one. The most ancient writers, who take all these deeds of Jason as things done, since they pertain to religion, and religion is not to be refuted without the shipwreck of religion, twist themselves marvelously here to block every gap, writing diversely of the Argonauts' return: for Orpheus in his Argonautica says they returned by the same sea by which they set out for Colchis; Hecataeus(?) [the name is corrupt] says otherwise. [A page of the old print is here badly disordered; the voyage home is not to our purpose.]

[pp. 76–78: Second allegory — the golden apples of the Hesperides]

Among the labors of Hercules, laid upon him by Eurystheus under a fated necessity, is numbered also the carrying off of the golden apples from the gardens of the Hesperides — a task so hard that, tried and abandoned in despair, with danger, by all who had attempted it, it seemed fit for Hercules alone, the tamer of monsters. Nor was it permitted — as will be said more fully under the labors of Hercules — for Hercules to refuse the burden laid upon him, however hard and however impossible it might seem to human eyes.

Those who had charge of the Egyptian allegories tell that Juno, when she married Jupiter, gave him for a dowry certain trees bearing golden fruit; and Jupiter, holding them in the highest price and wishing them kept in the safest place for an eternal memorial, commanded them to be guarded by the Hesperidean nymphs, with a sleepless dragon set over them. The Hesperides were the daughters of Hesperus, the brother of Atlas — named by some Aegle, Arethusa, and Hesperthusa. The dragon who kept watch night and day over the apples was born of Typhon and Echidna, had a hundred heads, and spoke with varied voices. Others add that the gardens, not far from the city Lixus in Mauretania, in the farthest part of Ethiopia(?), were ringed on every side by the mountains of Atlas — for Themis had answered him that a son of Jupiter would one day come and pluck the golden apples. Virgil too remembers the matter, in the fourth of the Aeneid:

[Virgil's verses on Atlas holding up the sky, the Hesperides' temple, and the dragon guarding the golden bough — the scan's Latin of the verses is corrupt; the drift is Atlas at the world's edge, the Massylian priestess, the temple of the Hesperides, and the sacred fruit watched by the dragon.]

Hercules was sent, then, to fetch these golden apples — but not knowing where in the world they were, he made for the nymphs dwelling in a certain cave by the Eridanus, as Themis(?); and by them he was taught to consult Nereus on the matter. Nereus, questioned by Hercules, told him to go to Prometheus, by whom he was advised what to do: namely, to send Atlas in his place, and himself to bear up the sky until Atlas returned. But others prefer it that Hercules himself, after speaking with Prometheus, set out, slew the dragon, plucked the golden apples, and carried them back with him to Eurystheus.

Pleasant as this allegory is to hear, it was not devised for idle ears, but has come down from the most ancient times to our own age to exercise the minds of the ingenious rather than to delight the sense of pleasure. For what is more welcome than gold? What lovelier than apples? What more beautiful than gardens? If a man hears that golden apples grow on trees, a certain inborn appetite makes him long to enjoy them. Hence the newness of the thing drew the hearer into no small wonder, and so gave very many the occasion to pry and search what kernel might lie hidden under so fair a rind.

For many of the heathen did not take the thing as told, to the letter, but interpreted it far otherwise. Aretas(?) understood, in his Libyan matters, sheep with golden(?) fleeces, kept by an inhuman and savage shepherd, and therefore said to be guarded by a dragon. But he seems seduced by the word melon, which means both apple and sheep. Pherecydes referred it to apples of a golden color, and Lucian agrees with him — to which Virgil's verse in the Eclogues also looks: "Ten golden apples for thee, girl; tomorrow I shall send more." Of the later writers, some sniffed a history under these things: that there were sheep of a yellow and golden color, belonging to the two brothers Hesperus and Atlas. Others apply it to astronomy, turning the golden apples into golden stars and the dragon into the sun(?). Some fit it to morals and the teaching of manners, as if misers guarding their riches were like serpents watching golden apples.

To these we shall first give our answer, and then our own interpretation. Since the subject must be understood according to its predicates, it is a marvel that Aretas makes sheep out of apples. For though the Greeks call both by the name melon, they differ far in thing and attribute: these bear wool, perchance golden; those are borne golden by trees. Nor is there any reason why all the authors should have put apples for sheep, or wished sheep to be understood for apples. Is there perchance one single author who simply used that ambiguous word, so that it is not settled whether he meant apples or sheep — and do not many remember this allegory? I think very many do. It is a play on the name, then, and a violent wrenching from tree-fruit to cattle — although cattle yielding golden wool are also found, as was told before. Utterly frivolous is what he adds: that the shepherd was inhuman, and therefore called a dragon. O wretched sheep, that with golden wool should suffer so savage a shepherd! They were surely worthy, once the shepherd was slain, to be seized by Hercules. Childish on both counts: to believe such a shepherd was called a dragon, and that sheep truly bore golden fleeces.

Those who grant they are apples, but of a golden color, look perhaps to citrons or oranges. But since this fruit, good in itself, is not so marvelous or rare, but spreads always, as now, in its native lands into its own kind, it does not seem worth the price to have guarded it so with nymphs and a dragon in a remote garden. Then too we read of golden apples, not of things imitating the color of gold. Those who make a fable of these things do violence to the story(?) — for from the sky(?) they make the tree which the dragon called Ladon (as Apollonius, book 4, of the Argonautica(?)) embraces; and they make Hercules the sun, whose twelve labors are the twelve signs of the zodiac — the stars scattering at the sun's approach. Some things fit; but those who opine thus seem to me to wander and to see nothing of things not understood, as if they were things understood — about which, enough.

In like manner, those who made these things for morals in the first intention confess that in the physical arcana they see nothing. In which, though we do not imitate Lynceus — who is said with sharpness of sight to have pierced to the inward parts of the earth and gazed, as if face to face, on the riches hidden in earth's locked closets — yet, unless the son of Saturn deceives us, we shall give an interpretation of this allegory more evident and more genuine than those given before.

[pp. 80–84: The true meaning of the apples]

By Hercules, the most enduring victor of so many labors, understand in the first place the artificer of the golden medicine, to be shown below, unfolded under his labors. This premised — though we do not deny that Hercules' example may also be used, not unfitly, for morals and for kindling virtues — by Atlas understand the most celebrated mountain of Mauretania in that time, as still, [rich] in minerals of every kind, whose use for the philosophical medicine [is great]: the garden of the Hesperidean(?) nymphs truly allegorically(?). For Atlas was the brother of Hesperus, who is set toward the setting sun with respect to Egypt and Greece. This is also called the seat of Mercury, which by some is thought to have been born in Atlas(?).

What some fable about Atlas — that he found the sphere and taught those Moorish peoples — does not agree with the truth; nor what others say, that he was a king of Mauretania turned by Perseus into a mountain of huge greatness, the head of Medusa being shown. For these are the poets' fictions and emblems, under which they shadowed and depicted other more secret things lying beneath. They call Atlas the astronomer because the stars can be most clearly observed from his top — as Africa and Egypt rejoice in a clear sky, untroubled by rains and clouds — and then because he is of such height that his peak seems hidden among the clouds and stars — and thirdly because he is thought, as it were, to bear up the sky on his shoulders. For he is said to present, in rising, the figure of a statue, with his peak as its head.

This Atlas — whose daughters the Pleiades are said to be (of whom one, called Maia, bore Mercury to Jupiter, as will be told below under the genealogy of the gods) — had for brother Hesperus, and from him three granddaughters, the Hesperides: Aegle, Arethusa, and Hesperthusa. By Hesperus and his daughters we understand the same mountainous places of Atlas. For though Atlas is taken as one mountain, yet under the same name and continuance it embraces all the other mountains lying about it; for Atlas runs through nearly all of seaside Africa, and so comprehends not one mountain but many thousands — as is likewise clear of Taurus in Asia, the Alps, the Apennines, and the Pyrenees in Europe, that under each single name countless mountains and hills are contained. So of the Pleiades and the Hesperides we understand that they are parts, and as it were daughters propagated from those great and broken-high mountains, Atlas and Hesperus.

From Maia, then, Mercury is not unworthily born, to be called the son of Atlas; and by Aegle, Arethusa, and Hesperthusa the trees bearing golden apples are kept — whence they are called the Hesperides' [apples]. The dragon set to guard them, born of Echidna and Typhon, is said to have a hundred heads. Of the same parents a numerous offspring is read to exist — and always venomous and monstrous: as the dragon deadly to Cadmus' companions at the spring, another at the Golden Fleece, a third here at the apples of the Hesperides, a fourth at the oxen of Geryon; likewise Cerberus, the Sphinx, the Chimaera, and other such dread and multiform monsters, of which in their places. They were called by the name of monsters by the ancients because of the vanity(?) of their nature, as described.

Of the dragon, as of the kind — and likewise of the native place of the golden apples — enough has been said from these things. Now we shall see only the species of such trees, and of their fruits. It pleased some of the natural philosophers that a tree is an inverted animal — for the reason that it has its roots toward the center of the earth, or toward the lower parts, which answer to the mouth of an animal set toward the upper parts; but if we consider the belly, and its mesenteric(?) veins, as the beginning of the distribution of nourishment through the veins to the body in an animal — [the print is disordered] — after the same manner the mines, from which metals are drawn and medicines made useful for the curing of human bodies, present a certain tree of the vegetable kind. For their roots — which are twofold, of sulphur and of quicksilver — are nearer the center, and thence, tending toward the upper parts, they marvelously extend their veins through the rocks and tracts of earth, so that, if they could be seen with the eyes, they would truly present the likeness of some great tree spreading its branches far and wide — as is plain from the experience of those who search out those veins.

If this is so, it is settled what is to be understood by those trees bearing golden apples. For these are trees not growing in the air, but under the earth, which bear the golden apples — provided they be discerned from the wild ones, which are of a better(?) nature. For here lies the whole difficulty: namely, to choose which are fit for the art. Therefore Morienus, near the end of his treatise, adds these notable words: "Likewise know that the greater root of this work is in the inquiry into the species, which are better for this magistery — for every mine is of many kinds."

Of these mineral trees growing under the earth nearly all the authors have made mention, in these or other words, hinting the same thing: namely, that the golden fruits are to be taken with their tree, and transplanted. So Flamellus: "The fixed grain(?) is not to be separated from its tree, nor can it elsewhere receive nourishment." And soon after: "From this let it be transplanted(?) after the taking away of the fruit, into a more fertile and nobler earth, which will give more nourishment in one day than the former in a hundred years, by the continual agitation of the winds. Another earth, because it is nearest the Sun, to grow, to be green(?)... and in every hour, without intermission(?), the tree bedewed(?) with the sweetest dew is beheld(?) and nourished more in one year than in the former earth in ten thousand." Greverus too demonstrates this mineral tree, whose roots, he says, are of Mercury, and its branches of the four imperfect metals; and in them he sets silver and gold as the flowers and fruits. Nothing more evident could be said for declaring the subject of the art, and for expounding what the golden apples of the Hesperides are, and where they grow.

Nor should it be laughed at as a marvel of nature's doing, when we see daily the grafting of shoots of divers kinds of trees upon wild or ignoble stocks, and the ennobling of the fruits brought about — so that what art can do in vegetables, which are of heterogeneous and not similar parts, is made manifest. Whence in the mineral kind above all — which is purely homogeneous — it is reasonable, and most known by experience, that it can be done much more easily. The same has long been known to happen in man himself, and in man's own body: how, namely, a nose, ears, and like parts can be repaired and regilded(?) out of living human flesh — of which thing we were eye-witnesses, when at Bologna we familiarly knew the most excellent Gaspare Tagliacozzi performing such works.

Countless examples from the works of nature and art testifying the same thing might be brought in — but we judged it superfluous to draw them out, since the sense of the artificers does not seek aid or proof from reason, but the contrary. Let this one suffice in this place: In some places, when in winter time all things are stiff with frost and ice, if a shoot from an apple(?) tree be cut off about the beginning of December and for some days be set in warm places or hot-houses in water, it puts forth leaves and flowers, conspicuous for their loveliness and newness — a thing known even to the vulgar. But the philosophers therein acknowledge the first matter lying hidden in the shoots of trees, and judge likewise of the golden apples contained in their boughs and roots. To him that understands, enough. Or if you please, we shall add a rarer example of vegetable nature, which may not a little illustrate this matter. There are those who about the [first(?)] of November hide cabbage leaves in a pit a foot and a half under the earth, and leave them there under the winter's snows and frosts until the 25th of March — and then they take out not cabbage leaves, but the purest seed, as sworn witnesses have affirmed to me that they did it yearly. In which transmutation we see that vegetable thing (as they tell of other plants too) reduced by putrefaction alone to its first matter, from which it was made — or rather to its last, to which it tends. Which must also be done, in its own manner, in the chymical artifice.

Our tree, then, bearing golden apples, is made known by reasons enough — as is the exposition of this allegory. But that Hercules might find this tree, [he was sent] to Prometheus — that is, to him named from forethought — to whom he was sent by Nereus to take counsel: who brought fire to men, and is held for a Titan bearing fire, a friend of Ocean, a contemplator of divine things, to whom with Vulcan and Pallas there was a common altar; by whose counsel Saturn was cast down by Jupiter into Tartarus. Whence Saturn, driven out, came into Italy to Janus, then reigning there, by whom, kindly received in hospitality, he taught him the way of life and the tilling of the fields. Janus, for the teaching received, rewarded him with half his kingdom, and on the coins first stamped by his device put a ship on one side and a two-faced image on the other — because the kingdom was ruled by the common counsel of Janus and Saturn, as Ovid tells in the first book of the Fasti.

But we do not doubt that by Prometheus is expressed the forethought and speculation of the mind about this work — whence he had a common altar with Pallas and Vulcan; nor is it said amiss that he gave Jupiter counsel about imprisoning his father and thrusting him down to the underworld. For from the speculation of the mind all this is foreseen: namely, that after the dusky blackness whiteness will follow.

Of the kingdom of Janus and Saturn the old histories indeed make mention, but out of the poets' fables. For the heathen were persuaded that Saturn, the god, the son of Heaven and Earth, was driven by Jupiter from the kingdom of(?)... [the print is corrupt] ...and many other things besides. But all these are hieroglyphs, made by the Egyptians or Greeks to wrap up, as in veils, those things which they wished.

By the two-faced Janus we understand the one matter or thing, by us called Rebis, containing two things — or if you would rather, the double mercurial substance. Why Saturn is joined to him as partner of the kingdom has a far other reason, which anyone may divine who knows not the cause why of old at Rome a Triton was seen above the temple of Saturn. For those are riddles, and somewhat obscure. But why money was first coined under Saturn's auspices, the reason is evident: because he is the first of the planets, who bears the keys to the treasures — if you look to his color and order. Money (pecunia) is so called from cattle (pecus) — not, as has been vulgarly held, because a sheep could be bought with it, but because it had a sheep stamped on it, together with a ship — that is, the Argo. For that it was the badge of that navigation is not doubtful, containing both the ship and the beast whose fleece was golden.

Besides many others, Greverus among the later writers makes mention of these golden apples, and he may be read himself. From the same garden of the Hesperides was also taken the golden apple of Discord, the first cause of the Trojan war — of which we shall speak below, in Book 6.

[pp. 87–88: Atalanta]

In the first place, those three golden apples which Venus is fabled to have given to Hippomenes, to be thrown in Atalanta's way: for this royal maiden was not only the fairest in beauty, but also the most celebrated in hunting and in the art of throwing the javelin, and she drew many suitors to her. But her father Schoeneus would wed her to none, unless he should outrun her — her, the swiftest of foot — in running; to the victor the maiden, to the vanquished death, was appointed. When many had tried in vain and met death, Hippomenes, by Venus' counsel and gift, threw the three golden apples, one after another, before the running maiden — and she, held back by the desire of picking them up, was beaten, and given as wife to Hippomenes. But he, impatient of delay for love, lay with his bride in the temple of the Mother of the gods, and kindled the goddess' anger against himself — whence both, conspicuous for their lion-like fierceness, ranged the woods as lions.

Atalanta is said to have contended with men in hunting, and to have been rewarded by Meleager with the hide(?) for the slaying of the boar, of notable greatness. And when, hunting near(?) Stymphalus(?), at the shrine of Aesculapius, she labored [with thirst(?)], she is said to have struck [the rock] with the point of her spear and drawn forth a most cold fountain bursting from the stone. That these are chymical things no one does not see — unless he be blinder than a mole.

Some nevertheless apply it to denoting loves or avarice, or marriages won by gold — which we do not grudge them, so long as they do not grudge us that we restore the more hidden physics, as by right of return, to its own proper right. That the maiden is our royal matter is brought in by the testimonies of many; and she is fleeting, and has wings on her feet — namely, Mercury's sandals. Therefore she outruns every runner, unless she be handled by art and with a gentle(?) hand. How this must be done, this allegory of the wise shows: take then the golden apples, and cast them before her one after another — and you shall come off victor. Nothing more open, nothing more fitting.

Afterwards, in the shrine of the Mother of the gods, they join in the bond of love: that is, in the known vessel, or in the house of the old woman(?), they are turned into lions — because they begin to be fixed(?), and to have dominion over other animals. For whatever fleeting things they easily take for prey, and turn into their own nourishment. And why do those overcome by the maiden suffer death? Because, as Bacall(?) says in the Turba, "Nothing breeds more grief of heart than error in this art." For while he gapes to have the world, he will find nothing in his hands.

She slew the boar because she is another Diana. She drew the fountain from the rock near the shrine of Aesculapius because the hard stones of the philosophical rock give water: so Ripley in his(?)... "He brought water out of the earth, heaven out of the hard stones."(?)

[pp. 88–91: The Ceryneian hind]

Among the golden allegories we may also number that hind, famous for her golden horns, brazen-footed, caught by Hercules. And what artifice is it, you will say, to catch a hind? You will judge it altogether childish that such a thing should have been commanded — or done — by so great a hero as Hercules is believed to have been. I confess, the matter would be lighter than should be taught by letters, unless great arcana lay hidden under the hunting of this hind.

The hind had golden horns and brazen feet; she was sacred to Diana, and inviolable. Not with arrows or darts, then, nor with dogs, but by running was she to be caught. For it was not fitting that blood should flow from wounds in her; for so, her strength lessened or utterly quenched, she would perish: alive and whole, with health unharmed, she was to be taken — by running alone, then, was she to be wearied out.

She roamed on Mount Maenalus, where the boar and the lion and beasts of that kind dwell. To her Hercules was sent by Eurystheus, to bring her back — and he is said to have spent a whole year in running before he could weary her. But at last, fleeing to Mount Artemisius and to the river Ladon, when she was now now(?) crossing over, she was caught, and carried to Mycenae on his shoulders(?).

Then they say Eurystheus was so struck with fear at Hercules' valor that he got himself a brazen jar for a hiding-place, nor would admit Hercules into the city. But she is of the fleeting animals, of black blood and melancholic flesh — and therefore of inborn fearfulness. She seeks safety in running, if she be pressed, and therein is most excellent in swiftness. But since she has brazen feet and golden horns beyond the common nature of hinds, she is somewhat held and hindered in her flight, so that she can be wearied. For unless there were in the philosophical matter that very fleeting and volatile thing — that golden light, or golden radiance — it would scarce ever be observed to stand or be fixed. This is plain from the countless testimonies of the philosophers. So the Rosary: "Quicksilver," it says, "by itself avails nothing(?); but when it is mortified with its occult body, then it avails and lives with incorruptible life — and this body is of the nature of the Sun." So Arnoldus: "In our stone are Sun and Moon in virtue and power, though(?) also in nature. If this were not so, neither Sun nor Moon would be made thereof — for Sun and Moon in our stone are better than the common ones in their nature, because Sun and Moon in our stone are alive, and the common ones are dead, in respect of Sun and Moon in our stone." Therefore the philosophers called this stone the Sun and the Moon by turns(?)[the print is corrupt] — because in it they are potentially, and not visibly, but in virtue and essence.

Hence some of the later writers too remember this hind — but they join the monoceros(?) to her, when they say: "The philosophers say there are(?) in this wood, one praiseworthy and fair and eager; with a strong hand(?) I see..." [corrupt] Another unicorn the philosopher shows; and Basilius Valentinus tells that an ass(?) putrefied under the earth is turned into a stag with branching(?) horns. It has brazen feet, gleaming and white(?): for the thing whose head is red, whose eyes are black, and whose feet are white, is our magistery. And it is known how often the philosophical AES(?) is repeated(?) — for this is the foundation and base of the work, as not unworthily the feet present in the animal.

She is sacred to Diana, because it was the custom to sacrifice(?) the hind to her, as is plain in the allegory of Iphigenia. Diana is the lunar nature: for Sun and Moon is the whole composition. She is wearied by running — that is, shut in(?) by ascending and descending in motion(?), lest she flee, until at last the matter settles(?) in the bottom and ascends no more — [then] it is plain(?) in the glass. But if(?) there were(?) the force of fire, violence would be done to the fleeting animal. She flees to Mount Artemisius and is caught at the river Ladon — because thus the virgin had to remain inviolate, as sacred to Diana, who is called Artemis, as it were artemis(?), that is, cutting the air(?), as some will have it — or, as others, because she makes whole, as Strabo is author, book 14. Ladon is a river of Arcadia, from whose daughter Metope and Asopus, the Theban river(?), the nymph Thebe is fabled to be born, who gave the city its name. Ladon is also the name of the serpent guarding the golden apples, as was said. To whichever it be referred, the sense of the matter agrees.

On Maenalus, a mountain of Arcadia abounding in mines — whence the Maenalian verses — she roamed, because that place was most celebrated by the poets. On Hercules' shoulders(?) she is carried to Mycenae — [but] is not given up(?). But why did so great fear seize Eurystheus at the hind caught by Hercules, when at other times he had laid low more monstrous wild beasts — the lion, the boar, the hydra, and the like? So it seems that by art more is conquered than by Mars: by lingering(?) rather than hastening, by gentleness rather than violence. Hence he spent a whole year in continual running and pursuing the beast — for so the manner of the work requires. [A year is(?)]... [the print is here corrupt; the sense is that the work needs a full year's time, and cannot be perfected in a shorter space.]

The mythologists refer these fables to another meaning, expounding them at their pleasure: as that Hercules did the same(?), who(?)... [corrupt] — but these things, wrenched from their genuine nature to a foreign one, are neither constant(?), nor what use they may be to the mind of him that reads or hears, do they bring — besides(?) the darkness which we have in some measure dispelled(?).

[pp. 91–92: Midas]

Midas holds the symbol of gold besides(?): that, having hospitably received Silenus, the companion(?) of Bacchus, a partaker of humanity, he obtained his wish — namely, that whatever he touched should turn to gold — but, famished(?) amid his wish, unless(?) led by repentance of his vow he obtained again the un-gilding of the gilding, by this remedy: that he should wash himself in the river Pactolus, which carried this golden taint down with it to the sea(?), whence it left(?) golden sands.

A pretty little fable for ears not ungrateful, nor for eyes — since it shows nothing but gold, which it makes lovely to all — most apt for declaring and bridling the insatiable greed of misers. For that Midas is by his wish agreed to be many, but by his deed(?) very few: because to desire the gathering(?) of riches in hope is in every man's will, but to obtain it is in God's gift. But what god is he who granted the gift to the prayer? A fictitious and hieroglyphic one — not he who was truly God and is called so — who gives to mortals things more useful than pleasant, as a father to his little sons. For he who, as Midas(?), is the first of the golden gods(?), and therefore the bearer(?) of so great a gift... [corrupt] But that he should have kindly received Silenus, his teacher(?) or companion... [corrupt]

The common saying about the Sileni of Alcibiades has grown up: that without(?) they are(?)... [corrupt — the Alcibiades/Silenus image: without, ridiculous; within, divine images]. For sometimes(?) in(?) a despicable(?) little(?)... [too corrupt; the drift:] By this Silenus, then — as also by Pan and the Satyrs, the companions of Bacchus on his journey(?) — nothing else is understood but the wild and woodland(?) greenness(?) of the one philosophical [matter](?), which, the more humanely and gently it is handled, the sooner there follows from it the mighty god of gold, repaying the favor with manifold(?) thanks. This the wish of Midas denotes.

Properly, then, the golden(?) force impressed on its subject is understood by this — which is immense and ever multipliable, unless it be bridled and its(?) force quenched(?) in its own proper water. Of this the philosophers treat in many places — above all Lullius in his(?) Theory(?), cap. 23, where among other things: "And if(?)... she(?)... when she has(?)... that she is of greater(?) meditation(?), nor ought you for this to marvel at him the less(?), but to honor him the more, since it can come to a like(?) term by the discretion of the wise operator, and by the doctrine(?) which art confers above(?) in the multiplicative substance."

[pp. 92–94: Other golden things; the end of Book II]

To these we shall add also other golden symbols and hieroglyphs of antiquity, which truly were not in the nature of things, but were devised by artificers and poets, that other things might be declared by them: as the stone which Hesiod(?) remembers, set for a monument to mortals on Helicon — that they might see and touch this stone, so many most ancient poets feigned of Helicon to be climbed, on whose top wisdom is to be drawn from the Muses' spring. Saturn with Janus now(?); from whose blood scattered drop by drop(?) Chrysaor, the father of three-bodied(?) Geryon, was born — of whom more fully in his place below.

That we may meanwhile bring other things hither — [among them] the golden [verses](?) which are said to have been [Pythagoras'](?), lest(?) the philosophers should(?) hide their arcana in mystery(?); and the golden sayings(?) which still survive; and the golden crown, worn on the head by him first of all (perchance(?) the king of many nations(?)) — are numbered. But of these elsewhere more fully.

Now we proceed to the golden genealogy of the gods, to be handed down in the following book.
