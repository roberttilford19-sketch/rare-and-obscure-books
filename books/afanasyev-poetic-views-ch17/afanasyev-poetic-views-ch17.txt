THE MAN AND HIS BOOK



Alexander Nikolaevich Afanasyev (1826–1871)

Alexander Nikolaevich Afanasyev studied law at Moscow University and went to work, as educated men of his generation did, in the service of the state: he held a post in the Main Archive of the Ministry of Foreign Affairs, among the old papers of Russia. The archive gave him his training — documents, old words, old beliefs — and the archive also ended his career. In 1862 it was discovered that he had been passing documents from the archive for publication abroad, and he lost his post. He spent his last years in poverty and died in Moscow in 1871, aged forty-five, of tuberculosis.

What he did with his life, between the archive desk and the early grave, is the reason this book exists. Afanasyev gathered and published nearly six hundred Russian folk tales, one of the largest collections of folklore ever made in the world, and the work earned him the standing of a Russian counterpart to the Brothers Grimm. The tales made his name, and the tales travelled: they have lived in English for more than a century. But the tales were, for Afanasyev, the evidence — and the great work built upon the evidence is the book from which this fascicle is taken.

Poetic Views of the Slavs on Nature, issued in three volumes at Moscow in 1865–1869, is his theoretical masterwork: an attempt to read the whole treasury of Slavic tale, song, spell, riddle, and custom as the broken remains of one old poetic vision of the world, comparing it at every step with the traditions of the other Indo-European peoples. It is a nineteenth-century system, and it must be read as one: some of its etymologies belong to its age, and modern scholarship — the 2024 survey in Religions says it plainly — notes that some of the materials it cites are unreliable. But the same scholarship treats the book as what it is: a grandiose compendium of folklore and historical-ethnographic material, one of the founding monuments of Slavic mythology studies. To this day, that survey records, the book has never been translated into English; among our references, after research, none has been found.

Here, then, is the Boneyard pattern in its Russian dress. The tale-collector got his English shelf a hundred years ago; the thinker who explained the tales has stood outside the door ever since. Robert Tilford's series exists for workers of exactly this kind — men whose names the world half-remembers, whose books it cannot read. This fascicle returns the man's own system to the same shelf as his stories, under his own name, and in the plain English a humble reader deserves. Afanasyev proved his logic the hard way: he wrote it down, in three volumes, at the cost of everything he had. The least a rescue can do is carry it across the border of language.

CHAPTER XVII — THE TREE OF LIFE AND FOREST SPIRITS



PART I: THE WORLD-TREE (printed pp. 277–325)



The rain-cloud, a tree turned toward earth

The rain clouds, darkening the vault of heaven with their wide, many-branched canopy, were likened in deepest, immemorial antiquity to a giant tree that embraced the whole world — a tree whose branches are turned downward toward the earth and whose roots reach to the very highest heaven. [A memory of this image survives in a Russian spell, which says that on the island of Buyan stands a white birch, branches down and roots up, and on that birch sits the Mother of God.] Of this world-tree the most living traditions are preserved in all the pagan religions of the Aryan peoples; and after the excellent investigations of Kuhn there is no doubt that this fabled tree is the mythical picture of the cloud: the living water (amrita) at its roots and the honey dripping from its leaves are metaphorical names for rain and dew, and the sea where it grows is the waters of the heavenly ocean.

The Aryans distinguished three heavens rising one above another: the kingdom of air and clouds; the clear blue vault (dyaus); and the kingdom of eternal light, from which the sun and the other luminaries borrowed their wondrous brilliance, and from which came lightning itself, which gave earthly fire its being. From this third heaven the ever-unwithering fig tree (aśvattha) spreads its broad branches; beneath it dwell the souls of the blessed, and together with the gods they taste the immortal drink. Its branches go downward, its roots upward; upon it rest all the worlds; out of it the gods built heaven and earth; from its leaves drips amrita, and beneath it grow healing herbs. Later Indian traditions call this tree Pārijāta: it grows in the kingdom of Brahmā, beyond the stream that grants eternal youth, and bears all the fruits that exist in the universe. On its top sit two birds, one of which pecks the sweet fig; other birds dwelling there press and drink the juice of amrita. Of the lord of heavenly flame — the lightning-bearing Agni — it was told that fleeing from the gods he found refuge in the fig tree.

Zend mythology knows a heavenly tree of life from which came all healing and other plants and grains upon earth. They called it the painless tree, and Harvisptokhm — endowed with all seeds; it is also called the eagle's tree, because an eagle-like bird, Cinamru (the Senmurv), sits upon it: when it rises from the tree, a thousand new branches grow; when it settles, it breaks a thousand old ones and shakes the seeds from them. Another bird, Tschamros (Čamroš), sits always at the foot of the tree, gathers the falling seed, and carries it where Tistar (Tishtrya) takes up the water; receiving the seeds together with the water, Tishtrya scatters them in rain-showers over the whole universe. The most ancient likening of raindrops to grains sowing the earth gave birth to the myth according to which, together with the rain — the producer of earth's harvests — all vegetable seeds also fly down from the tree-cloud. Beside the painless tree stands the white plant Gaokerena, from whose juice is prepared the heavenly haoma (soma), the drink granting health and immortality, and which will therefore have an important part in the great act of the universal resurrection. Both wondrous plants serve to denote one and the same idea, only in two separate poetic images. Both grow in the sea Vourukaša, on whose shore lives a lizard created by the evil god of darkness, Ahriman (Angra Mainyu), which strives to destroy the haoma; but for its protection 99,999 bright spirits (the Fravashis) are appointed. This lizard corresponds to the demonic serpent of the most ancient epoch, which holds back the streams of rain.

In Germany to this day a cloud that resembles a tree in outward form is called Wetterbaum, Adamsbaum, Abrahamsbaum — a weather-tree; from the side toward which its top is turned they expect wind; if it blooms — that is, flashes with lightning — there will be rain. The Edda tells of the old, world, middle tree Yggdrasil. This tree is an ash (askr); its branches stretch through the whole universe, spreading over heaven and earth; its three roots run — one into the heavenly kingdom of the Æsir, another into the abyss of Hel (the world of death and destructive flame), and the third into the land of the giants of cloud and mist (the hrímþursar). Beneath each of the three roots flows a sacred spring. The heavenly spring is called the Well of Urd, beside which the gods took counsel about the creation of the world, and where they still gather to judge the affairs of the universe. Every morning the prophetic maidens — the Norns — draw water from this spring and sprinkle the branches of the world-ash with it, and from this comes the dew that falls upon the earth. The second spring, in the land of the giants, is Mimir's well, whose waters endow gifts of wisdom and foreknowledge; the name of the hellish spring is Hvergelmir. According to Kuhn's explanation, Yggdrasil is "the horse of Ygg" (Ygg being one of the names of Odin), which agrees with the common Indo-European picture of flying clouds as horses, and with the tradition of the Elder Edda that the horses of the Valkyries shake dew from their manes into the deep valleys.

Upon the branches and at the roots of Yggdrasil various animals are placed: an eagle, the squirrel Ratatoskr, four stags, and serpents. The chief serpent, Nidhöggr ("the malicious biter"), lies at the hellish spring and gnaws the root of the ash; the squirrel runs up and down, trying to stir up strife between it and the eagle sitting at the top. The eagle's name is unknown; but it is a wise, much-knowing bird, and between its eyes sits the hawk Vedhrfölnir. In the image of the squirrel, myth personified the gnawing teeth of lightning; in the light-footed stags that eat the ash's branches, the four chief winds, whose stormy blowing scatters the clouds. The birds seated on the branches of the world-tree are sacred birds, carriers of lightning and of the immortal drink (the rain-soma); the serpent is the personification of the evil demon who detains the blessed moisture of the rains and produces drought and failed harvests. [The memory of the old enmity of serpent and lightning-bird is met in many folk tales: in the underground kingdom — the realm of clouds — an eagle and his mate built a nest on a high, ancient tree; a many-headed dragon climbs the tree to devour the young eaglets; a hero appears, kills the dragon, and in gratitude the birds carry him out to the white world. A Serbian song tells of an apple tree on whose top a falcon built his nest, while below at the root lies a serpent threatening the falcon: "If I let loose living fire, I will scorch your nest, I will seize your nestlings."]



The ash, the manna, and the dew called "honey-fall"

From the tales of Odin's paradise (Valhalla) we learn that in its midst stands a mighty tree (Læraðr), whose leaves the goat Heidhrún browses, while the stag Eikthyrnir feeds on its branches. The goat is the animal consecrated to Thor; from her udders flows daily a full vessel of mead, drained at the feasts of the blessed heroes — just as nectar flows from the horn of the goat Amalthea. From the stag's horns flows inexhaustible water, pouring down into the underworld and giving birth to the stream Hvergelmir.

The occasion for joining the picture of the tree-cloud, in which the honey of amrita is enclosed, with the fig tree and the ash was this: from figs a sweet drink was really prepared in antiquity, and a particular kind of ash (Fraxinus ornus) exudes from beneath its bark a sugary juice called manna. Swarms of bees love to gather this juice; by popular belief bees feed on dew, and in the poetic language of the Edda the dew falling from Yggdrasil is called honigfall — "honey-fall." The ash begins to exude its sweet juice with the coming of spring, at the very time when the heavenly springs are unlocked and the gods send mortals the living water of rain and dew. [It is very probable that on the same ground healing properties were ascribed to the birch as well, whose sweet juice peasants love to drink; Czechs still drink it to enjoy health and not be barren in marriage.]

In closest kinship with the Scandinavian Yggdrasil stands the Greek world-tree meliē — the ash (of the same root as the word for honey) — from which the human race sprang. Virgil mentions an ash that reaches Tartarus with its roots, and as deep as its roots go down into the earth, so high do its branches stretch amid the spacious sky. In the Aeneid he speaks of the ash branch with golden leaves and fruits: no one could descend into the underground kingdom without bringing Proserpina the fruits of this branch as a gift; and as soon as one fruit is torn from it, another at once grows in place of the one lost. The Lithuanians call the ash the tree of righteousness; an old tradition says that the gods themselves, descending to earth to look more closely at the deeds of men, loved to gather in the shade of a thick, spreading ash and from there pronounce their true judgments. From beneath the roots of the world-tree flowed springs of wisdom and of all knowledge; here the gods came to quench their thirst, and therefore they knew all that was past, present, and to come, all good and evil, and could render holy and unalterable judgment. Under the influence of the same view, a dwelling here was given also to the maidens of fate, who determined the lots of men. Later we shall bring evidence that places of popular judgment were from of old appointed amid forests, under the shade of sacred trees, and that in the rustling of their leaves men sought omens of coming destinies.



The ash against the serpent; the German tale of the three metals

As rain — the life-giving juice of the tree-cloud — destroys the ruinous action of the drought produced by the demonic serpent, so the juice of the common ash can, by popular belief, heal the bite of the creeping snake. In Germany, to those bitten by a snake they gave this juice to drink, in hope of complete recovery. In Rus' they think that snakes (vipers) cannot bear the smell of the ash, and therefore if one puts on clothing washed in lye of ash wood or sprinkled with a decoction of its bark, leaves, twigs, and root, and wipes the whole body with this decoction, no snake will sting such a man — at the sight of him it will fall into a stupor. For a snake-bite they wet the sore place with ash decoction, or touch the wound with a finger dipped in it. A branch of the ash subjects all reptiles and snakes to the power of the sorcerer from a distance of several steps. To shelter from the blows of lightning, which pursues the devil, the best means, in the Czechs' opinion, is to stand during a thunderstorm under an ash.

The tradition of the world-tree and the springs flowing from it is not yet lost to popular memory. A German tale tells the tradition with these details. Once a shepherd boy drove his sheep to the field and saw a wondrous tree (Wunderbaum), so beautiful and so huge that he stood long looking at it in utter amazement. He wanted to climb that tree, and it was not hard, for its branches rose upward like the steps of a stair. The boy took off his shoes and climbed the tree; a whole nine days he climbed, and reached a broad field: there stood many halls of pure copper, and behind the halls a great forest of copper trees, and on the highest tree sat a copper cock, and beneath the tree murmured a spring flowing with copper — and that was the only sound to be heard; except for the spring all seemed dead, nothing moved or stirred, and no one was to be seen. Having looked his fill, the boy broke himself a branch from one tree, and since his feet were tired he thought to refresh them in the water. He put his feet in the spring, and when he drew them out they seemed covered with shining copper. The boy hurried back to the giant tree, which still rose high into the very clouds, its top impossible to make out. "Up there it must be better still!" he thought, and climbed higher; nine days the shepherd climbed without rest, and before him lay a broad field: on that field stood halls of pure silver, and behind them a forest of silver trees; on the highest tree sat a silver cock, and down at the root murmured a spring flowing with silver. The boy broke himself a branch and thought to wash his hands in the spring; he dipped them in the water — and they at once became silver. Again he returned to the giant tree, whose top was lost high in the heavens. "Up there it must be better still!" the shepherd thought, and climbed higher; after nine days he found himself at the very top, and before him opened a broad field: on that field stood halls of pure gold, and behind them a forest of golden trees; on the highest tree sat a golden cock, and down at the root murmured a spring flowing with gold. The boy broke himself a branch, took off his hat, and bent to look into the spring; his hair fell into the flowing gold and itself became golden. Having climbed down the giant tree, the shepherd hires himself to a king as a kitchen-lad and bargains for permission never to take off his hat, boots, and gloves, on the pretext that he has a bad scab. Then he performs a hard feat — he climbs the glass mountain on which a beautiful princess sat; under his copper feet the glass softened like wax, and he did not slip or stumble; the reward for this was the princess's hand.

Another German tale mentions three forests: in one, all the trees were copper, and in the midst stood a copper castle belonging to a copper dragon. A shepherd drives his goats into that forest, kills the dragon with his self-hewing sword, and carries off a copper bridle from the castle. In the evening he drove the flock home, and his goats gave incomparably more milk than ever before. Next day the shepherd drives his herd into another forest, where all the trees were of pure shining silver, kills the silver dragon, and takes a silver bridle. In the evening the goats gave three times the milk of the day before. On the third day the shepherd goes with his flock into the golden forest, kills the golden dragon, and seizes a golden bridle; this time the goats brought nine times the milk of the previous evening. Then he went to a dark rock — a cloud — from which a living spring beats; he wet his head in that spring, and his hair shone like pure gold. The three bridles taken had this wondrous property: if one shook them, at that very minute countless troops appeared in copper, silver, and golden armour — the spirits of the storm; with the help of these troops the fine fellow defeats his enemies and marries the beautiful princess. Norwegian tales speak of forests copper, silver, and golden owned by trolls (dragons, serpents); Slavic tales speak of them too, ascribing to these wondrous trees the gift of foretelling the future.

Shining with golden lightning, refracting in themselves the rays of sun and moon and coloured with brilliant hues, the tree-clouds gave birth to tales of metallic forests and of the golden fruits (apples) ripening in them; ancient man likened the radiance of the sun, the dawn, the night luminaries, and lightning to the gleam of copper, silver, and gold, and applied the same metaphorical expressions to the spring clouds lit now by storm flame, now by sunlight or moonlight. The epithets copper, silver, and golden accompany all the mythic images in which fantasy depicted the spring clouds: whether it calls them heavenly springs, mountains, palaces, or kingdoms, it always hastens to add the marks named. On the tops of the metallic trees sit copper, silver, and golden cocks — birds personifying lightning; according to the tales of the Edda, in Valhalla sings the mythic cock (Salgofnir, in the received form Gullinkambi). The May tree (maje), with which spring is met in Slavonia, is called kokotji — a cock-tree.

The tale-forests, by tradition's testimony, are in the power of dragons, the demonic representatives of the clouds; the hero who beats these monsters with his self-hewing sword — the lightning — resembles our mighty bogatyrs, whose arms are gold to the elbow and legs silver to the knee, and is no other than the thunder-god. In agreement with the most ancient picture of Perun as shepherd of the heavenly flocks, he appears in the tale as a shepherd; and the more his goats — the cloud-flocks — pasture in the copper, silver, and golden forests, the more milk — the more rain — they give.

The rain springs beating out of the giant tree-cloud flow with copper, silver, and gold, and turn everything that touches them to their own shining colours. Such pictures, arising on the soil of metaphorical language from the foundation just indicated, were fixed in the convictions of infant tribes by that closeness into which the old view set the concepts of light and water. As elements unusually mobile and everywhere swiftly penetrating, light and water produced one common impression of headlong fluidity. In Sanskrit vari means water; our var has the meaning both of hot water and of heat in general — so Afanasy the Traveller says in his journey: "In Hindustan there is no strong var (heat)." From the same root came vreti (to boil), vre-mia — time — that is, first the hot part of the year and then the whole yearly period, just as the word leto (summer) received the wider sense in the expression "so many let" (so many years). From Sanskrit tap were formed our topit' — to heat, to melt — applied alike to water and to fire (to heat a stove, melted butter, to drown a man, a flood — the water of snows melted by the rays of the spring sun, an inundation), and Latin tepeo, tempus (time). In Russian such expressions are in use as "the flame poured itself out," "the moon (or the sun) pours its light"; compare the German turns of phrase: "der Mond über den Berg sein Licht ausgoss," "die Sonne ergiesst ihre Strahlen." In dialect, for the morning dawn they say "the little sun sobs" — that is, the rising sun sprinkles its rays; the same verb is applied to brightly flaring flame: "the birchbark just sobbed out!" Above, testimonies were given that the heavenly luminaries were likened to vessels filled with shining liquid and to lamps in which oil burns. For the moon these metaphors exist: "I went past, I saw a wonder: a kettle hangs, of ninety buckets"; "above the yard, the yard, stands a bowl of milk." The pale light of the moon is likened to milk, to which the epic expressions "white light," "white moon" involuntarily led fantasy. By Bulgarian belief, witches milk the moon and from the milk obtained prepare a healing butter. By force of the same metaphor the matte band of the Milky Way got its name (Polish droga mleczna, German Milchstrasse, Latin via lactea). The folk think that the Milky Way is milk not drained by one child and spilt across the sky — a tradition belonging to distant antiquity, for it is met already among the Greeks: by their tales, Hera tore from her breast the suckling Hermes or Heracles and flung away the child she hated; drops of milk spurted from her breast and formed the Milky Way. Hence it is seen that poetic fantasy used the name given to the Milky Way and bound it with another metaphor, by which the rain sent by the clouds was called milk: in the misty patches of the heavenly vault it recognized the traces of milk-rain spilt by the thunder-god. In following chapters we shall show that rain clouds were pictured as women's breasts, and lightning as a beautiful and strong babe sucking milk from them.

The Greeks had a myth of the golden sun-cup in which Helios crosses the airy ocean, which agrees with the picture of the sun god floating in a boat met on Egyptian monuments. Roman writers call the sun fontem coelestis or aetherei luminis — a fountain of heavenly light; this idea joined itself chiefly to the morning dawn. The Greeks placed the sun's springs at the eastern edge of the universe, and so looked on the morning dawn as the source of daylight, with which she sprinkles from the east. But since with the dawn was joined the thought of the bright goddess of spring, and with the return of spring days the cloudy wells were washed open, it is natural that the picture of the sun's springs should be applied also to the living water of rain showers. Besides, the concepts of brilliance, purity, and transparency are in fact inseparable from water, which is often indeed compared with crystal; lit by the rays of sun and moon it truly strikes the eye with its golden and silver play of colour. The expressions "bright stream," "silvery river" belong among the commonest; in a folk spell we read: "Carry away, mother swift river, with your swiftness — with your golden stream"; in the Tale of Igor's Host: "already the Sula does not flow with silvery streams"; in the Judgment of Liubusha the water is called silver-foamed. The epithets swift (Serbian bistar — bright) and blue are given equally to both elements — to water (the blue Don, the blue sea) and to fire (blue flame; in the Tale of Igor's Host, blue lightnings); Czech pramen means a stream and a ray; mare Balticum is kin to Lithuanian baltas and means the White Sea; in the preceding chapter we indicated the link of the word white with the names of rivers, water nymphs, and elves — just as the adjective rusyi (light, fair) is kin to the words ruslo (riverbed) and rusalka.

The folk epos speaks fairly often of springs flowing with silver and gold, or replaces them with seething kettles into which one need only dip — for hair, hands, and feet to receive a silver or golden gleam. One of the wedding songs recorded by me in Moscow tells of a seething well whose foam is pure silver and gold:

> Boil, boil, well! > Boil, boil, icy-cold! > With spring water, > With silver foam. > There Annushka came out, > Bright Ivanovna came out; > She skimmed the foam, > She poured it out > On Nicholas's icon, > And again she poured out > A golden little ring, > And again she poured out > Two golden crowns.

In the Grimms' collection of tales, mention is made of a Goldbrunnen — a spring pure and bright as crystal; when the king's son wet his head with the water of this well, his hair became golden and shone like the sun ("und glänzte wie eine Sonne"). Russian tales speak of the two springs of Chudo-Yudo (the Sea Tsar or the serpent): if you drink the water of one spring, you become a mighty bogatyr; if you wet your head in the water of the other, your hair will be golden. They speak also of two wondrous ointments belonging to the same Chudo-Yudo: rub your head with one ointment, your hair will be silver; rub with the other, it will be golden. In a Serbian tale a boy hires himself to an old vila (a mountain fairy) and finds a pot at her place: he dipped his hand in — his hand became golden; he ducked his head — his head was gilded. In a Norwegian tale, a widow's son in service with a troll enters the forbidden chambers: in one room stood a big copper kettle seething hard, though there was no fire beneath it; the youth dipped his finger in the kettle, and the finger was gilded; in another room he bathed himself in a kettle and became terribly strong, and as rosy and white as blood with milk. By another Norwegian tale's testimony, a king's son, hired to a giant to herd goats, dips his glove in three seething kettles, and it becomes first copper, then silver, and at last golden.

All these details point to the spring rain clouds, which in the metaphorical language of the most ancient epoch were called heavenly springs and vessels (barrels, kettles, mugs) filled with living water; therefore these springs and vessels are in the power of cloud spirits and nymphs (giants, dragons, vilas, the Sea Tsar), and their waters not only gild but beyond that endow with indescribable beauty and bogatyr strength. Chained by winter cold, the clouds freeze, become lifeless, and give no rains; but with the coming of spring the storm flame melts them, and they pour upon the earth in noisy streams. Just so earthly springs, under the influence of spring warmth, cast off their ice, become living, swift-flowing waters, and by their murmuring, splashing, and foam recall hot boiling water. That is why the wells and kettles of the spring storm clouds are called "seething." The tale heroes who bathe in them represent now the golden-curled sun, now the thunder-god, victor over the demons of darkness and cold; in the sad time of winter they are clad in misty clothing and hide their beautiful forms, their golden gleam, from mortal eyes, but with spring, bathed in the streams of rain, they stand forth again in all their shining beauty and enter into marriage union with the goddess Earth. Golden hair, hands, legs, and fingers are old metaphors for the sun's rays and for lightning.



The singing tree, and the Slavic world-tree

Russian folk tales, among the other feats performed by the fine fellow Perun at the return of spring, make him fetch living water (the rain) and the singing tree — that is, the tree-cloud, whose thunder-voiced song resounds in the wild howling of the storm (compare the traditions of magic musical instruments). To master the cloud-trees, the hero of the tale needs the help of the wild winds on whose wings the walking clouds are brought and carried away; or, in the metaphorical language of ancient poetry, he must whistle on a magic pipe — and the wondrous garden, obedient to its sound (that is, to the blowing of the whistling whirlwinds), will follow him to the end of the world. To these moving trees corresponds the myth of the wandering cloud-mountains, of which fairly clear traditions have been preserved in the family of Indo-European peoples (see ch. XVIII).

Nor have the Slavs forgotten that giant world-tree which plays such an important role in the mythologies of Zend and Scandinavian. A Serbian song places it in paradise and gives it golden branches with silver leaves:

> There grew a tree amid paradise, > A noble laurel, > Nobly it bore fruit, > Let down golden branches, > Its leaves are silver; > Beneath it a holy bed, > Strewn with every flower, > Most of all with basil > And with red roses; > On that bed rests a saint, > Saint father Nicholas.

Ilya the Thunderer comes to him: "Rise," he says, "let us go into the forest, build ships, and ferry souls from that world to this." Prophet Ilya and St. Nicholas appear here in place of Perun and the ancient god of the seas. Of this tree a Little Russian Christmas carol also remembers:

> At the master's uncle's, > There in his yard, > There stood a tree, > Slender and tall, > Wide with leaves, > And curly; > And on that tree > Golden bark, > Pearly dew. > Whence came paradise birds, > Pecked away the golden bark, > The pearly dew.

Then it is told that the master's daughter — the mistress congratulated on the feast — came out, gathered that golden bark and pearly dew, carried them to a master craftsman, and begs: "Forge me a wedding ring for my marriage." One cannot fail to notice the full agreement of the song given with the traditions of other peoples: the paradise birds breaking off the golden bark and shaking dew from the tree are identical with the birds of the "painless tree," to which are ascribed the breaking of branches and the shaking from them of the fruitful seed that falls to earth together with rain-showers. As from the ash Yggdrasil, so from our tree falls dew, given the epithet "pearly" on the ground of the ancient likening of morning dew-drops to precious stones and pearls.

In the apocryphal Conversation of Panagiot with the Frank Azimit (from a sixteenth-century manuscript) the world-tree is described thus: "And in the midst of paradise the animal tree, which is divinity, and the top of that tree approaches the heavens. The tree, golden-like in fiery beauty, covers all paradise with its branches; it has leaves from all trees, and fruits likewise; from it comes a sweet fragrance, and from its root flow, with milk and honey, twelve springs." Common folk are still convinced that somewhere far away, in the east, there is a land of eternal summer planted with gardens of golden and silver trees and ringing with the songs of paradise birds, in which rivers flow with milk and honey, silver and gold.



The oak of Perun

The tradition of the world-tree the Slavs refer above all to the oak. In their memory is preserved a tale of oaks that existed before the creation of the world. In a carol of the Carpathian Russians it is sung that in the time when there was neither earth nor sky, but only the blue sea — the airy ocean — in the midst of this sea stood two oaks, and on the oaks sat two doves: the doves went down to the bottom of the sea, brought up sand and a stone, from which were created the earth, the sky, and the heavenly luminaries. In one of the apocryphal tales of the creation of the universe mention is made of the iron oak, "which is first planted," on which rest the water (the airy sea, the sky), fire (hell), and earth, and whose root stands on the power of God. By the testimony of spells: on the sea, on the ocean, on the island of Buyan stands the mokretsky oak, and beneath it lies the serpent Garafena (Gorynych?): the epithet "mokretsky" points to its link with rain springs. Another epithet given to this tree names it holy. Curious is the following war-spell of a fighting man going to war: "On the holy ocean-sea stands a raw, gnarled oak, and an old master man hews that raw oak with his steel axe; and as chips fly from that raw oak, so may every enemy fighter-fellow fall from me (name) to the raw earth every day, every hour" — that is: as from the blows of Perun's axe (the lightning) the clouds vanish, so may enemy warriors fall from my blows.

A Russian folk tale known to other Slavs tells of an oak that grew to the very sky; an old man climbed that tree — climbed and climbed and got up to the sky, where sat a golden-combed cock, a bird that neither burns in fire nor drowns in water, and there stood wondrous millstones — the emblem of the spring storm, which grants fertility to earth and their daily bread to men. By a Khorugan (Slovenian) tale, the vilas — cloud maidens, kin to the Norns — dwell in a hollow and feed on sugary foods, that is, on sweet amrita; a Polish tale mentions an oak with golden leaves and acorns. Joining together these scattered features preserved in various monuments, we are convinced of the complete agreement of our old oak with the Scandinavian ash: upon it rest the three great worlds — heaven, earth, and hell; on its branches nest lightning birds, and at its root lies a terrible serpent; finally, at this tree flow living springs and dwell prophetic maidens.

In Kaluga province, near Meshchovsk, stand two dry oaks under which, by local accounts, witches gather for their noisy games. In Germany they think that witches, gathering oak leaves into a shirt and hanging this bundle on a tree, can call up winds, disperse clouds, and restore clear weather; and to produce a storm, they boil oak leaves in hot water. By Russian belief, witches stick a knife into a tree and thereby make milk flow from it — that is, driving the sharp lightning into the tree-cloud, the witches pour out the heavenly milk of rain. The folk tale epos knows an oak under which gatherings of spirits take place; from its branches falls healing dew — a detail recalling Yggdrasil; a paradise bird flew in, sat on the oak, and told: whoever washes in this dew will receive health; his bloody wounds will heal and his blind eyes will see. Instead of the paradise bird, in the Khorugan tale they point to the healing dew of the vila, and in the Czech to the witch.

Not the Slavs only — other peoples also join mythic pictures of the tree-cloud with the oak. Wise Väinämöinen, the Kalevala tells, planted an acorn; the oak took root and began to grow not by days but by hours; it stretched so high and spread its branches over such immense spaces that no cloud had passage, and no rays of sun and moon had access to the earth. Väinämöinen began to consider how to fell the giant tree, but nowhere was such a strong man to be found who could take up this hard task. Then Väinämöinen prayed to his mother that she send him the help of the mighty power of water — and out of the sea came a tiny little man, a thumbkin, from head to foot clad in heavy copper, with a tiny axe in his hands; he felled the tree, the sun lit the earth, and everything on it came to life, flowered, and moved — that is, the storm dwarf, dwelling in the rain sea, broke the cloud with his lightning-axe, freed the sun from behind the dark cloud covers, and watered the earth with the life-giving moisture of rain. Under the influence of the metaphor that likened the sun's radiance to the gleam of noble metals, the tale arose that behind the tree-cloud darkening the day sky are hidden countless treasures; so in a Russian tale an oak is mentioned which, if you tear it up by the root, beneath it you will find rich hoards of silver and gold.

To the episode of the Finnish poem corresponds a Norwegian tale: right before the windows of the king's castle grew an oak, so huge that it let not a single sunbeam into the rooms; however much they tried to fell it, it was all in vain: with every blow struck on the oak, it became thicker and stronger; at last a self-felling axe was found (Thor's tool), which laid the giant tree low. It is remarkable that this tradition of the oak the tale joins with another — of a hard rock in which a well had to be dug to water a whole kingdom thirsting for lack of water; this hard task is performed by a stone self-digging pick (another picture of lightning, which digs the cloud mountains and rocks).

During the long migrations of the Aryan tribes, some of the common names that served to denote any tree became particular, began to be assigned to one known genus or kind only, and, conversely, some particular names were generalized. At first the word dub (oak) enclosed the general concept of a tree, which is still heard in the derivatives dubina, dubinka, dubets — a stick; just so Greek drys (oak) corresponds to Sanskrit dru (tree), Slavic drevo, Cymric deru, Armoric dero. Later these names (oak, drys) merged with the concept of the strongest and longest-lived tree, which was consecrated to the thunder-god. Among the Serbs the oak is called grm (an oakwood is a grmik), which without doubt points to its closest relation to Perun and the heavenly thunders. Old charters, defining by this sacred tree the boundaries of clan lands, called it "Perun's (oak)." In agreement with the demonic picture of the clouds, with which righteous Perun constantly battles, the Serbs give the oak the epithet "of the enemy" (vrazhy, from vrag — the enemy, the devil): "if every day were Christmas, there would be no enemy's oak" — that is, if every day were Bozic (Christmas), all the oaks would be cut down for Christmas logs.

Ancient man obtained fire from wood, and with wooden branches and chunks maintained its sacred flame on the hearth; how else could he explain to himself the appearance of storm flame than by this means familiar to him? It is plain that in the cloud, which gives birth to lightning and is devoured by it, he had to see a heavenly tree, and in the rain its juice driven out with the help of fire. By force of this view, rain received the metaphorical name of resin or tar. With his thunder club Perun bores the tree-cloud, and kindling the storm flame sends rains, and with them abundance of the fruits of the earth and every sufficiency. On earth, the representative of this heavenly flame and its life-giving properties was the so-called living fire, obtained by friction from oak wood. By the chronicle's words, the Lithuanians took care that before the idol of Perkun an unquenchable fire of oak wood should burn; among them is preserved a belief that grain seeds were sent down to earth in the husks of acorns — which directly equates the oak with the painless tree of Zend mythology, from which seeds were carried over the whole earth. By Slavic traditions, seeds are brought by the spring winds from the evergreen gardens of paradise; and the Germans, looking at the opened acorn, conclude by its signs whether there will be a harvest, or hunger and diseases.

At the Dodona oracle, highly honoured in all Greece, grew a shady oak consecrated to Zeus; believers came to hearken to the noise of its leaves stirred by the wind, and in these mysterious sounds they heard the prophetic words of the god himself; on the oak's branches sat sacred doves, and at its root beat an inspiring spring known by the name of Zeus's source. The Romans consecrated the oak to Jupiter, for which reason the acorn among them was called the fruit of this god — juglans (Jovis glans, Jupiter's acorn). The fable of the golden fleece — the spring rain-bearing cloud — tells that it hung on an oak and was guarded by a terrible dragon. The Celts regarded the oak with religious reverence; the prophetic Druids chose oak groves as places of their constant sojourn and performed no sacred rite without an oak branch. In lands settled by Germanic tribes there were oaks consecrated to Donar; one such oak grew in Westphalia near Thor's mountain (Thuneresberg), and another, named in an old chronicle robur Jovis, in Hesse, not far from Wuotansberg. Traditions preserved in Lithuania and the Prussian chronicles assure that the forbidden oaks of Perkun, under whose shade his idols also were set up, were evergreen in summer and winter alike; especial honour among the Lithuanians was enjoyed by old, age-old oaks; they surrounded them with fences, and in the epoch of conversion to Christianity the people sooner agreed to the destruction of idols than to the felling of these trees. Sacred oaks with altars to Perkun beside them were at Romove, Kreve, Vilna, and other places.

Of the Slavs we have the following reports. Sefriz mentions a large branching oak that was revered as the dwelling of a deity. In Helmold it is said: "On the way (from Stargard to Lübeck) we turned into a grove, the only one in that land, for the whole of it is a bare plain. There, among old trees, we saw oaks dedicated to the god of that country, Prove (Perun). They were surrounded by a yard and a wooden, carefully finished fence with two gates. …This place was a sanctuary for the whole land; it had its priest, its feasts, and various rites at sacrifices. Here, after the feast, the people (the veche) gathered with priest and prince for judgment. Entrance into the yard was forbidden to everyone except the priest and those who wished to bring sacrifices, or who, threatened by danger of death, sought refuge." As soon as the Christian enlighteners approached the grove, they at once broke the gates, of the broken logs built fires around the sacred trees, and gave them to burning. Following the example of the gods, who gathered to decide the fates of mankind beneath the world-tree, the Slavs rendered judgment and justice beneath old oaks, and deeply believed that all verdicts pronounced under their shade were uttered at the deity's prompting. By Mr. Sreznevsky's indication, "in Slovak tales there occur cases like that of which we read in the Song of the Kraljevo Manuscript, 'of the great defeat,' how they gather to judge and counsel on the most important affairs in the depth of the forest — in such a thicket as a sunbeam could not reach." Among the Germans, likewise, sacred forests served as places of popular assemblies and judicial proceedings, and gave refuge to criminals.

Constantine Porphyrogenitus testifies that the Rus', coming to the island of St. George, performed sacrifices beneath a great oak. The Spiritual Regulation (1721), among superstitious rites, points to the following: "also in another place priests with the people sing molebens before an oak, and the priest distributes that oak's branches to the people for a blessing." In Ukraine, in the so-called Green (Trinity) week, they prepare a "gaming oak": they set up on the pasture or square a long pole with a wheel fastened at the top, all wound with grasses, flowers, and ribbons; around it they mark a small ditch and set up cut birches. Between Kiev and Pereyaslav this pole is called the "dry oak." Around it games are performed and a ritual song is sung:

> Unfold yourself, dry oak! > For the frost will be on you. > I do not fear the frost; > Spring will come — I shall unfold!

The rite consists in calling up spring, whose life-giving power brings rain clouds and dresses the forests in green and flowers. The oak here is the symbol of Perun's tree-cloud: winter, which steals the rains, dries up its blessed juices, and it too is numbed by the cold, like earthly trees in the period of the winter months; with spring it comes to life again and begins to bloom with lightnings (Perun's flower — see ch. XVIII). The wheel points to that hub in which the thunder-god turns his club to kindle the living flame of the storm. [A rite similar in meaning, though differently dressed, is known among the Khorugians under the name of the linden festival. The Lithuanians had a sacred linden (not far from Rostenburg), to which the sick were brought in hope of healing.]

Under the influence of the mythic pictures indicated by us, the oak — and likewise any other tree struck by lightning — received, in the plain folk's opinion, the same healing, enlivening properties as are ascribed to spring rain and to the thunderbolt. To have good horses ("in body"), they advise putting in the stable a piece of wood broken by thunder. If at the first spring thunder you lean your back against a tree (or a wooden wall), the back will not ache. In Tula province peasants try to find in the forest preserves old oaks at which springs flow out; they strip bark from their branches, soak it in the spring, and then wear it in little amulet-bags as a protection against toothache. The golden, indestructible teeth of the thunder-god made men ascribe to him, and to all his attributes, the saving power to still toothache. Therefore folk medicine proposed biting with the sick tooth an oak tree or a stone (the symbol of the lightning hammer). Now this superstitious remedy is met mostly in a setting renewed by Christianity: instead of Perun's sacred stones they bite the stones of the church porch; and healing trees for toothache are sought behind monastery walls. The folk near Moscow recognize as such the trees growing in the yard of the Khutyn monastery; near the Paleostrov monastery, in St. Cornelius's cave, is seen the stump of a tree which grew in a cleft of the rock and was destroyed by the teeth of pilgrims (they gnawed, and still gnaw, it for toothache). At the Dmitriev monastery the tooth-sick gnaw the staff of the elder Peresvet, companion of Dmitri Donskoy; and in various other places, the surviving wooden tombs of God's saints.

In Germany, children suffering from a fracture are carried to an oak which, dividing below into two limbs, above grows together again into one trunk and so forms a cleft. Through this opening the patient is drawn, and they believe that as the divided tree grew together, so the child's broken bone will grow together. Sick sheep are made to crawl through a split young oak, and sick children through a specially split cherry tree, which is then bound so that it may grow together again. Among us similar rites are observed. So in Pronsk district, at the end of the last century, there existed a thick old oak with a through cleft, greatly respected among the folk; through its cleft they drew, some three times, children sick with hernia, and afterward bound the tree with a sash or belt. In Voronezh and Saratov provinces to this day they carry sick children into the forest, split a young green oak in two, draw the child between its clefts three times, and then bind the tree with a thread. By Sakharov's words, children suffering from consumption are laid for a stated time in a split tree, then carried around the tree thrice nine times, and children's shirts are hung on its branches. On returning home they bathe them in water taken from nine rivers or wells and sprinkle them with ashes from seven stoves.

From fever and other diseases peasants bathe in rivers, forest springs, and wells, and after bathing wipe themselves with a clean rag and hang it on a neighbouring tree or willow bush; instead of the rag they hang a shirt or a scrap of clothing, and leave them hanging until they quite rot away. The sense of the rite is this: washing and wiping the sickness from his body, the patient as it were takes it off himself and, together with the rag and the cast-off shirt, hands it over to the bush or tree — the earthly representatives of that heavenly, paradisal tree which exudes the living water that heals all hurts and illnesses. As the abandoned scrap or shirt rots away, so must the sickness itself perish. Later, when the clear understanding of the old pictures was lost, this rite received the character of a sacrificial offering to forest and water spirits.



The aspen

No less curious beliefs does the folk join with the aspen — a tree to which mythic properties are assigned hardly otherwise than in consequence of the kinship of its name (Serbian jasika) with the word for ash (Serbian jasen, Polish jesion, Lithuanian osis, Anglo-Saxon æsc, Scandinavian askr, Old High German asc, from Sanskrit as — to throw, to shine, in Pictet's opinion). As to the ash is given power to numb serpents, so of the aspen they affirm that a killed snake must be hung precisely on this tree; otherwise it will come to life and bite. When the bogatyr Dobrynya killed the serpent, he hung him on a gnarled aspen: "Dry yourself, Serpent Gorynych's son, on that gnarled aspen!" The aspen has a like saving action against sorcerers, vampires, and witches, who in folk beliefs play a role almost identical with the serpent's: as he sucks out the milk-rain of the heavenly cloud-cows, so they steal rains and dews, milk and suck dry those same cows, and, waking in spring from winter death, love to drink their fill of warm blood (another metaphor of rain). Forgetting the link of the old metaphorical expressions with the heavenly phenomena of nature — reading in them not the original sense they really enclosed, but, on the contrary, understanding them literally — the folk ascribed to sorcerers, vampires, and witches the milking and spoiling of ordinary cows, the coming out of graves after death, and the sucking of blood from living people. To defend themselves and their herds from these imagined woes, they resort to the help of the aspen.

The mythic tree-cloud, from beneath whose roots streams the living water of rain, blooms with golden or fiery flower — lightning; on its top sits the lightning-bearing bird, the eagle or the golden-combed cock. Demonic serpents, sorcerers, and witches, greedily flinging themselves on their beloved drink of living water, at the same time undergo the blows of the thunder-arrows, which strike the predators and make them flee in fear and trembling. The sharpened aspen stake received in the folk's eyes the significance of Perun's club (Donnerkeil). So that a dead man in whom an evil sorcerer, vampire, or witch is suspected cannot come out of the grave, peasants drive an aspen stake into him; to guard cows and calves from witches' attacks, they set aspen trees, felled or torn up by the root, at the gates and corners of the cattle yard; in time of cattle plague, driving out Cow Death, they beat her (that is, wave in the air) with aspen logs. By the tales' testimony, sorcerers risen from their graves have an aspen stake driven into the heart, are beaten with an aspen log on the backswing, and their corpses are burned on an aspen pyre.

In the Christian era, folk fantasy — transferring onto the traitor Judas the most ancient pictures of the cloud-demon (the giant or the serpent) — created the legend according to which Judas hanged himself on an aspen, and since that time its leaf trembles forever. [Others assure that he hanged himself on an elder, and for that reason it is unfit for building and is counted the devil's dwelling.] In her turn the witch can use an aspen stake or branch for her magic charms: striking a sleeping man in the breast with this branch, she deals him an unseen wound and greedily drinks his blood — just as the spring blows of lightning aimed at the cloud, still embraced by winter sleep, pour rain out of it; or, in metaphor: Perun's branch opens the breast of the cloud demon and draws hot blood from him. Having milked a black cow, the witch pours the milk into the earth and at once drives in an aspen stake; by this charm she takes the milk from cows and dries up their udders — just as the blows of lightning, bringing the milk-rain down to earth, by that very act dry up the black clouds (see ch. XXVI).

As a saving tool against demonic delusion, the aspen can serve also as a healing means for driving out the unclean power of diseases. Those broken by paralysis are made to press their bare feet, lying, against an aspen log. From fever and other illnesses they treat thus: they bore a hole in the wall or door at a distance from the earth equal to the patient's height, cut off his nails and some hair, put both into the prepared opening, and plug it with an aspen peg; sometimes they bore the hole in an aspen tree and stop it with the patient's nails and hair. They also read a spell over aspen twigs, which are then laid on the patient. When the teeth ache, they take an aspen twig and three times read over it a spell: "On the sea, on the ocean, on the island of Buyan stand three high trees; beneath those trees lies a hare; remove yourself, toothache, to that hare!" After that the aspen twig is applied to the sick teeth. The handing over of the disease to the hare, of which the spell speaks, encloses the same sense as the request addressed to the mouse, to give its iron tooth in exchange for a spoiled one.



The golden apples

In the paradisal gardens and groves, on the shady trees of the spring clouds, ripen golden fruits (apples), giving eternal youth, health, and beauty. By their wondrous properties these fruits are entirely identical with the immortal drink — the living water. The folk tale epos commonly sets both pictures side by side and joins with them an equal life and healing power. The shining lightnings, as was shown above, were likened by the Aryan tribes to golden balls and eggs; applied to the cloud gardens, the golden ball-lightnings gave birth to the myth of the golden fruits ripening in the happy season of spring and summer on the tree-clouds. A Serbian song depicts the storm in the poetic picture of Thunder's game with Lightning:

> Lightning outplayed Thunder > With two or three apples > And four oranges.

In another Serbian song a beauty — a cloud maiden — tosses up three golden apples, and by that very act calls down from the sky three lightnings. But since lightnings, flashing in the clouds, bring down to earth the living water of rains — or, in the language of the old metaphors, since the living water is the juice pressed out by the golden fruits — by force of this poetic expression the golden apples received the significance of amrita. The tradition of the golden apples is as common to all Indo-European peoples as the tradition of the living water.

Greek mythology knows the famous garden of the Hesperid nymphs, in which golden apples grow; by King Eurystheus's command Hercules had to go there and bring the rare fruits; on the way to the Hesperides' garden Hercules meets the giant Antaeus, struggles with him, and after various difficulties fulfills the task. If we recall that the black clouds in the ancient metaphorical language were called night, it will be clear why the golden apples are in the possession of the Hesperides — maidens of the evening gloom — beyond the much-sounding ocean, that is, the sky; there a hundred-headed dragon guards them, whose eyes never close in sleep — the demonic serpent, the well-known stealer and hoarder of the living water. Hercules, god of the spring storm, struggles with the demons of the dark clouds and, conquering them, obtains the golden fruits — that is, pours out the life-giving rain.

Scandinavian mythology tells of the golden apples kept in Asgard (the dwelling of the heavenly rulers) by the goddess Idunn, wife of Bragi; feeding on these fruits the gods obtain immortality and remain forever young, just as the gods of Greece remained immortal and forever young feeding on ambrosia and nectar. The evil giants, with the help of crafty Loki, once stole Idunn together with her wondrous apples: Loki lured the goddess out of Asgard's walls, and the giant Thjassi, in eagle-shape, from whose wing-beats wild winds arose, seized her and carried her to his dwelling. When Idunn disappeared, the Æsir soon grew old; strong grief took hold of them, and they began to threaten Loki with death if he did not repair his error. Loki borrowed Freya's falcon dress, flew to the land of the giants, and found Idunn there; she turned into a nut, Loki became a falcon, seized the nut, and directed his swift flight to Asgard. The eagle Thjassi began to pursue him, but the gods built a fire, singed his wings, and when the giant fell, they killed him at once. At the base of this tale lies the thought of the theft of amrita by the demons of the winter clouds — at which nature and all her creative powers visibly decay — and then of the happy return of the stolen treasure in spring, when the evil demons perish in the storm flame and under the blows of Thor's club.

The same pictures and the same images we meet in the tale epos. In the Russian tale identical with the German "The Golden Bird," golden apples are mentioned which ripen by night and are stolen by the fire-bird: a poetic picture of the storm, whose stormy breathing tears the fruits from the tree-cloud — or, what is the same, scatters golden lightnings and pours out the living water of rain. In the Khorugan redaction of this tale, instead of golden apples the talk is of the theft of a famous grapevine which every hour gave a bucket of wine; wine, as we know, is one of the names of the immortal drink. In German tales the tree of life (Baum des Lebens) is presented with golden apples; it grows at the end of the world in a wondrous garden beyond the sea; the ravens, Odin's messengers, which by our beliefs bring living and dead water, obtain golden apples for tale heroes too. In other tales the falcon takes the ravens' place: he steals the golden fruits, unweariedly guarded by a witch on a high glass mountain (the sky) or in an underground kingdom (in the dark caverns of the cloud mountains); let us recall that the thunder-god in falcon shape steals the immortal soma. The peel and juice of the golden apples instantly heal wounds.



The golden apples, concluded

In our common folk they tell: whoever eats apples before the second Spas — the feast on which these fruits are hallowed in church — his dead kinsmen shall not receive in paradise, on Spasov day, the golden apples with which the Mother of God portions them out. In the epistle of Archbishop Vasily of Novgorod, a monument of the fourteenth century, mention is made of the paradise planted by God and of the apples growing in it, which give healing from sicknesses. In connection with these data arose the superstitious custom of treating illnesses with common apples hallowed in church — witnessed by the denunciatory words of the sixteenth century: "the infirm demon, called tryasets (the shaking fever), they pretend to drive out with certain false writings, writing the names of the accursed Hellenic demons on little apples and laying them on the holy table (var.: on the throne) during the liturgy." For the life-giving properties ascribed to the heavenly apples, Russian tradition gives them the name of the rejuvenating, or youth-restoring, apples: one need only taste of these fruits and at once you become young and healthy, despite your advanced years. The curious Russian tale of the rejuvenating apples and the living water, known among other peoples too, conveys one of the most ancient myths. The aged and blinded tsar of whom it speaks personifies the winter season, when all on earth withers and grows decrepit, and the all-seeing sun loses its bright radiance. Depicting the seasons as living, human-like beings, the people's fancy presented Spring as a beautiful youth, and Winter as a white-haired, blind old man. To restore the tsar's youth and sight, the tsarevich-son must obtain the living water, which heals blindness, and the youth-restoring golden apples — that is, must summon spring with its beneficent rains, golden lightnings, radiant sun, and all the luxury of the vegetable kingdom. The living water and the golden apples possess an equal creative power: alike they renew the decrepit old man, make him a blooming youth, and even liken him to a seven-year-old child; to the sick they give strength and health, to the dead life; ugliness they turn into beauty, impotence into bogatyr might; and both the one and the other are found in a far-off land — in the ever-unfading garden — and are guarded by dragons and giants. In one variant of the tale of the rejuvenating apples and the living water, around this garden lies a huge serpent, closed in a ring, its tail and head joined together. This serpent reminds us of the Eddic giant Jörmungandr, in whom the representation of the rain-clouds enveloping the sky was combined with the idea of the all-embracing ocean-sea; thus the detail fully corresponds to the Greek tradition that the golden apples grow beyond the limits of the much-sounding ocean surrounding the earth — that is, in the distant regions of the cloudy sky. By the tales' testimony, the garden with the youth-restoring apples and the springs of living water lies in the divye — the heavenly (see I, 127–8) — kingdom, where reigns the Tsar-Maiden of indescribable beauty and immeasurable strength, who disports herself with her host of brave warrior-maidens in bogatyr games and feats. In some variants the living water stands with her in a little flask under her pillow (and that flask is never emptied, take from it as you will!), or flows straight from her hands and feet. The mighty beauty lives in a golden palace and unites in herself the traits that identify her with the goddess Iðunn and the valkyries. Iðunn, keeper of the golden apples, dwelt in Brunnakr (brunnenfeld) — that is, amid the springs of living water; and by another myth's indication she sat upon the world's ash Yggdrasil and guarded the inspiring mead Odrerir. The Swedes remember a land of eternal youth (land der jugend), where grows a tree with priceless apples, and beside the tree beats a spring whose waters shine like pure gold; whoever tastes these fruits or drinks this water, like sparkling golden mead, becomes young again, though he has lived to gray hairs. The possessor of the heavenly rain-springs, the Tsar-Maiden appears at the head of the cloud nymphs, with all the warlike setting that from of old was given to the goddess of the spring thunderstorms and to Odin's companions, the valkyries; her kinship with these swan-maidens is indicated by one redaction of the Russian tale, which calls the Tsar-Maiden the Swan; besides, it is known of the valkyries that in the abode of the blessed they offered to gods and dead heroes the horn filled with the immortal honey-drink. The journey to the promised land of the Tsar-Maiden is made on a bogatyr steed, on the wings of the falcon-bird, or (as in the Serbian redaction) straightway with the wind's help, and is attended with various difficulties. The tsarevich who obtains the living water and the youth-restoring apples is the thunder-god; coming into the Tsar-Maiden's palace, he finds her sleeping — that is, bound by winter sleep. Gazing on her wondrous beauty, the tsarevich could not restrain himself: taking advantage of the maiden's sleep, he deprives her of her innocence, steals the living water and the youth-restoring apples, and hastens on the return road; his bogatyr steed leaps over the high palace walls, but catches its hoof on the stretched strings, and at once ringing and thunder resound through the whole kingdom; the Tsar-Maiden awakes, pursues the abductor, and enters into marriage with him. In such bold poetic images the myth depicts the spring thunderstorm, when the thunder-god, awakening to life the cloud-maiden benumbed by winter's influence, enters with her into a love-union. In the ancient metaphorical tongue, rain is the fleshly seed; the shedding of this seed — the fabulous beauty's loss of her maidenhood — is in essence the same as the theft of the living water and the rejuvenating apples. Clasping the Tsar-Maiden in his embraces, Perun unites with her by the phallus-lightning and drinks deep of the love-drink — which is the same as depriving her of the living water (see I, 459–460). The ringing and thunder of the strings is the metaphor of thunder (I, 333). — Another group of folk tales tells of three sorrowful events: in a certain kingdom the tree that bore golden apples suddenly became barren, the spring of wine or living water ran dry, and the beautiful tsarevna vanished none knew whither, or fell under a spell, withered of grievous sickness, and sank into unawakable sleep. The whole land clothes itself in mourning and gives itself to despondency, until the tsarevna's deliverer appears; with her return, her awakening from sleep, or her recovery, the tree begins again to bear golden apples, and the spring to give wine or living water. This stolen, enchanted tsarevna is none other than the goddess Iðunn in the power of the giant-demons.

The golden fruits of the heavenly gardens possess the same great fertilizing power as the seed of the spring rain. The Völsunga saga tells of a king: long married, he had no heir, and prayed earnestly for children to Odin and Frigg. The goddess heard his prayer and sent to him a wish-fulfilling maiden (wunschmagd); in the form of a bird (a crow) the maiden flew to the hill where the king sat and dropped an apple onto his knees; the king brought the apple home and gave it to the queen to eat, and soon after she conceived and bore a son. Above (p. 155) we pointed out that tradition joins the same wondrous property to the mythic fish-cloud: the barren queen tasted of the fish and at once became pregnant. This detail the tale-epic varies also thus: that instead of the fish, the childless wife eats an apple. There was a man, says a Serbian tale, who had a wife and had no children; he had a bitch and she had no pups, he had a mare and she had no foals. He abandoned his hut and set out to wander the white world; it happened once that he stopped by a certain well. At that hour there appeared before him a stranger, gave him an apple, and bade him return home: "jabuku oguli, oguljine podaj kuji i kobili, jabuku rozpolovi, te pola podaj ženi da izjede, a pola ti izjedi: što je dosad bilo nerodno, odsad sve će biti plodno" — "peel the apple, give the peelings to the bitch and the mare, halve the apple, give half to your wife to eat and eat half yourself: all that till now was barren shall henceforth be fruitful." In other tales a youth, having caught a fish, releases it into the water for the wondrous gift of doing all according to his wish. Seeing the beautiful tsarevna, he wished that she should conceive a child, and in that very minute the tsarevna conceived and in due time bore a boy with a golden apple in his hand. The tsar grew angry at his daughter, and wishing to learn the child's father, gathered into the palace hall the unmarried men of the whole kingdom: at whom the child throws the golden apple, he is the guilty one of the dishonor. The boy threw the apple at the youth whose wish had been the cause of his supernatural birth. By these data is explained the important significance the apple acquired in the Serbian wedding rite. With them it is the custom that he who seeks a maiden's hand should send her, through the matchmaker, an apple with small silver coins stuck into it; if the maiden accepts the apple, it serves as a sign of her consent; otherwise the bridegroom must seek himself another bride. "Otišli na jabuku" means: they went to arrange about the wedding. Among the Mazurians a branch with an apple is a necessary attribute of wedding merriment. In a modern Greek tale the princess, to point out whom exactly she wishes to have as husband, throws an apple at the chosen lucky one.



The nut

Up and down the trunk of Yggdrasil runs a squirrel, in whose image fancy personified the lightning flashing in the clouds. This ancient representation made men seek and find nut-groves in the cloud gardens growing on the high heaven. As the common squirrel feeds on forest nuts, so it was thought that the squirrel-lightning gnaws the very same fruits growing on the world-tree-cloud. These heavenly nuts, like the apples of the tree of life, shine as pure gold and possess the same power of sending fruitfulness. A Polish tale knows a wondrous squirrel which, sitting on a tree, gnaws nuts — but not common ones: the nuts' shells are golden, and their kernels are pearls (I, 614). By Serbian belief, if thunder peals on Elijah's day, the nuts in that year will grow worm-eaten, spoil, and fall from the branches. At the base of this omen lay the age-old myth that during the thunderstorm Perun breaks off the heavenly nuts and gnaws them with his sharp teeth; among the various likenesses of lightning, it was compared also with the worm (I, 395; see ch. XXI). A Serbian tale mentions two nuts given to a brave youth by prophetic crones: when one nut was cracked, a river flowed out of it, and when the other was cracked, flame burst out of it — that is, the heavenly nuts, broken by Perun, produce rain and lightnings. In accord with this, a Norwegian tale speaks of a great walnut from whose opening pours an inexhaustible water. The tale-hero comes into a kingdom suffering from lack of water (that is, from summer drought); with the help of a self-digging pick (the lightning) he digs in the hard rock (the cloud) a well, lays in it the wondrous nut — and in a moment the well is filled with water.

In Rus' they believe that a twin nut (dvoeshka) brings happiness and wealth, and therefore they keep it and carry it in their purses, that money may come, and on their necks, to escape sicknesses and all misfortunes. Among the Bulgarians nuts are an indispensable belonging of the ritual repast on Christmas Eve; part of these nuts is kept through the whole year, and if someone's throat falls ill, then they take the Christmas nut and rub the sore place with it. In some places in Germany (in the Altmark and Westphalia) they shower the newly-wed with nuts, as with us they shower them with grain and hops — the symbol of intoxicating drinks: beer, braga, and mead; though in Poltava province the showering of the newly-wed with nuts also exists (see ch. XXIX). Into the grain meant for sowing, the Germans throw a handful of nuts (or peas), that the coming harvest may be fruitful. In the Black Forest the messenger sent to invite to the wedding carries a nut branch. With such a branch in hand they leap in the Pfalz over the Ivan's-day bonfires, touch it to the kindled flame, and then stick it into the cow-sheds, for the protection of the household cattle from sorcery and the unclean power. From a nut branch is also made the Wünschelruthe — the emblem of lightning (see ch. XVIII). To express the thought of the liberation of the goddess of living water and of spring fruitfulness from under the giants' power, the old myth paints such a picture: Loki, the god of the thunderstorm flame, in the form of a swift-flying falcon carried off in his beak Iðunn, turned into a nut. The prohibition against cutting the nut-tree was long observed in various localities as strictly as the prohibition against touching with the axe the old century-enduring oaks. Ebo points to a great nut-tree near Stettin; by this tree was a spring, and it was held dedicated to a god.



The worship of forests and trees

The traditions of the heavenly, paradisal gardens in the course of time came to be applied to earthly places and groves, and communicated to them a sacred character. Besides, the beauty and mysteriousness of the huge virgin forests of the most ancient epoch could not but strike the poetically attuned imagination of the infant tribes. In the life of the vegetable kingdom they contemplated the same eternal struggle of divine powers which they observed also in the elemental phenomena of nature. With the beginning of spring the fields, bushes, and oak-woods deck themselves with green and flowers; evil winter strips from them these luxurious attires and casts all into deathlike torpor until the new return of the vernal days. Following the suggestions of the metaphorical tongue and of the primitive views of mother-nature, closely bound with it, ancient man scarcely knew inanimate objects; everywhere he found reason, and feeling, and will. In the noise of the forests, in the rustle of leaves, he heard those mysterious conversations which the trees hold among themselves; in the crack of the broken branch, in the creak of the split tree, he recognized painful groans; in withering, a drying grief — and so forth. The tree shuddered, pined, and even shed blood (the sap), being touched by sharp iron: "Nichit' trava zhaloshchami, a drevo s tugoyu k zemli priklonilos'" — "the grass droops with pity, and the tree bowed to earth with grief" — says the Lay of Igor's Host; with like expressions the folk poetry is filled.

All this led to the worship of forests and trees. Testimonies of such worship have been in part adduced above; but there are other indications of the monuments and of folk tradition. The church statute of St. Vladimir mentions the superstitious who continued to pray "in the grove" (v roshchen'i); in the Life of Prince Konstantin of Murom we read: "duplinam drevyanym vetvi ubrustsem obveshivayushche i sim poklonyayushchesya" — "hanging the branches of hollow trees with kerchiefs and bowing to them" — a custom preserved to this day in Little Russia, where on great oaks they hang towels and hanks of thread, as a gift to the rusalkas. The Gustyn chronicle speaks of the offering of sacrifices "to the groves" (roshcheniyam). Kirill of Turov rejoiced that with the acceptance of Christianity trees would no longer be called god, though his hopes proved premature; for even after that the double-faithful people long preserved a reverent respect for old trees, told of spirits dwelling in the dense forests, performed under their shade their games, sacrificial and other rites. The meeting of spring, the feasts in honor of renewed nature (Semik, Trinity) and of the creative powers of summer (Kupalo) are to this day celebrated in forests and groves. The Semik birch, decked with many-colored rags and ribbons, is borne with triumph into the village, as the very sign of the goddess Spring, returned to earth. To the birch are ascribed healing properties: through this tree (sometimes through a shaft) the sick are doused with cold water; under the threshold of a new stable a birch log is buried, that horses may thrive. Yards, huts, and the very churches are decked on Semik and Trinity with felled young trees, flowers, and herbs. The Kupala fires in Little Russia are commonly kindled in forests. When from an acquaintance-gardener they take a fruit-graft or layer, they count it a duty beside the tree from which the branch was cut to lay in gift a small coin; they think that without this neither graft nor layer will take. The Slavs to this day count certain groves sacred and call them gai-bog and bozhnitsy; in Rus' to the reserved forests, in which cutting is forbidden, is given the name bozhelesye; in Olonets province they give the forest the epithet pravedny (righteous). By Ditmar's testimony, the forest surrounding the temple at Retra was held sacred; the same religious significance was ascribed also to Svitibor: "the grove Holy Bor (Zuitibor) was revered by all the inhabitants as a divinity, and never from time immemorial was its inviolability broken." Helmold says that the Baltic Slavs honored forests and groves as sanctuaries, and prayed by trees and springs; there is preserved also a notice of the groves of the goddess Zhiva (see ch. XXVIII). Yet in the eleventh century the spiritual authorities demanded of the Czechs that they bring no sacrifices to trees (ad arbores sacrificia nullo modo fiant) and seek no help from them (non ad arbores aut ad fontes auxilium quaerere).

By Cosmas of Prague's words, the ignorant people of his time cherished a religious respect for trees and groves, prayed and brought sacrifices to the mountain and forest nymphs (oreades, dryades), and therefore Prince Bretislav (1093) resolved to hew down and commit to fire all the oak-woods revered as sacred. From the songs of the Kralodvorsky manuscript we learn that Chestmir, before setting out on campaign against the enemy, "pode w še drwa w zložie obieti bohom"; and Zaboj, complaining of the oppression of the unbelievers, said: "where our fathers gave food to the gods, whither they went to utter their prayers, there they hewed down all the trees, destroyed the idols of the gods, and drove out of the forests all the ravens." The Czechs to this day continue to pray under trees; whoever does this on Good Friday, before sunrise, shall be free the whole year from all sicknesses; sufferers from fever bend the knee before the elder and thrice pronounce: "Elder! the Lord God has sent me to thee, that thou take my ailment upon thyself." On the Christmas holy days the Czechs, turning to the trees, ask of them foretellings of the future and help in sicknesses. In Saxon Lusatia and in the land of the Slovaks there exist forbidden "holy groves," where, by folk tales, wander the shades of the princes of old (the blessed heroes), pray and bring sacrifices to their pagan gods; there dwell prophetic spirits, and maidens go into the forests "to listen to the spirit," which by the rustle of wind-shaken and falling leaves foretells the future. Among the Serbs certain great, shady trees (oaks, beeches, and others) are revered as inviolable; to them is ascribed great power, so that if anyone hewed down such a tree, he would either soon die or be punished with an incurable sickness. He who fears — has he not hewn a sacred tree? — must on its stump cut off the head of a living hen with that same axe with which the tree was felled: this rite, pointing to an ancient propitiatory sacrifice, saves from the coming calamity. Sacred forests and groves were had by all the Indo-European peoples; the Greeks in their midst built temples and altars, and punished the sacrilegious felling of such trees with death. The Germanic tribes had their heilige Wälder, where from of old were performed feasts and sacrificial rites; even after the acceptance of Christianity the people continued under certain trees to kindle fires and make them the usual offerings.

We have seen that the contracting of marriage unions among the pagan Slavs was performed by water, as the element serving for the emblem of the fertilizing seed of rain: but as the rain-clouds were likened not only to springs but also to forests, so marriages could be performed also by sacred trees. Beauplan in his "Description of Ukraine" relates that the unmarried youths, obtaining brides by abduction, had to carry them off into the forest and hide there whole days together, and only in that case escaped pursuit and wedded the stolen maidens unhindered. The Serbian otmičari carry the maiden off into the forest, and there a priest invited beforehand weds the couple under an oak. Among our schismatics holds to this day the custom by which a young fellow, having agreed with the maiden, sets out with her to the sacred oak, drives around it thrice in a circle, and the marriage union is counted concluded. Stenka Razin, having seized power on the Don, forbade divine service in the Orthodox churches, and those desiring to enter marriage he ordered to be wedded about the willows. The memory of such weddings is preserved also in the folk epic; thus the bylina of the bogatyr Dunai says that he with his mate "were betrothed round the broom-bush, were wedded"; the folk saying hints at the same: "venchali vokrug eli, a cherti peli" — "they wedded round the spruce, and the devils sang." In forests and groves the pagans buried also the dead, because these places reminded them of the blessed, paradisal gardens, where after death the souls of the righteous must dwell. Otto of Bamberg forbade the Pomeranians to do this, and Prince Bretislav the Czechs. In the poetic picture of the Kralodvorsky manuscript the souls of the departed are depicted fluttering about the trees. In folk sayings has survived a living memory of that age-old, prehistoric epoch when the Slavs — savages — lived in the dense forests and worshipped the "groves": "v lesu rodilis', pnyam molilis'" — "in the forest were born, to stumps prayed"; or: "zhili v lesu, molilis' pen'yu" — "lived in the forest, prayed to the stump"; "v lese zhit' — pen'kam Bogu molit'sya" — "to live in the forest is to pray to God to the stumps."

Presenting the clouds as heavenly trees, transferring this representation to earth and binding it with the common oak-woods, ancient man recognized the forests and groves as the dwelling-place of the cloud-spirits, and to these last gave the character of forest genii. As in the image of the vodyanye the idea of the rain-streams is bound with the earthly springs, so in the leshie are clear the traits of their original, elemental significance, combined with the forests and trees.



PART II: THE FOREST SPIRITS (printed pp. 326–349)



The leshie: their names, their winter sleep

This is proved both by the above-explained tradition of the origin of the vodyanye and the leshie (see p. 235) and by many other beliefs living among the people. The word leshy in the provincial dialects and in the old monuments means: of the forest, forest-covered; in various provinces and districts the leshy is called leshak, lesovik, lesnik, lisun (properly lisun), and even les. The leshie (Czech lesní mužové) are the same as the Greek satyrs, the Roman fauns and silvans, the German Waldleute, Holzleute, and Moosleute. They live in the forest thickets and wastes, but commonly with the first frosts (at the beginning of October) they sink through the earth, disappearing for the whole winter, and in spring leap out of the earth again as if nothing had happened. Parting in autumn with the forest, they rage, break trees in vexation as though they were fragile reeds, and drive all beasts into their holes; the whole of that day a terrible wind howls through the forest. In this curious belief is clearly told the identity of the leshie with the creative powers of summer. Yielding to winter's influence, the clouds cease to flash with lightnings, to rumble with thunder, and to pour with rain; this torpor, or winter sleep, fancy joins with all their mythic personifications. As the vodyanoy sleeps the whole winter and only in April wakes up furious and noisy, so the leshie sink through the earth in the cold time of autumn, and in the dark underworlds rest until their spring awakening. One of the most-used metaphors in the old poetry was the representation of clouds and storm-clouds as gloomy mountain caves and the underground kingdom; thither hide the spirits of the summer thunderstorms for the winter; there, as the ancient myths witness (see ch. XVIII), Odin rests with all his furious host, and lies the stolen hammer of Thor (the lightning). We have indicated the grounds for which the spirits of the thunder-clouds from of old received a demonic character; with this character appear in the traditions both those of them that peopled the aerial spaces, the water-springs, and the mountain gorges, and those that dwell in the forests. Here are the testimonies of folk proverbs and sayings: "went to the forest, but met the devil on the way"; "brother is brother, but if not, give back the cross, and off to the devil in the forest" (the usual expression in a quarrel of sworn brothers); "prishov z' lesu i idzi sobi k besu"; "pad chernym lesom spatkausya chort z' besom"; "push the devils, but not in our forest"; "from an empty hollow either the screech-owl, or the owl, or Satan himself." The Poles have a belief that the devil, turning into an owl, loves to sit on the old willow, which is why the common folk fear to cut this tree, lest they fall under the anger of the unclean power. The Polish saying "zakochał się jak diabeł w suchej wierzbie" corresponds to the Great Russian "fell in love like the devil with the dry osier" and the Little Russian "b'yetsya, yak chort kolo sukhoi verby", "zakokhavs', yak chort v sukhu grushu (abo verbu)." Hearing mysterious sounds in the forest, the Poles spit to all four sides, to drive the unclean one from themselves. Both leshie and devils the people call by the same names: shatun, vorog, yols; compare: lyad — the devil, lyadashchy — worthless, and lyada (lyady) — a field with the roots of felled trees, lyadina — a forest, lada and lyadka — a marsh overgrown with bushes and forest. In the Romance tales the ancient Roman god of the underworld Orcus is taken for a forest spirit (Ital. orco, Neap. huorco, Fr. ogre) and is presented as black, shaggy, and for the most part a giant; the German tales lay his role upon the devil. The dark underworlds, the kingdom of the all-darkening clouds, gave birth to the myth of the deep dens of hell, and the above-mentioned tradition — that the leshie sink through the earth — has beside it the following analogous beliefs: in the middle of November all the unclean flee from the earth, fearing the frosts; winter disperses them and they hide in the abyss; but at the beginning of February, with the first beginnings of spring, the unclean spirits fly out of hell in the form of birds. The ancient tale of the thunder-demons disappearing in autumn, in the days of the leaf-fall month, and appearing again in spring, the folk fancy — in accord with the bestowal upon these spirits of the forest type — bound up with the dying and revival of life in the oak-woods and groves, which toward winter cast off their leaves, and in the vernal season dress in green and flowers. Leaving the earth, the leshie raise winds, break trees, and scatter their yellowed leaves: in these common phenomena of stormy autumn the poetic view of the people discerns their vexation, a feeling of discontent and longing for dying nature. Yet the leshie do not all disappear for the winter; in some localities they are confused with the demons of the winter blizzards. The rushing whirlwinds, in the peasants' opinion, are the leshy's doing. Thus trees broken by the tempest in the forests are commonly reckoned among his pranks; by folk belief (Vyatka province) the leshy never walks simply, but before and behind him is always accompanied by a strong wind, and by the wind's direction one may conclude whither exactly he holds his way. No one has seen him leave anywhere the trace of his feet, though he passed over sand or snow: this is because he sweeps his track with the whirlwind, as the witches do (see I, 569). In the month of August the villagers guard by night their sheaves from the leshy's frolic, who scatters them, raising whirlwinds; to hinder him, they go to the threshing floor in inside-out sheepskins and draw about the sheaves a circular line with the poker — that is, they close the threshing floor on all sides as with a fence; the inside-out sheepskin is the emblem of the cloudy garment in which the thunder-god arrays himself, the pursuer of demons, and the poker the emblem of his lightning-bearing club. They assure also that the leshy fears a firebrand; and the Swedes, to drive off the forest spirit, advise throwing at him a piece of iron. Both firebrand and iron are symbols of lightning. Since by whistling one can call up a storm, the peasants not only do not venture to whistle on the water, but do not do it at night in the forest either, fearing to wake the leshy, who, waking, hastens to answer it. When a fearful whistling of the leshy (the wind's howl) seems to the traveler, he hurries to run in the opposite direction.



Giants and dwarfs: the leshy's stature and shapes

The mighty phenomena of the thunderstorm our forefathers personified now in the image of giants, identical with the huge clouds covering the whole horizon, now in the image of dwarfs, identical with the little lightnings dwelling in the cloud mountains. Both representations are given also to the leshie, who are now lower than the grass, now higher than the tallest trees. As the cloud advancing upon the sky from a scarcely noticeable black spot afar quickly grows in its volume and reaches gigantic dimensions, so the leshy can instantly grow tall and diminish. Of bogatyrs and giants the tales express themselves that they grow neither by days nor by hours, but by minutes. Commonly in the forest the leshy is even with the tall oaks and pines, and on the glade — with the grass. The Belorussians affirm that the leshy's growth depends on the height of those trees near which he stands or walks — that is, by the original view the forest-spirit's stature was determined by the dimensions of those fleeting cloud forests in whose midst he marched across the sky. In Kiev and Chernigov provinces they distinguish lisuns and poleviks: the first are presented as giants of grayish and ashen color; of the last they tell that they are even with the height of the grain growing in the field, and after harvest diminish and become as tiny as thorns — that is, their growth at that time does not exceed the cut stalks of straw remaining on the root. Just so the Germans present the forest spirits both as giants (schrat, waldschrat, and Scand. skratti — gigas: this name, on a par with the word troll, combines in itself the meanings of devil, leshy, and giant) and as little ones (schretle); those of them that wander singly take for the most part gigantic forms, and those living in societies (in crowds) are confused with elves and dwarfs: undersized, with old faces and tousled hair, dressed in moss and leaves, or in green clothes with red facings, they by these outward signs recall the dwarf zwergs. Those tree openings through which superstitious mothers drag sick children are called in Sweden efenluckor; in Rus', to be rid of sicknesses, they hang cloths and shirts on trees, and these offerings are counted the usual gift to the rusalkas — for whose kinship on the one side with the elves, and on the other with the forest nymphs, see ch. XXIII. Like the leshie, the elves possess the power of increasing and diminishing at their pleasure; both the one and the other are equally dangerous as stealers of children (see ch. XXIV). By a testimony belonging to the thirteenth century, the Germans recognized the wind as a forest spirit which could grow ever higher and higher, until it became even with the tallest tree. Ranking the leshie among the giants, who personify the whirlwinds, the tempest, and the thunderstorm, the traditions endow them with extraordinary strength and sternness, and ascribe to them the same endless battles in which the giants constantly engage. The folk sayings express themselves: "strong (or: hale) as the lesnik!" "with the lesovik there's no coping!" In Vladimir province the leshy is called the dikinkiy muzhichok (the wild little peasant), as the Poles call him dziki mąż, and the Germans wilder Mann; and with us, and in Germany, this name ("the wild man") serves preeminently for the designation of the giant (see ch. XXI). Like all thunder-spirits, the leshy can take various forms, in which the poetic fancy from of old personified the stormy and thunder-bearing clouds, and thereby draws near to the werewolves. Most often he appears as a strapping peasant, but even in this human guise he keeps his demonic marks: on him is a sheepskin coat, but as always happens with the unclean power, ungirt and wrapped with the left flap over the right (I, 187); his eyes are without brows and lashes — a belief which remains for us unguessed. They present him also as one-eyed, which points to his kinship with the cyclops-giants. If the leshy shows himself naked, without clothes, it is easy to notice how like he is to the commonly accepted image of the devil: on his head horns, his legs goat's, his head and all the lower half of his body shaggy, in tufts, his beard goat's, wedge-shaped, on his hands long claws. Near Ryazan they believe that in the forests dwell tsarki with golden little horns. The German schrat is depicted as wild, stern, and shaggy, with tousled hair (schrötleinzopfe); Old High German scrat or scrato in the old monuments is explained as pilosus, and scraz, sereza — as larvae, lares mali; Old Czech scret — daemon, screti (scretti) — penates intimi et secretales, Germ. skiet, skřitek — penas, idolum, Polish skrzot and skrzitek — a spirit presented with tangled, bristling hair, Slovene škrat, škratiz, škratelj — a mountain dwarf. These names have one common root with the verb skryt' (to hide). The word kryt' (to cover) is identical in meaning with the verbs oblachat' (whence oblako — the heavenly cover) and obvertyvat' (oborotit'), and the just-adduced names point to spirits clothing themselves in cloudy garments. Since the clouds were likened to beasts' skins (sheep's, goat's, and so forth) and to various shaggy animals, the forest spirits are drawn by the Slavs' imagination as shaggy beings, with goat's legs, beard, and horns, recalling the satyrs and fauns of the antique world and the goats of Bacchus and Thor, or dressed in sheepskin coats. These coats, like their shirts as we shall see, are not girt and fly free in the wind, like the cloudy mantle of the Wild Huntsman (Odin). Here lies the explanation also of the close bond of the leshie with the mountain dwarfs (the mountain-cloud) and the werewolves: in the old glosses adduced by Hanka, the word vilkodlak (werewolf) is interpreted faunus. In his quality as thunder-demon, the leshy draws near also to the serpent, the evil stealer of rains; in Belorussia among the manifold tsmoki (serpents) they know also the forest tsmok, which kills the masters' cattle, sucks at night the cows' milk, and makes the fields barren. Consequently to the leshy is ascribed the same sucking of milk from cows as to the witches and the fiery serpent: a belief arisen in consequence of the forgetting of the myth of the sucking of milk-rain from the cloud-cows by the lightning demons. In Olonets province they are convinced that every shepherd must present the leshy with a cow for the summer; else he will grow angry and spoil the whole herd. In Arkhangelsk province they think that the leshy, if the shepherds manage to propitiate him, pastures the village herd; and in Little Russia the lisovik is counted the shepherd of wolves (the clouds, see I, 711). — In many localities they tell that the leshie have green hair on head and beard; such green hair is given also to the vodyanoy and the rusalkas. This belief on the one side witnesses to the evident identification of the leshie with the vegetable kingdom, and on the other points to that close relation in which stand with them the water-spirits, the keepers of the rain-springs.

In the spring thunderstorm the ancient tribes saw a stubborn and death-dealing battle of the cloud-giants; this warlike type is bestowed also on the leshie. The lesoviks, the peasants tell, wage frequent wars among themselves; but as beings of titanic breed, they know neither cannon, nor guns, nor powder, but break their adversaries with century-old trees, which they tear up then and there by the root, and with hundred-pud stones, broken from the cliffs; thrown by the leshy's hand, these stones and trees fly ten times swifter than a bullet and to huge distances — fifty versts and more. Forests broken by the tempest and mountain avalanches are the traces of their fierce battle. In such a grandiose picture the people depicts the blows of the thunderstorm and the flight of the stormy clouds, transferring the ancient representations of the heavenly trees and the mountain-clouds to the common forests and cliffs. Bringing the mythic tales down to earth, laying upon them everyday traits, the people tells that in every country the leshie are subject to their own voevodas and tsars, and that they too have soldiering (the levy), as among men. Over the Russian lesoviks reigns Musail (Mafusail)-les. As soon as among the peoples of various states war begins, the leshie of these lands enter among themselves into bloody battles: whole hosts of them meet, leading with them the forest beasts, and fight unsparingly until the human war ceases. The same part in folk strifes is ascribed also to the Serbian vilas, kin to our rusalkas (see ch. XXIII).



The leshy's lordship and the thunder-laughter

The leshie are the lords of the dense forests, and in some regions they are called the forest tsarki, the masters over the forest. In great forests two and three leshie hold sway. In Arkhangelsk province there is a tale of how two leshie quarreled with a third in dividing the forest lots, bound him and cast him off; by chance a hunter came upon him and freed him; in gratitude the leshy bore him by whirlwind from Novaya Zemlya to his home, and afterward went for him as a recruit and served out the hard service. When the leshy goes his rounds about his domains, at his approach the forest rustles and round about the trees crack. By night he comes to sleep in some stanok (forester's hut), and waking in the morning appoints the place of his future night's lodging. If the forest hut chosen by him is taken by a belated traveler or hunter, the leshy strives to get him out: now he will rush as a whirlwind over the hut and shake its roof, now fling open the door, now shake the nearest trees and raise a terrible noise; in a word — to him are ascribed all phenomena called forth in the forest by gusts of wind. If the uninvited guest even then does not obey, trouble threatens him: he will either lose his way in the forest, or stick fast in the marsh, led thither by the angered forest-spirit. Together with the forest lands, under the leshy's rule and protection stand also every beast dwelling in the forests, and every bird found there. His most beloved animal is the bear, of whom the traditions speak as of one of the chief embodiments of the thunder-god. The leshy is a great lover of wine (the metaphor of rain), yet never drinks a single bucket without toasting in order also the bear. Besides this beast, he takes no one into service to himself, and when, drunken, he lies down to nap — the bear walks about him on patrol and guards him from the vodyanye's attack. Squirrels, arctic foxes, and field mice, as is known, migrate from one place to another in great herds, and periodically (every two or three years) appear in multitude now here, now there: this is called the techka. The peasants explain it by the leshie driving the beast-herds from one pine-wood to another. In 1843 in the forests of the Varnavino and Vetluga districts there suddenly appeared a huge quantity of running squirrels; the local muzhiks said that the leshy was driving the squirrels from Vyatka province to Vologda; and others added that one leshy had lost his squirrels at cards to another leshy, and therefore was driving them from his own domain to another's. The same explanations are given in other localities; compare on p. 244 with like tales of the vodyanye. Success in the hunter's craft depends on the favor to him of the local leshy. Whoever takes it into his head to "forest" (hunt the forest beast) must first of all bring something in greeting to the leshy, that the catch may be lucky and that the mysterious master of the forest may not tangle the trapper in the thickets. For greeting they bring into the forest a crust of bread (or a pancake), with salt on top, and lay this offering on some stump. The Permyaks pray to the leshy yearly, bringing him a packet of leaf tobacco, of which, in their opinion, he is strongly fond. On the Beskids and in the woody places of the Volga region the hunters have the custom: the first catch to leave in the oak-wood, as a sacrifice to the forest spirit. In the spells uttered for success in beast-catching we meet an address to the leshie: "dodite vy, satanaily, d'yavoly, leshie, v takoi-to ostrov, prigonite rusakov i belyakov (zaitsev) na moi kleti postavnye — sumerechnye, vechernie, nochnye, utrennie i poludennye; prigonite, ostanovite i v moikh kletyakh primknite" — "come ye, satanails, devils, leshie, to such-and-such an island, drive the rusaks and belyaks (hares) to my set snares — twilight, evening, night, morning, and noon ones; drive them, stop them, and in my snares shut them fast." Called to help by the all-powerful word of the incantations, the leshy drives into the trapper's pitfalls, traps, and nets wolves, martens, foxes, squirrels, hares, and brings to his gun both beast and forest bird. If we recall that the thunderstorm was depicted as a heavenly hunt, it will be understood why the leshie, by folk beliefs, drive the forest beast, and why to them precisely the hunter addresses his requests.

The leshy loves to wander through the forest, to hang and sway on the tree branches, as in a cradle or on a swing, for which in some provinces they give him the name zybochnik; appearing now here, now there, he whistles, guffaws, claps his hands, shouts aloud in various voices: neighs like a horse, lows like a cow, barks and mews. His guffaw is heard, by folk tales, forty versts around in circumference. This titanic laughter, whistling, clapping, and shouting are old metaphors for the designation of thunder and of the howling winds. The likening of thunder to laughter is met in the poetic tales of the Indians; among Slavs, Germans, and other kindred peoples it gave birth to myths of Satan's terrible guffaw resounding in the hellish dens, and tales of the thunder-goddess, whose merry laughter falls silent for all the time of winter (see I, 602, 778). The Greeks transferred this metaphor to the crackle of logs burning on the hearth; Aristotle expresses himself of the fire's crackle as of the laughter of Hephaestus and Hestia. The horse, the cow, the dog, and the cat are zoomorphic personifications of the thunder-clouds, and therefore the leshy shouts with their wild voices, striving to frighten the timid traveler. Having brought the leshy down from the cloudy heights into the common forests, the folk memory kept all his characteristic marks, created once under the influence of the metaphorical tongue; the leshy's guffaw and shouts continue to be heard by superstitious villagers in the crack of falling trees, in the noise of leaves and the howling of winds shaking the oak-woods. The echo, resounding from the depth of the forest thicket, the villagers count the leshy's answer, and think that he purposely answers man's voice, to lure him into the impassable thickets or marshes and there tickle him to death; especially often exposed to this danger are hunters and woodcutters. The rusalkas, in whose image fancy combined into one the representations of the water and forest maidens, also love to sway on the tree branches, also burst into evil laughter and tickle to death the unwary travelers lured to them. Shchekotat' originally meant: to utter strong, sharp sounds, like the nightingale's whistle (I, 302): "o Boyane, soloviyu starogo vremeni! aby ty sia plky ushchekot al'" — "o Boyan, nightingale of the old time! wouldst thou tickle these hosts" — exclaims the singer of the Lay of Igor's Host. The nightingale's shchekot, as explained in ch. VI, served for the metaphor of the loudly resounding songs of the thunderstorm. From the notion "to utter sharp sounds" the word shchekotat' passed to the designation of that action by which they are called forth, and namely to the designation of tickling, by which loud, involuntary laughter is excited. In like manner the verb loskotat' — to tickle someone (loskat', loskonut' — to deal blows) — in the Polish tongue (łoskotać) keeps the meaning: to clap, to crack, to thunder. In the rushing flight of the howling tempest the thunderous, elf-like spirits (the leshie, the rusalkas) fall upon the demons of the dark clouds, spin and tickle them and make them laugh with thunderous laughter, until they perish in terrible convulsive movements — that is, until the clouds are scattered by the thunderstorm. In this picture, sketched with a bold poetic brush, lurks the basis of the folk belief in the tickling by leshie and rusalkas of the people seized by them; the formation of this belief was accomplished under the influence of those mythic representations by which the elves and the elemental beings kin to them were recognized as stealers of human souls (see ch. XXIV).



The leshy's track

Those cases when the traveler loses the road (which, of course, not rarely happened in the dense virgin forests of the old time), and all the unpleasantness experienced therewith, the people's superstition ascribes to the evil pranks of the leshy: he purposely tangles, or by the folk expression obkhodit — walks around — those wandering through the forest, that is, as it were shuts them in with an invisible circular line, from behind which man can no longer get out without outside help. Among the peasants walk a multitude of tales of how the leshy leads astray. Wishing that someone should lose his way and wander in the forest till deep night, he with design moves the road-marks from one place to another, or himself turns into exactly such a tree as serves in the forest for a landmark: into a spruce, a pine, into the moss growing on the tree-trunk, and stands aside from the true road; through this he tangles the reckonings of the neighboring inhabitants, leads them into the thickets, and having had his fill of sport, bursts into loud laughter. Sometimes he shows himself to the traveler in the form of a gray old man, in a beast's skin and with a great staff in hand, or taking upon himself the guise of some one of his acquaintances; he begins a conversation with the one he meets and imperceptibly leads him from the road. Thus, for example, a muzhik drives all alone; a passer-by overtakes him, and word by word begs a lift to the village; the muzhik agrees; they drive, peacefully conversing... Suddenly look — the passer-by is as though he never was, vanished from sight! and the muzhik with his cart is in the marsh or the ravine; he looks about and sees: on a steep, inaccessible precipice, whither no man could by any means climb, stands the leshy, whistling and clapping his hands. Be on guard in the forest: whether it seems that a child cries, or the groans of the dying are heard — hasten not to help; whether there seems to you a noisy river where before you met none — go straight, not turning aside; else you will land straight in the quagmire. All this the leshy befools! Let us note that the child's cry and the groans of the dying were heard by ancient man in the howlings of the thunder-tempest, and the noisily pouring river he saw in the rain-streams. As a spirit whose appearance is always accompanied by whirlwinds, the leshy sweeps away every trace — covers the road, veils it with dust, sand, heaps of leaves and snow. Hence, by folk conviction, whoever gets upon the leshy's track (upon the path by which he lately rushed), he will unfailingly lose his way in the forest and not return home till the next morning, or till the lesovik himself leads him out to the road. In connection with this stands the belief forbidding the sweeping of huts on the day of some kinsman's departure, lest his road be dusted over (I, 37–38). Whoever loses his way in the forest, whom the leshy has walked around, his only means to find the road: to take from his feet the bast-shoes or boots and turn the insoles in them — that is, taking the straw out of the bast-shoes, to lay it so that the padding that lay by the toes should end up at the heels and vice versa. Footwear is the symbol of step, of movement; turning it back to front, the lost wanderer believes that his feet will move not to those places whither the leshy directs, but in the opposite direction, and thus will lead him out to the straight road. The leshy's elemental significance shows itself brightly in his mischievous character: he blows at the traveler with the whirlwind, dusts his eyes, tears the cap from him, can freeze his sleigh to the earth, so that the horse is not strong enough to stir from the place — and so forth. "Go not into the forest, there the leshy jokes!" advises the folk saying. The leshy's jokes are dangerous; by that closeness into which the tongue, and after it the beliefs, have put sicknesses in relation to the elemental spirits — the elves (see ch. XXII) — he, joking upon man, sends upon him grievous ailments. If it happens that someone falls sick, returning from the forest, the villagers say on this account: "stalo z lesu" (Chernigov province) — "it came from the forest" — or "sled leshago pereshel" — "the leshy's track crossed"; of the kazhenniks (the crippled, the half-witted) they think that they have been walked around by the leshy. To propitiate the leshy, they bear him in gift a loaf of bread and a pinch of salt, wrapped in a clean cloth. Coming into the forest, the sick man prays over the bread-and-salt, leaves there his offering, and returns home with the full conviction that the sickness remained in the forest. There are also other indications of the sacrificial gifts with which the leshy was once honored: once he helped a peasant — chopped wood for him — and he thanked him with a palyanitsa; from the muzhiks working in the forest he begs pies, and when they give him, cries: "shel, shel, da nashel!" — "went, went, and found!"



Lisunki and divozheny

The manner of life of the leshie is described variously: some of them love to live in the forest thickets solitary, apart, like savages, enduring near them none of their brethren; others settle in great villages, build in the forests roomy houses, with all necessary offices, where their wives keep house and their children grow. The lisunki (leshachikhi) — the forest maidens and wives — are the same as the leshie, only in the feminine personification; the folk imagination endows them with such huge and long breasts that they are forced to throw them over their shoulders, and only then can freely walk and run. This tradition points in the lisunki to the cloud nymphs, who are constantly depicted as full-breasted, since the clouds themselves were likened to women's breasts, pouring from their teats abundant milk-rain. In Poland and Galicia the lisunki are called dziwożony, in Bohemia divé ženy; they are distinguished by a wild and malicious temper, their bodies are covered with hair, their long loose braids stream through the air; their breasts are so great that, washing linen (that is, engaged in the rinsing of the cloudy garments and covers in the rain-springs), they use them instead of beetles; on their heads they wear red caps. In the forests of the Giant Mountains mornings and evenings roams a black giantess, whom they call lesní paní. The Scandinavian mythology knows forest wives and maidens under the names íviðjur and iarnviðjur (from íviðr — forest, grove; iarnviðr — eisenwald); in Germany, beside the Holzmännlein, are known: Holz- (or Wald-) frauen, wilde Weiber (the divozheny), Moosweibchen, Lohjungfern, and Holzfräulein. Of the wild wives they tell that they live in the forest — in caves and rock clefts — wear a shaggy or green dress, have long, loose hair, and are not always unkind to men, sometimes counsel and help them. The Holzweibchen not rarely comes to the woodcutters, asks to mend the broken wheel of her cart, and like Berhta rewards the labor with the remaining chips, which afterward turn into pure gold, or gives a ball of thread which — unwind it as you will — never diminishes. By chance a peasant woman came upon a weeping child, born of a forest wife, and from pity nursed him at her breast; and the grateful mother gave her for this a piece of tree bark, which afterward turned into gold. A like tale exists also in Little Russia: it befell a baba to find in the forest the leshy's little one; the child lay naked and wept bitterly, the baba covered him with her svitka — and lo, a lisunka appeared and rewarded her with a handful of hot coals; when the baba returned home, instead of coals there proved to be bright ducats. These golden gifts point to the forest spirits' possession of those dear treasures which are hidden in the cloud-forests; and the ball of thread to the lisunki's bond with the fateful spinners (the norns). By German tales, the forest maidens betray an involuntary fear and give themselves to swift flight as soon as they espy the Wild Huntsman's train. In the tempest's noise Wodan chases the mythic wife with great, snow-white breasts, seizes her, and throwing her on the swift horse, carries her to himself. In middle Germany they tell that the wild huntsman, accompanied by his retinue and dogs, pursues a whole crowd of white-breasted forest maidens, fleeing from him through the aerial spaces. This beautiful poetic image of the thunder-tempest driving before it crowds of rain-bearing clouds must later have merged with the representation of the tempest in the forest, which breaks trees, tears off the green leaves, and drives them far in its swift flight; for as soon as upon the cloud nymphs was fixed the name of forest maidens, they were recognized as the personification of the green oak-woods. The leshie love to turn into plants, and to this day are not rarely presented as spirits dwelling in the trunk of this or that tree. To call forth the leshy one must hew young birches and lay them in a circle, tops inward; then take the cross from oneself, and standing in the circle, shout loudly: "dedushka!" — and the leshy will at once appear. Or, in the night before Ivan's day, one must go into the forest and hew an aspen — so that it fall with its top to the eastern side; on the felled stump he who wishes to see the leshy stands with his face to the east, bends down, and looking between his legs, says: "dyadya leshy! Pokazhis' ni serym volkom, ni chernym voronom, ni el'yu zharovoyu; pokazhis' takim, kakov ya!" — "Uncle leshy! Appear not as the gray wolf, not as the black raven, not as the blazing spruce; appear such as I am!" Then the aspen's leaves will rustle as from a light breath of wind, and the leshy appears at the call in a muzhik's guise. They assure that one must hew a pine in the forest in one's sleep(?), and hew so that in its fall it break two aspens; on these broken aspens one should stand with one's face to the north and say: "lesovik-velikan! prishel k tebe rab (imyarek) s poklonom, zavedi s nim druzhbu" — "Forest-giant! thy servant (name) is come to thee with greeting, make friends with him." When the leshy appears, with him one can enter into a compact; by this compact the muzhik gives him his soul into bondage, and the leshy binds himself to help the muzhik in the hunt, to drive beasts to him, to guard his herd, and to seek out lost cattle. To acquaintance with the leshy only sorcerers venture. At the base of these beliefs lies the thought that the leshy, as the spirit of thunderstorm and whirlwinds, appears only at the time when Perun's axe begins to hew the cloud-trees, and therefore only sorcerers, men of supernatural, magic powers, can call him forth. The being of the forest maidens the traditions bind with close, unbreakable bonds to the life of various trees; when a tree is hewn, together with it must die also that forest nymph whose existence has grown together with this tree. Such are the Greek meliads and dryads (dryades) — nymphs of the ash and the oak; if the oak withers and dries, together with this the dryad's powers weaken and she dies; every cut dealt the tree she feels as her own wound. Like beliefs were spread also among the Celts; Jacob Grimm points to traces of them surviving among the Germanic tribes; and among the Slavs are preserved a multitude of poetic tales of trees which (when they are hewn) utter plaintive groans and shed blood (see ch. XIX). The elf-king's soldiers are by day trees, and by night brave warriors; the folk sagas give the elves green garments and settle them under the tree bark; the peasant, betrothed to an elfmaid, embraced instead of his bride an oak trunk. In Saxony there was honored in olden time the tree Frau Ellhorn (sambucus nigra); he who wished to make use of its branches had to address it with the following prayer: "Frau Ellhorn! gib mir was von deinem Holz, dann will ich dir von meinem auch was geben, wann es wächst im Walde!" These words were pronounced kneeling, with bared head and folded hands. In Süderhannland a servant took it into his head to hew a beautiful, shady juniper tree; but scarcely had he set to the work when from the tree resounded a voice: "hau den Kacholder nicht!" There is preserved also a tale of a fairy dwelling in a pine tree, whom dwarfs serve. A Serbian song tells how a brave youth prayed God:

"Daj mi, Bože, zlatne roge, srebrne paroščiće, da probodem boru koru, da ja viđu, šta ju boru."

God gave him a golden horn and silver antlers, with them the youth pierced the pine bark — and in the pine a young maiden, bright as the sun ("al' u boru mlada moma, pak zasija kano sunce"). The golden horn is the lightning which bores the cloud-tree. The Czechs have many traditions of beings like the Greek dryads. Thus of one linden they tell that in it lived a white wife, and when she came out thence — she struck all with her radiance; she was now kind, now evil, and recalled now the poludnitsa, now the divozhena. By this tree were performed folk feasts and spring games. The life of another woman was mysteriously bound with a willow; by day she remained in her family, and by night her soul left her body and withdrew into the willow. When her husband learned of this, he hewed the willow — and in that same instant his wife also died. Only maternal love continued to live in the felled tree; a cradle made of it rocked the orphaned child, and when he grew up and fashioned himself a pipe from a young shoot on the willow's old stump, that pipe with its melodious sounds talked with him like a tender mother. In the wild hunt, or more directly in the spring thunderstorm, the stormy, thunder-bearing spirit chases the cloud maidens, overtakes them and violates them (bores with lightning). Hither belong also the antique tales of the lustful satyrs pursuing the beautiful nymphs. Our villagers tell that the leshie are "fond of women," steal the unwary maidens and force them to live with them in a love-union. In the thunderstorm and whirlwinds in some provinces they see the wedding revelry of the leshie, whereas in other localities these natural phenomena are explained as the weddings of devils and witches. Their weddings the leshie celebrate noisily, with wild bacchanalian revelry; their train is always accompanied by strong winds and devastation. If the train gallops through the village, then unfailingly at many houses it will carry off the roofs, here and there scatter the barns and ricks; and if it drives through the forest, it will fell the trees (compare with the vodyanoy's wedding, p. 239). Whether the tempest tears up by the roots a whole row of trees, or heaps somewhere piles of fallen wood — the peasant is convinced that precisely in this place swept the leshy's wedding. Rare is the peasant who dares in the summer season to lie down for rest on the forest path; for they fear lest by chance the wedding train of the lesoviks ride over and crush the sleeper. In Arkhangelsk province they think that the whirlwind rises because the leshy gives himself to dancing at his wedding with the lisunka. The demonic revelry, filled with every temptation, calls forth (by folk belief) the heavenly thunders, whose blows not rarely turn the leshie and lisunki into wolverines — and therefore this beast is counted accursed: a belief witnessing to the close bond of the thunder-spirits with the werewolves. On the day after the wedding, the leshy, by Russian custom, goes with his young wife to the bathhouse, and if at that time someone happens to pass by, they unfailingly douse him from head to foot with water — that is, the thunder-spirits, entering into the spring marriage, bathe in the rain-streams and drench all who come under the rain.
