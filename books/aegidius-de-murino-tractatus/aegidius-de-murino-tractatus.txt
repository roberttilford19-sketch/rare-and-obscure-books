# The Treatise on Measured Song (Tractatus Cantus Mensurabilis)

**by Master Aegidius de Murino (14th century)**
A fresh English rendering, plain and complete

---

**A fresh translation, not a reprint.** Master Aegidius de Murino's
*Tractatus cantus mensurabilis* — the working handbook that taught a
14th-century choirmaster how to build a motet voice by voice — is here
rendered whole into plain English: the method of composing motet
tenors, the perfect and imperfect modes, the division of tenors, the
ordering of rests, the five-voice motet, and the forms of the ballade
and the rondellus, translated sentence by sentence from the Latin of
Edmond de Coussemaker's edition, vol. 3 (Paris, 1864), pp. 124-128.

Original: Latin treatise, 14th century. Basis for this rendering: the
Latin text as printed in Coussemaker, *Scriptorum de musica medii aevi
nova series a Gerbertina altera*, vol. 3 (Paris: A. Durand, 1864),
pp. 124-128, read in full in the searchable transcription of the
Thesaurus Musicarum Latinarum (Indiana University), entry AEGTRA_TEXT,
verified live 2026-10-11 (provisional mirror, the main TML site being
down for a university-wide outage).

**How it is presented:** Aegidius's text in clear modern English, with
the Latin term kept in parentheses where English has no single word
for it. The treatise's notated tenor examples are described in words;
no notation is drawn or faked. A short preface explains the
conventions, a note describes the state of the Latin text, and a
glossary gathers the book's terms.

**Reader note:** This is a craftsman's manual, not a philosopher's
book: how to take a chant tenor, lay it out ("ordain" it) and "color"
it in perfect or imperfect mode, stack contratenor, triplum, and
quadruplum above it, fit words to music, set rests that do not break
the pattern, and close with the open and shut cadences of the ballade
and rondellus. Nothing in it is praised or modernized — it is
presented as Aegidius made it, for the reader to judge.

## Dedication

**Master Aegidius de Murino** — who he was: a 14th-century master of
music known to us by one thing only, the name set at the head of this
treatise, *Magistri Aegidii de Murino*, "Master Giles of Murino."
No chronicle records his birth, his town beyond the name Murino, his
pupils, or his grave.

The circumstances of his death are therefore unknown, and are not
pretended here; his book is his only monument. The treatise's own
closing line — *Explicit tractatus cantus mensurabilis secundum
magistrum Egidium de Murino* ("Here ends the treatise on measured
song according to Master Giles of Murino") — is the whole of his
epitaph.

Why his work is vital — proved, not praised: the treatise itself is
the proof. Where other writers of his century dispute the philosophy
of notation, Aegidius writes the workshop manual: take a tenor from
the antiphoner, "ordain and color" it, build a three-, four-, or
five-voice motet over it, divide and diminish it, sign its repeats,
set its rests, fit its words — and then the ballade and the
rondellus with their open (*apertum*) and closed (*clausum*)
endings. It is one of the few surviving practical composition manuals
of the ars nova generation, and it closes, as a master closes a
lesson, with the proverb *sapienti et intelligenti pauca sufficiunt*
— "for the wise and understanding, few words suffice" — adding that
what stands written above was written *ad doctrinam parvulorum*,
"for the instruction of little ones."

Its relation to this rescue: the Boneyard's mensural-notation row
began with Franco of Cologne's *Ars cantus mensurabilis*, which taught
every note to carry its own counted length. Aegidius is the ideal
small follow-on in that row — the foreman's handbook that puts
Franco's counted notes to work in real motets, voice by voice. The
series rescues the working knowledge of dead masters; this is working
knowledge in its purest form.

Where a reader goes next: Edmond de Coussemaker, *Scriptorum de
musica medii aevi nova series a Gerbertina altera*, vol. 3 (Paris:
A. Durand, 1864), pp. 124-128 — the printed home of the Latin text;
and the Thesaurus Musicarum Latinarum, entry AEGTRA, for the
searchable transcription.

## How to read this book

Aegidius assumes you sing in a choir and know the chant book (the
*antiphonarium*). His technical words are kept in Latin in
parentheses at first use:

- *tenor* — the given chant melody that "holds" (*tenere*) the piece;
  the foundation voice.
- *contratenor* — the voice set "against the tenor."
- *triplum* — the third voice above; *quadruplum* — the fifth voice
  of all (tenor, contratenor, motetus, triplum, quadruplum).
- *motetus* — the composed upper voice carrying the text (what we
  would call the motet voice proper).
- *modus perfectus / imperfectus* — perfect mode (three, or six,
  *tempora* to the note) and imperfect mode (two, or four).
- *colorare* — "to color": to lay the tenor out in a repeating
  rhythmic pattern; a *color* is the repeating pattern itself, and
  the *talea* its rhythmic shape. "Do not break the color" means do
  not spoil the repeating pattern.
- *ordinare* — "to ordain/order": to arrange the tenor's notes in
  their measured lengths.
- *pausa* — a rest. *Punctus divisionis* — the dot of division.
- *discantus* — discant: note-against-note counterpoint sung above
  or below a given voice.

Bracketed notes in the form [Editor's note] are the editor's, not
Aegidius's. Notated examples in the Latin are described in plain
words in brackets, e.g. [a notated example follows]; no notation is
reproduced or reconstructed.

## Note on the Latin text

The text is short — five pages in Coussemaker — and reads as one
continuous lesson with no chapter breaks; paragraphing here is the
editor's. A few spellings are the edition's (*zeze vel cese per
semi* for the customary *diminutio per semi*; *Vironellus* for the
virelai-type form). Transmission scars are left visible and flagged
where they matter.

## The Treatise on Measured Song, according to Master Giles of Murino

### On the way of composing the tenors of motets

First take the tenor of some antiphon or responsory or other chant
from the antiphoner; and the words ought to agree in subject matter
with that of which you make the motet. Then take up the tenor, and
you shall ordain and color it as will appear below, in the perfect
or the imperfect mode. The perfect mode is when three *tempora*,
or six, are counted to the note. The imperfect mode is when two
*tempora*, or four, are counted to the note — and [this] when the
tenor is well ordained.

Then, if you wish to make a motet with four [voices], then you shall
also ordain and color the contratenor above the tenor; and when you
wish to divide the contratenor, then take the tenor — and the
contratenor, if you compose with four [voices] — and, they being
ordained, [set] the triplum above, well, so that it agrees with the
tenor and the contratenor. And if you wish to make the upper voice
itself agree, then divide the tenor into two parts, or four, or as
many parts as pleases you; and when you have made one part over the
tenor, then that part must be figured just like the first part, and
like the other part; and this is called "coloring motets."

Item, you can add another subtlety there, and this is: if you wish,
you can make it in the perfect mode — that is, compare over the
*tempora* together, and after three *tempora* there must remain the
dot of division. This done, proceed to the motet, that is, to the
fifth [voice]; and you shall make it agree, and color it, with the
triplum and the tenor and with the contratenor, if it is with four
[voices] — and do so to the end.

After the song is made and ordained, then take the words that must
be in the motet and divide them into four parts; and so divide the
song into four parts; and fit the first part of the words over the
first part of the song, as best can be, and so proceed to the end;
and sometimes it is necessary to stretch many notes over few words,
over few *tempora*, until they arrive at the completion.

[Editor's note — in plain words: Start with a chant tune whose words
suit your text. Lay it out in measured note-lengths in threes (or
sixes) for perfect mode, twos (or fours) for imperfect. Build the
contratenor above it, then the triplum, then — if you want five
voices — the motet voice itself, keeping every voice in the same
repeating rhythmic pattern ("color"). Then cut your poem into four
and lay it over the music's four sections, stretching syllables
where you must.]

### How you shall ordain and color the tenors

Now it must be shown how you shall ordain and color the tenors; and
you shall ordain and color the contratenors just like the tenors —
yet the contratenor can be colored otherwise than the tenor, if you
wish. And first of the perfect mode, as will consequently appear.
And if the tenor is said more than once, the contratenor must be
signed. But if it is said twice — since the first time it is not
necessary to sign — when it is said twice, then put this sign: 2;
but if it is said three times, put this sign: 3; but if four times,
put this sign: 4. And according to the old masters it is figured
thus: II, III, IIII, 2, 3, 4.

[Editor's note — in plain words: Mark repeats. The old masters wrote
II, III, IIII; you may write 2, 3, 4.]

### Tenor examples in the perfect mode

This tenor is of the perfect mode, and each note is worth six
*tempora*, and it is colored, because all are alike, and it is
called an ordained tenor, because there is no mixture in it except
rests. [A notated example follows: an F-clef tenor made of two-note
ligatures, all of equal value.]

This tenor is of the perfect mode, and the full [notes] are fully
worth six *tempora*, and the void [notes] are worth four *tempora*,
and these three void are worth two full. And this tenor is called
mixed; yet it is colored; yet more void notes can be put in the
tenor if you wish — yet always guard the color. And so of the other
mixtures that are put. Understand: of longs and breves and rests.
[A notated example follows, mixing full and void notes.]

This tenor is of the perfect mode, and the greater [notes] are worth
six *tempora*, and each lesser is worth one tempus; and these six
must be dotted before and behind, because they can be counted with
the note going before or following. And these six lessers make up
one greater, and it is a mixed tenor; yet it is colored. [A notated
example follows, with dotted small notes between great ones.]

This tenor is of the perfect mode, and the greater are worth six
*tempora* and the lesser three. [A notated example follows.]

This tenor is of the perfect mode, and the greater are worth six
*tempora*, and after the greater there must be the dot of division.
And the first lesser that follows is worth one tempus, the second
two, the third three *tempora*. And it is a mixed tenor, yet
colored. [A notated example follows.]

This tenor is of the perfect mode, and the greater are worth six
*tempora* and the lesser three *tempora*; and they must be dotted
before and behind, because they can be counted with the great ones;
and this tenor is mixed, yet colored. [A notated example follows.]

This tenor is of the perfect mode, and the greater are worth six
*tempora* and each lesser one tempus; and it is a mixed tenor, yet
colored. [A notated example follows.]

This tenor is of the perfect mode; and each full [note] is worth
three *tempora*, and the void are worth two *tempora*; and the
color is mixed, yet colored. [A notated example follows on a
five-line staff with a high F clef.]

This tenor is of the perfect mode, and the first is worth one
tempus, the second two, the third three. [A notated example
follows.]

[Editor's note — in plain words: Nine worked examples of the same
idea: a tenor is "ordained" when every note has its counted length,
"colored" when the lengths fall into a repeating pattern, "mixed"
when full and void notes, great and small, are combined — dotted
small notes may be counted either way, but the pattern must never
break.]

### How such tenors may be divided

And such tenors can be divided in four parts in diverse ways. The
first time it is said as it stands; the second time it is divided,
or diminished, by half from the first — and then the first note is
worth one semibreve, the second two, the third three, and so to the
end.

Item, the third time it can be said in imperfect time — and then the
first note is worth one [tempus], the third two *tempora*, and so to
the end.

Item, the fourth time, this imperfect time is diminished by half,
that is, by the middle; then the first note is worth one semibreve,
the second one, the third two, and so to the end.

Item, this tenor can be divided in four parts otherwise, namely:
the first time, in the perfect mode; then the first is worth one
tempus, the second two, the third three, and so to the end.

Item, if the second time it can be said in imperfect time, then the
first is worth one tempus, the second one, the third two *tempora*.

Item, if the third time it is diminished by half from the first
utterance, then the first note is worth one semibreve, the second
two, the third three semibreves.

Item, if the fourth time, then it is diminished by half from the
second utterance; and then the first note is worth one semibreve,
the second one, the third two semibreves, and so to the end. And
rests can be put after the perfections, but always guard the color;
and it must be written under the tenor, thus: first it is said in
the perfect mode; second in imperfect time; third diminished by
half from the first; and fourth diminished by half from the second
utterance. [In the Latin: *primo dicitur de modo perfecto; secundo
de tempore imperfecto; tertio zeze vel cese per semi de primo; et
quarto zeze sive cese per semi de secunda vice* — the *zeze/cese*
being the edition's spelling for the customary "diminished by half."]

### Tenors of the imperfect mode

When this tenor is of the imperfect mode, then each [note] is worth
four *tempora*; and when it is of the perfect mode, then each is
worth six *tempora*, as said above of the tenors of the perfect
mode; and it is an ordained and colored tenor. [A notated example
follows.]

When you wish this tenor to be of the imperfect mode, then each is
worth two *tempora*; and when you wish it to be of the perfect
mode, then each is worth three *tempora*, as set above in the
tenors of the perfect mode; and it is an ordained and colored
tenor. [A notated example follows.]

When this tenor is of the imperfect mode, then the greater are
worth four *tempora* and the lesser two; and when it is of the
perfect mode, then the greater are worth six *tempora* and the
lesser three, as appears above. [A notated example follows.]

When this tenor is of the imperfect mode, then the greater are
worth four *tempora*, and each lesser one tempus. But in the
perfect mode the lessers are dotted, as appeared above; and it is
not necessary to dot them in the imperfect mode. [A notated example
follows.]

This tenor is of the imperfect mode, and the greater are each worth
four *tempora* and each lesser two *tempora*. [A notated example
follows.]

This tenor is of the perfect mode, and the first note is worth one
and the fourth three *tempora*, and the rests are counted by
themselves; and it can be said twice, if you wish, as it stands, or
a division can be made the second time by half from the first; and
then the notes are worth three semibreves, and each rest is worth
one semibreve. [A notated example follows.]

This tenor is of the imperfect mode, and the first two notes are
worth one tempus each, and the third is worth two *tempora*; and it
can be divided by half, if said twice — then the first two are
worth one semibreve each, the third two semibreves, and the rest
the first time is worth two *tempora*, the second time worth only
one tempus. [A notated example follows.]

[Editor's note — in plain words: The same game in the imperfect
mode: every note worth four (or two) instead of six (or three).
Small notes need no dots in the imperfect mode. A tenor sung twice
can be halved the second time — and the rests halve with it.]

### The master rests his case

Know that by the tenors said above, many other modes can be found,
and other tenors deduced, by the way of subtlety; and therefore it
is not necessary to compose contratenors. Of the many that can be
found, when a subtle singer exists, he can make many other tenors
of them; and therefore for the wise and understanding few things
suffice — but what is written above was written for the instruction
of little ones.

[Editor's note — in plain words: A good singer can spin new tenors
from these patterns himself; the master stops here, saying the rest
is exercise.]

### On rests, how you shall ordain them in tenors

Hence it must be said of rests (*pausae*), how you shall ordain
them in tenors. And first, of the perfect mode: the rests must be
counted by themselves. Then put in the tenors three, or six, or
nine rests of one tempus, and they must be dotted on either side,
as here. [A notated example of dotted rests follows.] And they must
not be put except after perfections.

Item, if you wish to compose or ordain rests with the perfections:
if the rest must go with the first, that is, with what is before
it, then it must be dotted behind, as here. [A notated example
follows.]

Item, if it must go with the perfection that is behind it, then it
must be dotted before, as here. [A notated example follows.]

Item, you can also put such rests in the perfect mode, and in no
other, because it signifies the mode of perfection, as here. [A
notated example follows.] Sometimes two can be put together, as
here — and they must not be put except after perfections; but
beware lest you break the color.

Item, if you wish to put rests in the imperfect mode, it is not
necessary to dot them, but they stand by themselves, as here — or
thus. [Two notated examples follow.] If they stand by themselves,
they are counted with the perfections [and] must not be dotted.

Item, if you wish to put rests of two *tempora*, then put them as
here — for as many spaces as they touch, so many *tempora* they
are worth. [A notated example follows.]

Item, if you wish to put rests for three *tempora* together, then
you must put one of two and another of one tempus, as here. [A
notated example follows.]

[Editor's note — in plain words: In perfect mode, rests are dotted
on both sides and sit only after a completed perfection; a rest
leaning forward is dotted behind, leaning back dotted before. In
imperfect mode no dots are needed. A rest's length is read off the
staff-spaces it covers: two spaces, two tempora — and a three-tempus
rest is written as a two plus a one.]

### If you want more

If you desire to have greater subtleties than are contained in this
little book, then study hard at music, and perhaps God will grant
you, by his grace, greater understanding and subtlety.

### Another way of composing motets

But there is another way of composing motets than said above,
namely: that the tenor goes above the motet. And you shall ordain
thus:

Take the tenor from the antiphoner, as said above, which you shall
color and ordain; and it stands in low gamma; and you can put it in
high gamma. And when it is well ordained, then make discant under
the tenor, as best you know and can. And if you wish to make it
with four [voices], then there must be a contratenor there. But the
contratenor must come first and agree with the tenor, otherwise it
cannot be colored. Item, if you wish to make a motet with five
[voices], it can be made in this way: make the tenor first, as said,
and make [it] discanted under the tenor, and agreeing; in this way
make the triplum discant above the motet, as best you know. Still
you can make another discant which sounds around the triplum,
brightening the triplum itself. And this fifth song is called
quadruplum; and so the motet will be totally full. And I believe
no more songs can be made together.

[Editor's note — in plain words: The upside-down motet: put the
tenor on top (low or high), sing discant beneath it, add
contratenor, then triplum above the motet voice, then a fifth voice
— the quadruplum — haloing the triplum. Five voices, says the
master, is the most that can sing together.]

### On keeping the mode through the piece

Item, if [you have] motet, triplum, quadruplum, you can always make
them well in the perfect mode, though the song were of the
imperfect mode. Sometimes the song is of the perfect mode and of
perfect time, and then the dot of division must always be put after
three, and the first perfection must endure to the end.

### The ballade and the rondellus

In this way the simple ballade must be made: first make *apertum*
[open] and *clausum* [closed], and at the end make *clausum* only.

Item, the double ballade has *apertum* and *clausum* before and
behind.

Item, the simple vironellus has *apertum* before and *clausum*
behind.

Item, the double vironellus has half *apertum* and *clausum*
before, and *apertum* and *clausum* behind.

Item, the rondellus has *apertum* before: when it is finished on
*ut*, it must be a tenth; and when it is finished on *la*, it must
be a fifth — and *clausum* behind. Thanks be to God.

[Editor's note — in plain words: The dance-song forms in brief: a
simple ballade opens, closes, and ends closed; a double ballade
opens and closes at both ends; the vironellus (virelai) likewise in
single and double shapes; the rondellus (rondeau) opens with a
leap — a tenth if it ends on ut (C), a fifth if on la (A) — and
closes behind.]

Here ends the treatise on measured song according to Master Giles
of Murino.

## Glossary

- *apertum / clausum* — "open" / "closed": the two cadence-endings
  of a repeated song-form; open leaves the melody hanging, closed
  brings it home.
- *color, colorare* — the repeating rhythmic/melodic pattern laid
  over a tenor; "to color" is to lay it out.
- *contratenor* — the voice composed "against the tenor."
- *discantus* — note-against-note counterpoint.
- *gamma* — the lowest note of the medieval scale (our G below the
  staff); "low gamma" and "high gamma" are low and high placements
  of the tenor.
- *modus perfectus / imperfectus* — perfect mode (triple division)
  and imperfect mode (duple division).
- *motetus* — the texted upper voice of a motet.
- *ordinare* — to arrange a tenor's notes in measured lengths.
- *pausa* — a rest; its length is read from the staff-spaces it
  covers.
- *punctus divisionis* — the dot of division, marking perfection
  boundaries.
- *quadruplum* — the fifth voice of a great motet.
- *talea* — the repeating rhythmic shape (paired with *color*).
- *tempora* — the counted time-units of a note.
- *tenor* — the chant melody that "holds" the composition.
- *triplum* — the third voice, above the motet voice.
- *vacua / plena* — "void" (outline) and "full" (filled-in) notes.
