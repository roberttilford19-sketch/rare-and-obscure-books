DEDICATION

**Who he was.** Gratian was a monk of Bologna, by tradition of the Camaldolese order, at work around the year 1140. Of his person almost nothing is documented — no certain dates, no recorded career [UNVERIFIED: even the Camaldolese affiliation rests on tradition, and the old story that he became bishop of Chiusi is unverified]. What survives is the work: the *Concordia discordantium canonum*, the Harmony of Clashing Canons, called after him the Decretum.

**His circumstances and death.** Nothing certain is known of his life or his death. He is one of history's great anonymous workmen: the man vanished, and the textbook remained — to govern Western Christendom for seven centuries.

**Why his work is vital — his logic proved, not praised.** Before Gratian, church law was a heap of canons that contradicted one another, with no method for choosing between them. Gratian drew some 3,800 texts into a single work, and his invention was procedural, and it is proved by seven hundred years of use: state a case, raise the questions it poses, set the authorities against each other at full strength, then resolve by distinction — this text is the earlier, that the later; this speaks generally, that to a special case. That method became the engine of the medieval university. On war he does not thunder; he reasons, article by article. Here he first gives the Gospel objections their full force — turn the other cheek, Peter's sword, vengeance reserved to God — and only then sets out the authorities, chiefly Augustine, and the distinction that decides: the precepts of patience bind the preparation of the heart, not the outward show of the body.

**His relation to this rescue.** The Boneyard rescues unfinished or untranslated works of the dead, and Gratian's Decretum has waited nearly nine centuries for a complete English rendering; the modern English volumes give slices only — Thompson (1993) the first twenty Distinctions, and Reichberg, Syse & Begby (2006) selections from this very Causa 23 — different slices altogether. This question, the medieval Church's charter on war, is rescued here as the medieval Christian counterpart to a Quran-alone just-war ethic — stated as the editor's framing, not as Gratian's claim: war only by right authority, only from necessity, never from greed or cruelty, with mercy owed to the conquered and the soldier's conscience sheltered by lawful obedience.

**Where a reader goes next.** Friedberg's edition (the source of this rendering); Augustine's Letter 189 to Boniface and *Against Faustus* book 22 (the deep sources of the canons); and the later questions of this same Causa 23 — what makes a war just, whether wrongs to allies may be repelled by arms — not translated here.

— *The Editor*

# WHETHER MILITARY SERVICE IS A SIN

*Gratian's Decretum, Second Part — Causa 23, Question 1*

## THE CASE AND THE QUESTIONS

[Here] the first question is asked: whether military service is a sin? (Qu. I.) Second, what a just war is, and how just wars were waged by the children of Israel? (Qu. III.) Third, whether injury to allies should be driven off by arms? (Qu. IV.) Fourth, whether vengeance should be inflicted? (Qu. V.) Fifth, whether it is a sin for a judge or his officer to put criminals to death? (Qu. VI.) Sixth, whether the wicked should be compelled to the good? (Qu. VII.) Seventh, whether heretics should be despoiled of their own and the Church's goods, and whether one who holds what was taken from heretics is said to hold another's property? (Qu. VIII.) Eighth, whether bishops or any clerics may on their own authority — or by command of the Apostolic See or of the emperor — take up arms?

[The case: certain bishops with the people entrusted to them lapsed into heresy, and began to compel the neighboring Catholics by threats and torments toward heresy. When this was learned, the Apostolic See commanded the Catholic bishops of the surrounding regions — who had received civil jurisdiction from the emperor — to defend the Catholics from the heretics, and to compel them by whatever means they could to return to the truth of the faith. The bishops, receiving these apostolic mandates, began to fight the heretics openly and by ambush with collected soldiers. At last, some of them put to death, others despoiled of their own or the Church's goods, others shut in prison and workhouse, they were compelled and returned to the unity of the Catholic faith.]

## QUESTION I

**GRATIAN.**

That military service seems foreign to the Gospel discipline may be proved thus: for all soldiering is instituted either to drive off injury or to inflict vengeance — and the repelling of injury, whether from one's own person or from an ally, is in either case forbidden by the Gospel law. For when it is said: "If anyone strikes you on one cheek, offer him the other," and again: "Whoever forces you to go a thousand paces, go with him two thousand"; likewise when the Apostle says to the Romans: "Do not defend yourselves, beloved, but give place to wrath" — what else are we forbidden but to repel injury done to our own person?

§ 1. Likewise, when Christ said to Peter, defending his master with the sword: "Put your sword back in its sheath; do you think I cannot ask my Father, and he will give me more than twelve legions of angels?" — and finally, as is read of blessed Andrew: when a crowd gathered to snatch him from the hands of an unjust judge and defend him from the injury of death, he on the contrary, teaching by example and word that suffering must be borne, begged them not to hinder his martyrdom — what else are we taught but to bear patiently the injuries of our allies as our own, and not to run to arms, but by their example to prepare our minds to bear like things?

§ 2. Likewise, when it is said in Proverbs: "Vengeance is mine, and I will repay, says the Lord" [Friedberg's Correctores note: these words are not found in Proverbs; they are Deuteronomy 32:35, the text Paul quotes at Romans 12:19.]; likewise, when it is said in the Gospel: "Judge not, and you shall not be judged"; likewise, when the householder says to the servants who wanted to gather the weeds: "Let both grow until the harvest, and then I will say to the reapers: gather the weeds and bind them in bundles to burn"; § 3. likewise, when that king who made a marriage feast for his son is said, having sent his armies — that is, the angels — to be about to destroy the murderers of the prophets and apostles who, invited to the wedding, scorned to come in belief; likewise, when Paul the Apostle says in the epistle to the Romans: "Judge not one another"; likewise: "Who are you to judge another's servant? To his own master he stands or falls. Whether we live or die, we are the Lord's. For to this end Christ died and rose, that he might be Lord of both the living and the dead" — in all these, what else is commanded but that vengeance on wrongdoers be reserved for the divine judgment? Since, then, as said above, soldiering seems instituted either to drive off injury or to inflict vengeance, and both are forbidden by the Gospel law, it appears that military service is a sin.

Here too Gregory [says]: [Friedberg's apparatus corrects the inscription: the passage is Origen's, from his homilies on Joshua (hom. 16).]

### C. 1. Earthly wars bear the figure of spiritual wars.

"If these earthly wars did not bear the figure of spiritual wars, the books of Jewish history would never, I think, have been handed down by the Apostles to be read in the churches to the disciples of Christ, who came to teach peace. For how would this description of wars profit those to whom Jesus says: 'My peace I give you, my peace I leave you?' and to whom it is commanded and said through the Apostle: 'Do not avenge yourselves, but rather suffer injury, and rather suffer fraud?' Whence at last the Apostle, knowing that we have no more earthly wars to wage, but that the soul's battles against spiritual adversaries must be fought with sweat, as a master of soldiering gives a command to the soldiers of Christ, saying: 'Put on the armor of God, that you may stand against the wiles of the devil.'"

**GRATIAN.** To these things it is answered thus: the precepts of patience are to be kept not so much in outward show of the body as in preparation of the heart.

Hence Augustine says in the sermon on the centurion's servant: [Friedberg's apparatus: the passage is Augustine's Letter 138, § 12, not a sermon on the centurion's servant.]

### C. 2. The precepts of patience are to be kept by strength of mind, not by outward show of the body.

"The just and godly man ought to be ready to bear patiently the malice of those whom he desires to become good, so that the number of the good may rather grow — not that he add himself by like malice to the number of the wicked. In short, these precepts belong more to the preparation of the heart than to the work done openly: that patience with goodwill be kept in the soul's secret, while openly is done what seems to profit those to whom we ought to wish well.

§ 1. Hence it is clearly shown that the Lord Jesus himself, the singular example of patience, when struck in the face, answered: 'If I spoke evil, bear witness of the evil; but if well, why do you strike me?' He did not at all fulfill his own precept, if we look at the words. For he did not offer the other cheek to the striker, but rather forbade him to do and increase the injury. And yet he had come prepared not only to be struck in the face, but even to be crucified and slain for those very men by whom he suffered these things — for whom he said, hanging on the cross: 'Father, forgive them, for they know not what they do.'

§ 2. Nor does Paul the Apostle seem to have fulfilled the precept of his Lord and master, when, struck in the face, he said to the high priest: 'God shall strike you, you whitewashed wall; do you sit judging me according to the law, and against the law command me to be struck?' And when the bystanders said, 'Do you insult the high priest?' — mocking them, he wished to warn them what he had said, so that the wise might understand that at Christ's coming the whitewashed wall was now to be destroyed — that is, the hypocrisy of the Jewish priesthood. For he said, 'I did not know, brothers, that he is the high priest; for it is written: You shall not curse the prince of your people' — though one brought up among that same people and trained in the law could not have failed to know that high priest, nor could those to whom he was so well known have been in any way deceived as to his not knowing. These precepts of patience, then, are always to be kept in the heart's preparation — goodwill itself, too, that evil be not returned for evil, always to be fulfilled in the will.

§ 3. But many things must also be done, even with the unwilling, with a certain benign severity, for punishment — consulting their profit rather than their will. For in correcting a son, however harshly, a father's love is never lost. Let what he does not want be done, and let him grieve who, even unwilling, seems to need healing by grief.

§ 4. And hence, if this earthly commonwealth keeps the Christian precepts, even wars themselves will not be waged without goodwill — but so that the conquered may more easily be cared for into the fellowship of piety and justice. For he whose license for wrongdoing is taken away is conquered to his profit, since nothing is more unhappy than the happiness of sinners, by which penal impunity is nourished and the evil will, like an inward enemy, is strengthened.

§ 5. And a little after: For if Christian discipline condemned all wars, this counsel would rather have been given in the Gospel to those seeking the counsel of salvation: to throw away their arms and withdraw themselves wholly from soldiering. But what was said to them was: 'Do violence to no one; be content with your wages.' And he who commanded that their own wage should suffice them did not prohibit soldiering."

### C. 3. In the business of arms many can please God.

Likewise the same [Augustine] to Boniface, [Letter 189]: [The inscription numbers this letter 207; the letter to Boniface is Letter 189, as the dedication states. The 207 numbering stands unexplained.]

"Do not think that no one who serves in the business of war can please God. Among these was holy David, to whom the Lord gave so great a testimony. Among these too were many just men of that time. Among these was that centurion who said to the Lord: 'I am not worthy that you should enter under my roof.'

§ 1. This, then, think first when you arm for battle: that your strength, even bodily strength, is God's gift. For so you will think not to use God's gift against the Lord. For faith, when promised, must be kept even to the enemy against whom the war is waged — how much more to the friend for whom it is fought? The will ought to have peace; war ought to be of necessity — that God may free us from necessity and keep us in peace. For peace is not sought that war may be waged, but war is waged that peace may be won. Be, then, peacemaking even in warring, that those whom you attack you may lead by conquering to the profit of peace. 'Blessed are the peacemakers,' says the Lord, 'for they shall be called children of God.' And if human peace is so sweet for the temporal safety of mortals, how much sweeter is divine peace for the eternal safety of angels? Let necessity, then, crush the fighting enemy, not the will. As violence is returned to the one who wars and resists, so mercy is now owed to the conquered captive — especially when no disturbance of peace is feared from him."

Likewise the same [Augustine] against the Manichees, that is, in Book 22 against Faustus, chapters 74 and 75:

### C. 4. What things are rightly blamed in war.

"What is blamed in war? That men, who will die sometime, die — that those who will live in peace may be tamed? To have blamed this is the mark of the timid, not of the religious. The lust of harming, the cruelty of vengeance, an unpeaceful and implacable mind, the savagery of revolt, the lust of domination, and the like — these are the things rightly blamed in wars. And it is often in order that these things may also be justly punished that wars themselves are undertaken by the good against the violence of those who resist — whether God or some lawful authority commands it — when they are found in that order of human affairs where the order itself justly constrains them either to command such things or to obey in such matters. Neither does John bid soldiers to leave arms, and Christ warns that tribute be rendered to Caesar, because pay must of necessity be furnished to the soldier on account of wars. Hence that natural order of mortals, fitted to peace, requires this: that the authority and counsel for undertaking war be in the hands of princes.

§ 1. But if war is waged from human greed, it does not harm the saints. No one has power over the saints except what is given from above. For there is no power but from God, whether commanding or permitting. Therefore the just man, if he should serve as a soldier even under a sacrilegious king, may rightly wage war at his command — if, keeping the order of civic peace, it is certain that what is commanded him is either not against God's precept, or it is not certain whether it is — so that the iniquity of commanding may perhaps make the king guilty, while the order of serving shows the soldier innocent."

### C. 5. Military service is not a sin.

Likewise the same [Augustine] in the book on the Lord's words, treatise [or sermon] 19: [Friedberg's apparatus, following the Maurists: this sermon is not Augustine's; it stands in the appendix of sermons (Sermo 82 in Appd.).]

"To serve as a soldier is not a crime, but to serve for plunder is a sin; nor is it criminal to administer the commonwealth, but to administer the commonwealth in order to increase riches seems damnable. For by a certain providence pay has been established for soldiers, lest, while a living is sought, plundering run riot. Likewise:

§ 1. The Lord himself said: 'Render to God the things that are God's, and to Caesar the things that are Caesar's.' What Caesar commands, then, must be borne; what he enjoins must be endured — but it becomes unendurable when the exactors heap up plunder. Likewise:

§ 2. 'The soldiers asked John, saying: What shall we also do? He said to them: Do violence to no one, bring no false accusation, but be content with your wages.' So ought every man who serves as a soldier to know himself. For Scripture speaks not only of those soldiers who are held in armed service, but whoever uses the belt of his own soldiering is enrolled as a soldier of his dignity — and so this sentence may be said (for example) to soldiers, to guards, and to all governors. Whoever obtains the pay publicly decreed for him, if he seeks more, is condemned by John's sentence as a false accuser and extortioner."

### C. 6. Those wars are peacemaking which are waged that the wicked may be restrained and the good upheld.

Likewise the same [Augustine] on the diverse observances of the Church: [Friedberg's apparatus: no book of Augustine "on the diverse observances of the Church" exists.]

"Among the true worshippers of God even wars themselves are peacemaking — those waged not from greed or cruelty, but from zeal for peace, that the wicked may be restrained and the good upheld."

### C. 7. The highest praise of soldiering is to show obedience to the interests of the commonwealth.

Likewise Gregory to all the soldiers at Naples [Book 12, Letter 24]:

"The highest praise of soldiering, among other good merits, is this: to show obedience to the interests of the commonwealth — to obey whatever is usefully commanded — as we have learned your devotion has now done, which obeyed our letters by which we appointed the distinguished man Constantius the tribune to preside over the guard of the city, and showed the fitting obedience of military devotion. Wherefore by the present writing we have taken care to admonish you that to the aforesaid distinguished tribune, as you have done, you ought to show all obedience for the profit of our most serene lords and for keeping the city — so that whatever has been well done by you hitherto may be increased by present vigilance and care."

**GRATIAN.** From all these things it is gathered that military service is not a sin, and that the precepts of patience are to be kept in preparation of the heart, not in outward show of the body.
