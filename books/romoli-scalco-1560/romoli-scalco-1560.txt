HISTORICAL TEXT — NOT CULINARY OR MEDICAL ADVICE. This is a
sixteenth-century steward's manual, rendered for reading. It is not
a cookbook to cook from nor a guide to remedies; Book I contains no
recipes, quantities, doses, or remedies at all.

DEDICATION — to Domenico Romoli, called Panonto

Who he was. Domenico Romoli was a Florentine gentleman of the
sixteenth century who made his life in the kitchens and courts of
Renaissance Italy. A modern food-history site quotes a contemporary
description of him as "a Florentine gentleman, well-versed in the
art of cooking as well as court, with a profound knowledge of the
classic and modern authors" (coquinaria.nl, "A recipe for crostini
from the sixteenth century").
He published under his kitchen nickname — Panonto, "oiled bread"
(bread rubbed with fat) — and he was proud enough of it to set it
on the title page of his book for all the world to read.

His circumstances and death. Almost nothing is documented about
Romoli's life beyond what the book itself tells. His dates of birth
and death are not recorded — UNVERIFIED, and this edition asserts
nothing about them. He flourished by 1560, the year his book first
appeared in Venice. A kitchen man's nickname became his immortal
byline, and then he walked off the page of history.

Why his work is vital — proved, not praised. The book kept selling
for generations: this rescue is made from the Venice printing of
1637, seventy-seven years after the first edition — proof that
readers kept it alive. The food historian Katherine Golden Bitting
judged it "one of the most famous Italian 16th-century cookery
books." It is not a mere recipe list but a complete working manual
for a princely household: how the steward runs his office, the
duties of every officer under him, the true season of every meat
and every fish, the service of the daily table and the wedding
feast. (The seasons of meats and fishes, and the menus laid out
for a whole year, belong to the later books of the same volume —
not to this First Book, which is the steward's treatise alone.)
It is a court kitchen on paper, written by a man who worked in one.

His place in this rescue. The Boneyard shelves the workers, not
the princes. Romoli was not a lord writing about banquets from
above; he was the man who staged them, and he wrote down how it
is done so another young man could learn. That is why he belongs
here: the craft remembered, the craftsman honored.

Where the reader goes next. The full 1637 text stands free on the
Internet Archive (scan identifier bub_gb_vZo37jO2HdQC). For the
book's standing among early cookery books, see Bitting,
Gastronomic Bibliography, p. 403. Reenactors have published
redactions of individual Romoli recipes online — useful, but no
complete English of this treatise was found.

AMMAESTRAMENTO DEL FAMOSO SCALCO DETTO IL PANONTO
Instruction of the Famous Steward Called Panonto
(Libro Primo — the First Book)
================================================================

TO MASTER FRANCESCO RODOLFI

It seems to me a reasonable thing, Master Francesco, that when one
friend lovingly asks another for some service which may be granted,
it should never be denied him. And since you have begged me to show
and teach you the business belonging to the office of a private
steward [scalco secreto] to most noble lords and most reverend
cardinals — it seeming to you that, by the long experience I have
of it, I may satisfy you — if this instruction of mine should not
reach the perfection of what might perhaps be said in this
profession, it will at least serve to stir the wits of others to
discourse of it in such manner as the matter of itself, being a
cheerful one, calls for, and as I shall best know how. I set myself
willingly to do it, for since you are a young nobleman of good wit,
it will be small labor for me to teach you, and [small labor] for
you to learn. Now, just as, setting yourself to love a beautiful
and graceful lady, you would so act as to come to the perfection of
your love, so — setting that aside for the moment, and giving
yourself with delight to this office — you will turn out most
admirable, for delight is what makes the work perfect.

CHAPTER I. How the office [of private steward] to whatever prince
you please is to be exercised.

The first lesson I would give you is this: that the officers you
shall choose under your office of steward be treated by you
humanely, and not with pride, so that they love and honor you and
rest content with you; they are to be kept by you in due esteem,
and at times you are to gather with them — yet always holding firm
the dignity of your office.

It is no small matter to make a good choice of the officers under
this office, for if they are able and faithful, the steward will be
reputed prudent, and besides they will do him honor in their
exercise of it; and if he choose them otherwise, no good judgment
can be made of him, and he commits an error fit to destroy all his
competence. Never set among those officers, my Francesco, to
please any person whatsoever — even, though I will not say by
whose hand, of your most reverend master's own [household] — any
man of whose record you have not first had good information; but
once you have found them obedient and faithful, you must know how
to acknowledge them, by doing them good and cherishing them.

When a steward thinks more of himself than of his master, seeking
his own private profit in everything, he will never be good; for
having his lord's life in his hands, he must never think of
himself, but of the duty and the weight of his office. And for that
cause his lord ought to keep him good and trusted — honoring him
and making him rich — so that he shall not be put in mind to seek
other riches or honors.

You must see that your officers are at least practiced and
competent — above all, since you are new to it — namely the cook, the credenza officer, and the buyer;
and since you must command them, you need, with their help and my
example, to learn, and to carry out all their business excellently.
Once you have found them, see that in commanding them you bear yourself toward them so that your
commanding seems rather entreaty than command — without injurious
words or words that offend, and without affectation, importunity,
or bravado, but with modesty. Do not imitate in this certain
stewards, whom I could well name, who think to show they know how
to do everything by shouting; for men so browbeaten by you will do
far less. Be ever obedient to your betters, modest with your
equals, and pleasant with your inferiors. And that your officers
may serve and obey with better heart, being commanded by you — who
come to be a father and a master to them — you must not fail them
in their lawful requests, but be their advocate with your common
lord; for you know above all how pleasing in God's sight is the
office of charity: favor them by placing them [well] and bringing
now one, now another, into his presence, pleading that they be well
treated and benefited, and that they be given good counsel and good
words with better deeds. So you will come to serve, at one and the
same time, God, your master, and your officers; and you will come
to be loved not only by them but by the household and those
outside; and you will be the cause that good officers become
better when they see themselves benefited and honored — besides
which another good will follow: that they, rising in time to the
rank of stewards, will by your good example do the same good works
toward others. See that you give heed and are diligent in looking
over all the things that shall serve for your master's table; and
above all else order that the kitchen, the credenza, and the secret
storeroom be kept as a miser keeps his treasure; and let no one set
foot there until you have served your lord morning and evening —
save your own ministers, helpers, and lads.

I have shown you in this discourse how you are to govern and keep
not only your officers but all the rest of your lord's household.
Now I must show you how and by what way each of the said officers
is to serve in his office; likewise at what time and in what
manner the gentlemen of the house, the chamberlains, cupbearers,
carver, squires, and pages are to serve and obey you, when by your
lord's order it falls to you to serve a noble banquet. But first I
must tell you how you are to govern yourself in your honorable and
befitting dress. You must be courtesy itself, with hands ever white
and delicate, without scab or any kind of itch — since you must
often handle the dishes for your lord's own mouth in making the
wonted tastings [credenze]. And to all the officers — even though
those tastings be ceremonies — never fail to use them in every
smallest thing, toward everyone, though he were your steward of
the household or your own blood brother; and the more so with
gifts, of which you may take all such things as seem fit for the
service of your master's table, consigning them to your secret
dispenser, who shall keep them in safe custody. But to return to
our discourse: I say that although you are not, nor would be, a
churchman, you must — serving cardinals, as you now do — wear long
garments, if not wholly then at least in part, of the finest cloths
in summer and in winter, and above all when you serve your most
reverend [master], and with a priest's cap; and let these garments
be neither colored, nor trimmed, nor embroidered — lest you come to
imitate Rosso the buffoon, or Pope Ape, or some other steward I
have seen, who, serving such great prelates, go about dressed in
certain threadbare doublets that scarce cover half their backsides,
or sling a cloak across the shoulder with a little feather in a
threadbare velvet cap. And for this I would dare say that no one
ought to set himself to this honorable office, young or old, who
has not the means to maintain himself in that degree — unless his
most reverend master give him the means by some way. In this, mark
well that avarice bring you no disgrace. But let us return to the
order of our officers.

CHAPTER II. Of the Buyer [spenditore].

You must know that, as has been told you, a great part of your own
honor comes to you from having that officer able in his office; and
if I would know precisely whence his competence proceeds, it would
be too great a trouble — but touching the substantial points, I say
that in my judgment he must be born and bred a refined gourmand,
and not a pig — for with that keen gourmand's polish he will come
to buy all his goods well. Being a novice, he must be pleasant, courteous, generous,
faithful, and diligent, seeking to gain practice with some of those
old and practiced buyers, and going with them up and down the
squares, markets, and shops; and with his good wit he will be able
to learn. Those too will make him competent: the collaroli, the
pork-butchers, the butchers, the market-gardeners, and the
fruiterers — who, from their eagerness to serve the courts of
nobles, will not fail to serve most devotedly, jealous only of not
losing that court.

Once he is, as it were, graduated, I would not have him do as some
of them have done and do — many shames in stealing; for such men
are thieves by nature, and avarice, from handling money, strangles
them as a rope would damn them were they hanged by the throat.
They reck not of the gluttonies and murders [of reputation] they
commit; they want, even from those of whom they buy, a gratuity, as
if they were Auditors of the Rota. I say nothing of the quibbles
they make over the ordinary of their spending, nor of the exchange
of gold for small coin. But the time of their greatest thievery
will be when you have to order a banquet or a great feast; for if
at ordinary meals they steal by tens, at such times they steal by
hundreds. I do not say this in general, for there are good ones
among them, lovers of their own honor and of their souls. You will
see such men — I speak of the wretches — when with petty thefts
they have heaped and put together a hundred or two hundred crowns,
straightway make common cause with some of the said tradesmen, or take friendship with some one of those crafty old women,
and furnish her with hens, capons, pullets, pigeons, geese, and
rabbits; nor will they lack, in season, cages or little rooms full
of turtledoves and other small birds; and in that way the goods get
paid for twice over. Marvel not if in this I rail rather too much,
for many such murders are done; and I would not have it befall you
to have such buyers — so open your eyes and your ears.

As for the goods brought home — I mean those bought for your lord's
own mouth — see that they are brought home in those clean,
lidded baskets, and let their little keys never be wanting,
though rogues or scullions carry them, as oftentimes happens. I
would have some lad of the house go with that buyer to carry the
goods home.

The buyer should be young, clean, and practiced, able to read and
write; nor let him lack a good provision, since he does well-nigh
all the business. See that this buyer keeps your storeroom
furnished at all times with variety, and that meats and poultry are
kept there tender and not tainted; and when you see that some thing
cannot last, keep your eye on it together with the dispenser, that
it be not left to spoil, but given to the common storeroom for the
household to enjoy. And when you have found a competent buyer —
for that is the first requirement in a court — bear with him if he
rubs you, so long as he does not bite.

CHAPTER III. Of the Private Cook [cuoco secreto].

I go sometimes considering with myself of what weight a secret
officer of those most noble princes is, since with so many perils
they put their lives into his hands. Among the chief ones the cook
is one; and it frightened me when I exercised this office which
you desire to exercise, and now I thank God most highly that He
let me come out of it with honor. Truly it is one of the most
troublesome offices that can be thought of; you must stand
continually with open eyes, if you would do your duty and no
mishap befall you. Think ever on the danger you are in; and to do
well, you must arm yourself with a breastplate compounded of
charity, faith, and diligence.

Now to return to our discourse: I say that after you have found
the buyer, you must provide yourself with a good and able cook,
who must be like an old physician grown old in his art. Above all, have no
dealings with drunkards — though they were the most excellent
masters of cookery in the world — for, having to serve you in
cooking the victuals, they would sooner cook themselves than the
first courses. I give you this warning because it has oftentimes
happened to me; beware of it then.

The business of a kitchen is noble, great, toilsome, and wearying;
and so, an old man not being able to endure it long, he should be
given a willing young man, [chosen] by him or by others his peers,
for under-cook; the helpers less practiced, and few lads; and with
your instruction you will keep them thus clean and neat. Furnish
them liberally with tablecloths, aprons, and dishcloths. Have them
keep their beards ever shaven, and likewise their hair; let them
have their botta aprons, their white shirts, with dress short,
clean, and close; see they be not taken full of sores or bad legs;
let them be pleasant, obedient, humane, cheerful — Italians if it
may be, and not northerners.

And when their dishes are in order and you would make a beginning
of serving, have them lay out their clean little table where the
dishes shall be set forth, furnished with all needful things that
you require morning and evening — such as lemons, bitter oranges,
cheeses, and sugar, with a little casket of secret spices, and his
clean knives, and your napkin with clean bread, laid out for the
wonted tastings of the soups and sauces, [and] a middle-sized iron
fork.

Above all you must keep that cook cheerful — but not with the
cheer that comes of too much drinking, as I have told you. I need
not now say more of the business of the rest of the officers, but
rather show you how you are to command them and order their
business and your own.

After you have supped at night, have your cook and your buyer come
to your chamber, and together you will gather [the list of] those
dishes that must serve for the morning; and so the buyer will
betimes carry them to the storeroom. And since he will perhaps not
have found all the things he had in commission, you must rise at a
very good hour, and leaving every other business, go see what he
has brought, order your dishes anew, and send to seek what is
lacking.

When you have finished the service of the master's dinner, you will
return to the kitchen, ordering supper; for many times in the
morning some little things or choice morsels are left over, which,
served cold, can do for supper. I would have you use diligence,
together with your cook, to come to know your lord's taste
entirely; for as one sees, some like no spices, or sauces, or sweet
things, or sharp things. Likewise you must know the appointed hour
of his wonted eating, morning and evening — though the appetites
of lords are hard to know, for they are mostly fancies — but you
must use what devices you can; for in this examination a man must
first be a diviner and then a steward. And since those hours of
theirs cannot be observed, no marvel if they are ill served; for
the dishes and first courses dry up, the sauces and soups spoil,
the boiled meats cool, and the roasts drip in their warming-pans.
But I tell you plainly,
if the appointed hour be known, nothing shall be missed. You must
use diligence with the small first courses, which want little
cooking — while you have the table laid, they will be done; the
great roasts will wait upon the spits till the serving; and so
neither your honor shall fail, nor shall your master lack the good
savor of all your dishes, when they are well seasoned by that able
cook. Nor will I say more of this, but come to the business of the
Credenza Officer.

CHAPTER IV. Of the Credenza Officer [credenziere].

Having somewhat sketched my instruction concerning these two
officers we have [treated], it remains to say that you must have a
diligent and practiced credenza officer — for he, without fail,
will much ennoble your office, if he be able and good. I marvel
exceedingly how nowadays the competence and grace of these credenza
officers has been so refined; in my time they were not at that
perfection and gallantry which is now the fashion — with the
mastery of so many salads worked and dressed in so many various
ways, with the admirable folding of tablecloths and napkins,
shaping them into whatever forms they please. And particularly I
would speak of our credenza officer Nicolò Molino, who — not to
blame the others — has not in Rome a more competent man.

Now I say that when in the morning you have visited the kitchen,
you must visit the credenza straightway, ordering all the things
that must serve for dinner — such as fruits and other like things —
urging him to be ready in laying out at the hour given him, in
order, and to have his public credenza in readiness, and to
furnish himself with all the things you shall have ordered him;
nor can it hurt to have him lay the credenza a little before it is
due, gladdening the sight of the gluttons and the daily diners
at the master's tables, and of the many strangers who for their
business come and go. And when your master's pleasure shall be made
known to you, you will straightway have the table laid, decked, and
furnished with all needful things; and so laid and decked it shall
stand, showing how it is to be served another time, following our
order.

CHAPTER V. Of the Bottler [bottigliere].

I would not be blamed for wanting to show you how the bottler is
to serve you, his office depending on the cupbearer — to say things
so known to everyone; yet I will tell you that when by your order
some banquet shall be served at your lord's instance, then you
shall command and order him the array of the wines and their sorts:
red, white, sweet, and sharp — letting him know which he is to
serve first and after. First, when melon or salad is served, let it
be a Greco or a white Salerno; in winter, malmsey, muscatel, or
vernaccia; with the antipasti and the boiled meats, small white
wines; with the roasts, red and biting wines; with the fruits, hippocras,
Magnaguerra, or red sweet Salerno.

Of all this the bottler must serve you. Nevertheless, while the
credenza officer lays and decks his credenza, have him lay and
deck his own clean little table of the bottle-office, with its
little white cloth covered with flowers and greens, making a show
of all his clean little beakers and flasks and other vessels of
crystal and of silver, awaiting the hour of his service. Of the
cup, the beaker, and the other vessels that serve for your master's
own mouth, let no show be made until he is set at table.

I am forced to step somewhat out of my course. If you remember, I
told you that if you would know how to command, you must know how
to do; and just as a cupbearer and a bottler must needs have taste,
savor, and smell — and that they be drinkers and not guzzlers —
you will know to use above all good diligence in this, so that you
may know all the faults a wine may have, and which wine will most
please your master. And here I
could tell you how and in what manner the cupbearer, by your
instruction, shall well serve his master — but I reserve it to tell
another time.

CHAPTER VI. Of the Dispenser [dispensiere].

It is become the custom in these courts — even of rich and great
lords — that one dispenser furnish and have care both of the public
storeroom and of the secret one, yea make them all one; which I
neither blame nor praise, but I say it would be an honest thing
that one man alone were he who keeps and handles only the things
that must serve for your lord's own mouth. But since it cannot be
done my way, let it at least be sought that, since there is but the
one dispenser, there be in that common storeroom a little cool and
not damp closet, white and airy, fitted with cupboards, little benches, racks, and tables,
as an able and good dispenser will know how to fit it. And in it
let all the secret things be kept; and he who has such charge must
be most trusty, pleasant, discreet, and diligent — for it is wont
to happen that while the steward serves, some unthought-of thing
is lacking for the kitchen or the credenza; and the credenza
officer having none to leave in his stead, it follows that, the
credenza being locked at that time, the door of it must be broken
— as has happened to me.

It does not please me that the dispenser go to carry to kitchen or
credenza the stuff the cook or the credenza officer needs, but
rather that they go to fetch it in the storeroom.

When your master is presented [with a gift of victuals], you will
diligently, together with your steward of the household, have kept
in that secret storeroom all of the present that can serve for that
lord's table.

CHAPTER VII. Of the Bread-Keeper [panattiere].

This office — whether of the secret or the open bread-keepers —
will give no trouble, for its few businesses. But I tell you that
when the pantry has no small storeroom, order for him a very good
cupboard with its little keys; and in it, by your order, let there
be kept not only the master's ordinary bread, but at all times many
little appetizing things — such as little cakes of elderflowers
with butter and sugar, with pepper and salt; olives, figs, raisins,
walnuts, saffron, fennel, pepper, ring-cakes, biscuits, little
arm-breads [bracciatelli] with anise, biscuits of barley meal,
little rings with eggs and sugar, little sugar-cakes, and
berlingozzi; and every day have the service of the bread
accompanied with these things according to the month and the
season. And I remind you that, although these things are of small
moment, they please greatly those without appetite.

CHAPTER VIII. Of the Overseer of the Dishes [soprastante del piatto].

It would not occur to me to want to show you the business
belonging to the overseer of the dishes — doing no injury to the
office of the steward of the household — for whenever the dishes
have been lifted from the master's table and are in his hand, you
have no more to do with them. And with forbearance, when you have
to serve a noble banquet by your master's commission, out of
regard for the foreign servants whom you must honor, it will often
fall to you to have an eye to everything — ordering that overseer
that, the first dishes being lifted, all the things that are least
touched and most whole he send straightway to the kitchen to be
kept hot, or to some other place convenient to you until you call
for them; and that they go not into the mouths of the rossi, and
that to please anyone he send no presents, nor touch anything
without your leave.

We are to sketch the laying and the service of a noble banquet in
the French style for the morning. Herein I will show you how you
are to govern yourself, having to serve twelve honored lords or
cardinals. You must use herein the greatest diligence, nor fail in
any point of what is shown you; and because in this act great
effort must be used, I might leave many things behind — so where I
shall fail, I will beg those lord private stewards more practiced
than I to add it here.

Before we give beginning to the banquet, we must see where and how
the table of the guests is to be set. Regard must first be had that
it be roomy for all, and that the dishes have not to incommode
you; and without further trouble, you will take the fashion of the
public table of your common master — and where it shall be laid,
let them take a basin and a ewer apiece, with perfumed water — and
since these great personages ever bring with them their
cupbearers, bottlers, and certain other gentlemen. When you would serve the
service of water to the hands, those clean squires of yours shall
present to their cupbearers a napkin apiece, using the ceremony of
the tasting [credenza]. And at this point your person must show
itself well dressed and clean; and you shall present yourself, with
your little wand in hand, before your most reverend master, to let
him know the hour of going to table. When these most reverend
guests shall have come forth to go to eat, let these graceful
gentlemen appear with their basins held high, and the tasting of
the water being made, they shall give water to the hands — two
guests washing in one basin — and their cupbearers shall serve
them with a napkin apiece.

At this point two comely carvers shall appear; and setting
themselves, one at the head and the other at the foot of the table,
they — once the guests are seated — shall at one and the same time
lift those cloths which we said serve for the cover of the table,
showing in a trice that noble order of those public little dishes,
keeping to the same places. There the carvers shall do their
service of carving, though it be but little — for they shall
dismember or carve some great or gross hen, some boiled meat or small roast; and you shall order
twelve of your gentlemen, that each do the service of the chairs.
And once they are set at table, that clean credenza officer shall
have dressed those delicate little salads; and with your gentlemen,
with a napkin apiece, you shall serve them; and with the same men
you shall go to the kitchen to serve your first dishes, finding
them set out and laid; and of each of the three plates you shall
have the cook make good tasting [credenza].

And not to entangle yourself: when the first plate with the eight
dishes is upon the gentlemen's arms, they shall come out of the
kitchen; the like in the second service and the third; you shall go
merrily to carry them to table; and before your most reverend
master you shall make sign of reverence. And those six trained
squires shall straightway lift the first dishes from the table, and
the three practiced gentlemen shall set the others upon it, each of
them before his four appointed guests; and these being set, with
the same men you shall go and serve those of the credenza.

But before I tell you more, I mean to give you a good reminder.
You must use a mean in these banquets: neither hurry them too much
in furnishing them, nor yet tarry so long that you keep the guests
ill at ease. But indeed such noble banquets as these should be
served rather more slowly than with too much haste, if they are to
be reputed well served; for in the first and second dishes the
guests satisfy themselves, and in all the rest they dally; and
where there are no entertainments of fair ladies, you may hurry
more — but where there are such entertainments, it matters not too
much. And when you would entertain them through the fennel [the
fennel course closed the meal], those clean squires shall lift all
the remainder of the fruits; four of your gentlemen, with a round
dish apiece, shall lift the four little salt-cellars; twelve others
shall lift the service of the bread; and the two carvers shall lift
the first tablecloth. And upon the other, water shall be given to
the hands in the order aforesaid; and washed and dried, the other
cloth shall be lifted by the same carvers. Then you may serve the
service of the toothpicks, with all those noble little things which
are shown you at the end of these March banquets, upon the carpet.
That done, you must use very great diligence to honor the servants
of the guests, as is shown you here below.

CHAPTER IX. The steward's diligence toward the servants of the
guests.

It has been small labor for you to have brought our honorable
banquet to a good end, in respect of what you must do to honor the
gentlemen of the guests and the other servants, if you would earn
the name of a competent steward — having to keep those means
which honor your lord — whereby he, through your competence, may
bring you to the end you have set before you. And likewise see
that you fail not to furnish liberally the grooms' table — for your
honor and your master's — with all the things that may let them
triumph honorably with their foreign comrades in their chambers.
You shall give order to one of your
practiced squires that, as their steward, he have care of this,
giving him certain other well-mannered servants and pages, showing
him where and how they may be lodged honorably; and put them not in
the public servants' halls this time, but rather they may well be
put in a secret, honored hall, where the credenza officer must not
fail to furnish them with tablecloths and napkins in various folds,
with salads and fruits and other needful things, and with plate
enough to suffice; nor let the bottle-office be wanting, with most
beautiful beakers, little flasks, and very good wines in abundance.
And the table must be furnished with those first little dishes in
the manner kept at the first [table]; and you shall order the cook
to provide it with certain extraordinary things — such as tarts,
pies, crostate — and let the other things go whole and hot to
table.

Now this table being in order, you — the banquet being finished —
shall go, together with the secret chamberlain, the cupbearer and
the carver, and six of the noblest and best-placed gentlemen of
your most reverend master, showing your humanity and courtesy,
without disrepute, in inviting the servants of the guests, showing them how dear it is to you to have to serve
them. Come to the place laid out, the deputed squire shall give
them water to the hands, each of you washing together with them;
and take heed that the whole household sit not at table with them;
and set not yourself at table before you have seen it all
provided, looking well everywhere to see if anything is lacking.
That done, you shall set yourself at the head of the table — not
for your own recreation, but for their entertainment — discoursing
of cheerful things, now presenting one, now another, yet ever
keeping your eye upon the table to see if anything be lacking; and
if it lack, remedy it deftly. For when those assorted gentlemen,
perhaps of diverse nations, see this great courtesy and
forethought of yours, they will give you infinite praise, and
through it your master shall gain honor.

And I assure you that in such banquets the servants of the guests
must respectively be as well treated as the guests themselves; for
the masters will not go blazoning and publishing the excellence of
the banquet and the good or ill treatment — but the servants will,
who talk often among themselves and with others.

CHAPTER X. Showing the steward the service to be done at his
master's ordinary table.

We have left behind, but not forgotten, a lesson that concerns you
in serving your lord's ordinary table, morning and evening;
whereupon we shall tell how you are to order the officers to
serve, and how they are to serve: cupbearer, carvers, squires, and
pages.

When your master lets you know, by one of his pages or
chamberlains, that he will eat, you shall send to the kitchen the
plate that shall be needed for the dishes, letting the master cook
know the hour of his service. But since I have already shown you
[elsewhere in this work] and ordered for him the daily service of
all the dishes that can serve for the morning, for the whole year,
the rest will be well; nor have I ordered you the suppers, not to
be long — but you may order them.

CHAPTER XI. The business of the squires and pages at the master's
ordinary meal.

It is the custom in those honored and great courts that, the pages
having served their lords some time as pages, when they have well
learned by long practice the furnishing of the table, and are come
out of pagehood, they are now taken for squires in the service of
the lord's eating. Such are they who, being noble and of good wit,
become with practice excellent stewards, and cupbearers and
carvers.

Seek to make three of those who seem to you most fit, calling
them, as has been said, secret squires; and one of them shall
serve the master's first dishes, the second the plate of the
boiled meat, and the third the salame — all three serving the
three first antipasti, as is shown you in our
order; the other squires and pages may serve the rest of the
table.

You shall go to the kitchen with these three squires of yours,
with a napkin apiece, and the others without — you entering alone
with the said three squires; and when you have had the secret and
covered dishes set out and served, and the wonted tastings made,
and taken them in hand, you shall come out with them; then letting
all the others enter for the other dishes, which the gentle cook
shall give you for the rest of the table. And once you are entered,
you shall let the cupbearer know his business.

CHAPTER XII. Of the Cupbearer [coppiere].

I must first, before I show you what the cupbearer's office is and
how he is to exercise it, tell you what manner of man he ought to
be — that office being so noble; and to describe him I know no
briefer way than to fashion him such as Count Baldassarre ["il Conte Baldassarre," Castiglione] fashions to form his
courtier: besides the gallantry of his person, mark that he be all
faith — young and not old, well-disposed and not rude, not
squinting nor blind, middling, neither too small nor too great,
fair of face and not ugly, cheerful and not melancholy, mannerly
and discreet; and that he have his hands white and delicate,
wearing on one of his fingers a pretty jewel of value, fair to
see; let his dress be honest and befitting, of rich long cloths and
not short, his sleeves above all; for nothing in the world let him
make show of those broad lettuce-ruffs of his shirt-sleeves,
worked in a thousand colors, like your strumpets; he shall wear a priest's
cap, scarlet hose, shoes of black velvet — and not red, for the
greatness of his office does not call for it — having in his keeping
the life of that most noble cardinal his master, or other prince.

And when he goes to take the cup or beaker, let him settle his cap
and ask the bottler the quality of the wine he would serve for the
first drinking; and having had his tasting made by the bottler,
largely, of the wine and water, let him take it and think in what
manner and gallantry he is to carry this service in hand. It will
be most seemly to carry it in a cup of gold — at least gilded — a
covered beaker, and his little flask of water, carrying it held
high, without timidity or faint-heartedness — as many do from
their worthlessness, who are ever afraid of tripping with their
feet, their eyes continually fixed on the ground, and like senseless
men go trembling with that service of the cup, so that at that
clatter one might easily make a morris-dance of it — not knowing
what they are about; but doing it our way, he shall carry it held
high and firm of arm, walking and showing a cheerful face.

And come before his lord, with his right hand let him uncover the
beaker, and deftly pour a little wine into the cup, putting it into
the master's hand; and likewise a little water; and the tasting
being made and the wine watered, let him set the cup beneath the
beaker. Let him make this ceremony quickly, chatting with His Most
Reverend [Lordship] of the quality of the wine until he has drunk;
and with a graceful act let him bow low — and not hunched —
and the beaker being taken up again, return to the bottle-office,
awaiting at his convenience the next return.

CHAPTER XIII. Of the Carver [trinciante].

The carver's office shall be made for a well-disposed, deft, and
clean young man — greedy for others and not for himself; and
though I would show you in what manner and from which side he is
to begin to serve, I know not how to be brief. But if you have a
novice in hand, he must keep continually beside an able carver when
he works, minding one thing alone at a time: how that carver
dismembers, how he carves small, how he cuts a thrush, or carves
fruits. And let him fix it in his memory; and returning to his
chamber, let him make trial of it. And having no beef, fowls, or
the like at hand, let him take a turnip, a great hard-crusted loaf,
or a well-closed cabbage-head. These will serve to learn small
carving. And to learn dismembering, let him make friends with one
of these able carvers, bringing with him good capons or pheasants
— providing himself above all with those knives which are nowadays
used for that purpose.

But to return to our first purpose: I say that, we having now
appeared with the first dishes, this carver of yours shall go to
the credenza to take, where it is locked up, the service of his
knives and the round dish of the service of the master's bread —
locked and covered with those most beautiful worked napkins. And
as soon as the lord has washed his hands and is set at table, let
him present himself with his gear at the table, leaving the master
on the right hand; and above all, let no man in the world be set
at table above him, unless it were another cardinal or some other
great personage.

And not to forget, we return to the cupbearer, who by your
commission shall already have taken the ewer and the flagon with
the water for the master's hands; and that discreet squire shall
present the napkin, folded between two round dishes, to the first
prelate — [or to the] servants of the house, if not to the steward
of the household or the first gentleman there be. The cupbearer
shall thus wash his master's hands; and a clean chamberlain shall
take the chair; and the others being seated by other squires and
pages, let the service of water to the hands be done, and let the
daily diners who eat at table with him be seated. And at this hour
the carver shall spread before him that napkin which serves for
the cover of his service; and with his right hand he shall take
the round dish of the bread service, and lifting with his left that
dish he keeps for a cover, he shall lay it face-down ["vi porrà à
bocca"], setting with his right the bread before the prince, near
his right hand, settling himself with his clean napkin carried on
his left arm.

And at this hour let the three squires appear with the first
courses, and present them at table beside the carver's left hand —
he giving them the wonted tastings; and with the same [squires]
you shall serve those of the credenza, keeping this order down to
the fruits. And your master being satisfied, or dallying, at this
hour, the squires shall lift all the fruits and the other things
that serve the master, and the pages shall lift the rest.

The carver, as he presented the first service of the bread, shall
now lift it; and with another clean round dish let him sweep the
table before the master; and with the wonted ceremony let him
cover again the one and the other service, consigning it to
another squire to carry it back — using his grateful reverence.
And at this hour the cupbearer must come, as at first, to give
water to the hands. All being washed and dried, the carver shall
take the tablecloth from the head where the master sits, and
kissing it shall lift it; and from the foot of the table, by a
secret squire, the service of the toothpicks shall be presented to
the carver; and the ceremony of the tasting being made, he shall
serve it.

At this hour you shall show how pleasing it has been to you to
have served your office diligently — with that courteous presence
and fine reverence, your cap in hand and your voice low, near the
master — you shall say to him "Prosit" [much good
may it do you].

I will not tangle your brain showing you how many, and in how many
ways, and in various of those noble sorts, are the many ceremonies
used in this table-service of those most reverend cardinals —
which, being for the most part Spanish ceremonies, I will tell
only three or four of, which truly seem to me to befit them, and
which please me much.

When your most reverend [master] washes his hands, before and
after, all the bystanders must stand far from the table and with
no cap on head. And that prelate who before has served with the
napkin — if he were seated, because he had eaten at the most
reverend's table — must rise to his feet, and taking the napkin
again, serve it anew; and taken back and returned to the squires,
he may sit again in his place — rising afterwards together with
the others.

And I tell you moreover that it is my opinion it is an honest
thing that when the cardinal thus rises, all the guests and other
daily diners must rise to their feet — yet not from their places;
and that till the master is risen and dried, they must not sit
again, but stand thus with cap in hand.

The other [ceremony] is that when the cardinal drinks at night —
the carver having nothing else to do — he must take the nearest
candlestick with its light, and bringing it near and holding it
high above the beaker, when the cupbearer comes, let him hold it
steady until [the master] has done drinking — or else a
chamberlain or gentleman [shall do it].

This next is of greater weight: that you must in seemly fashion
keep the bystanders and chatterers of these noble tables so far
off that you may without their disturbance serve your office;
which will be hard, there being many great presumptuous ones who
are not ashamed, not only to thrust themselves all beside the
master, but for the most part of them to be continually leaning on
his chair. And it is a great shame. It will fall to you to find
the way; but of this I will discourse to you more fully when we
come
to the order of a most beautiful wedding pair.

CHAPTER XIV. Showing the steward how he is to serve a pair of
most noble nuptials, or a banquet of gentlemen, for fifty guests at
table.

Having to order and serve a banquet of most noble nuptials, you
must — after you have examined the services and thought very well
upon your honor; think of the joy and good entertainment which the guests are to take of these nuptials. To
serve them as is the custom in Rome will be hard; nor does it
please me at all to separate the men's table from the women's; and
that, the room not being roomy for all, in some of them [rooms]
you lay one — whether for the men apart or for the women — and in
another you lay the other. It is not possible for the nuptials in
this way to be well served, nor the guests well satisfied; for the
chief joy of nuptials is the honest conversation and the sweet
sight which men have of women, and which women receive in being
entertained by men — which cannot take effect with them parted at
the tables.

The women's servants will triumph and the men's will struggle; and many times, leaving the men's table,
they go to the women's service — where the steward suffers great
pain, nor knows where to turn; and if there be nobles there, they
will not be merry or pleasant. But it delights me that you observe
the custom of the noble nuptials of our Florentine citizens; and
keeping that order, you must first find a hall so great that it
easily and commodiously receive all the tables you shall need.
And I would fain have the decking of it be by the hands of some of
our divine Florentine wits — of whom I have seen miracles. And
where that decking or laying is done, see that no man in the world
enter, until the hour of going to table — save those who have care
of it.

I remind you, moreover, to have other chambers decked near that
hall, where before the banquet those most noble ladies and
gentlemen guests may commodiously be entertained, with infinite
instruments of various musics and other pleasant pastimes.

In the guests' hall you shall lay two tables along the wall, so
that they may easily sit on the inner side — leaving between the
one table and the other a space through which they may
commodiously pass; and the said most noble ladies, accompanied
each by her husband or near kinsman — and where there is neither
the one nor the other, [by] those most noble guests. And I tell
you plainly that the office of setting the ladies at table must
not be yours, but a qualified lady's, who with another gentleman
of the house, or kinsman of the one or the other spouse, shall have
this care.

And the decking being most beautiful, your competent credenza
officer will make it honorable — if in a recess of the hall he lay
a credenza for show, beginning its steps near the dais, coming to
end upon a table high to a just man's breast, its top four palms
broad; and let it be furnished with vessels of gold, or gilded,
and most beautiful silvers, and others varied likewise — all
things for show; and the richer it is, the better. The table's top
may serve for all the comfits for the service of the guests; and
two practiced and well-trusted servants shall be its guardians.

At the other head, over against this, you shall lay the
bottle-office table, which shall liberally give all its service —
so far from the wall that the bottlers may commodiously serve it.
Over against the guests' table you shall lay the public credenza
for its service; and before this table a magnificent one, where
the credenza officers, passing between the one and the other, may
easily set out and serve all their dishes.

I would not have a most merry thing forgotten, and to our purpose
— most of all for the guests. Above the bottle-office table you
are to fix in the wall a little table after the fashion of a
pulpit, so broad that all the musics may easily be given there,
with those Lombard sounds.

Now, as I have shown you the laying of the table in the French
style, I must show you this one with its service. The two tables
of these guests being first covered with two most noble carpets.
The credenza officer shall lay them with two tablecloths apiece, worked and fancied after his fashion. And for each, then,
five most beautiful salt-cellars, with six silver candlesticks; the
service of the bread in a round dish, with cacchiatele and
Florentine bread; knife or fork; little ring-cakes,
biscuits, and morseletti of Naples.

And the number of the carvers shall be five; that discreet credenza
officer must present himself and set the service of their knives
in their places, so that each may serve ten guests. It will
not be amiss to have two most beautiful perfume-burners. At that
hour the credenza officer shall serve all those little things that
go for the accompaniment of the salad; and before the service of
the carvers' knives, a plate of salt-cured capons and a plate of
cold roe-deer pies — as is shown you at the beginning of those
nuptials.

I would fain furnish you with lights enough that all might be
seen; nor know I on which side to begin. But the show-credenza
being full of them, and the bottle-office, and the musicians' dais
with the guests' table, they may perhaps suffice; but if you see
they do not, hang from the dais one of those chandeliers with so
many beautiful works and brass candlesticks — which truly give
great light and seem of gold; in them let there be thick tapers
of white wax. And this light will be more
sweet-smelling than that of the torches hung with iron wires from
the dais — which, besides giving little light, fill the hall with
smoke.

But to come to the conclusion of what most imports: I say you must
remember the two lists for kitchen and credenza, which I showed
you to be needful when I described to you the banquet in the French
style. I say moreover that, for the cooks' great business, the
kitchen at this time must be well lighted and without smoke; that
its overseer be a most practiced and diligent man. And having to
serve five plates, with six varied dishes apiece, I would have you
take five most practiced squires, with five other pages — clean
and honorable servants; each of them, like stewards, with their
five helpers, shall observe their carver as their prince, keeping
this order from beginning to end.

Your practiced overseer shall have each plate set out and laid by
itself, with all its varied dishes — tables being laid in the
kitchen sufficient for the setting-out. And since the number of
those squires and servants is great, you must make election of a
practiced under-steward — so that when you have had the kitchen
and credenza dishes set and served, those squires shall return
trotting to the kitchen; [under] this under-steward they shall
enter one by one into the kitchen, each taking the first plate he
finds ordered there; and the dishes taken, they shall return forth
— the second entering, the third, and so to the fifth, each
awaiting the other.

In the meanwhile you shall watch and guard the table, [to see] if
aught be lacking; and at the hour that seems good to you, return
to the kitchen, your squires being returned furnished with your
dishes. And that you may know: for this service of kitchen and
credenza the number of thirty of them is needful. And when you are
at the service of the soups and sauces, part of the servants
ordered for the table shall supply — of whom you shall order two practiced ones for each of the carvers: one to keep him
furnished with silver round dishes, the other, when a round dish is
carved, to go present it diligently to that guest, first or second.

These shall lift all those plates which the carver shall have
furnished, ever leaving one apiece, until the other dishes appear
— giving them into the good keeping of the overseers of the
dishes. And for the service of handing the drink — you mean the
cupbearers — ten noble youths shall suffice; and to replenish the
table, six, who shall be six-and-forty in all. And for greater merriment, at every coming you make with the kitchen
dishes, let the Lombard instruments strike in, sounding while you
serve and set them on table. At this hour they shall halt,
beginning again other sweeter musics. Now it seems to me there is
nothing more to send, save to give water to the hands and set them
at table.

For this, then, order basins: five ewers with perfumed water,
putting them into the hands of five of those clean cupbearers, and
to ten others of those noble youths a perfumed towel apiece for
the service of the hands — each basin serving two guests at a
time. And they being in order, call five pages with five white
torches; and go to bid the bridegroom and the bride; and that
deputed gentlewoman and gentleman of whom we spoke shall couple
man and woman together to go sit at table. And see that, as they
move, a page with his lighted torch be ready for every ten couples;
and go you before, having the door of the decked hall — seen no
more by them — flung open, with great noise of those merry sounds
and other various musics. And as soon as all are entered,
each youth with his basin shall serve ten couples, and wash them
in the order said above.

Seated at table, those five clean carvers shall appear, setting
themselves where the service of their knives shall be, doing their
office. And you, seeing the first dishes served, shall go with five
pages with the torches to the kitchen — finding your squires in
order, as has been said — ordering a page with the torch for each
of the first squires, and the other [page] before [them]. And
continuing that order to the last course, you shall make an end of
your banquet. And in clearing the table the order shall be kept
which was told in serving the table in the French style; and the
same shall be done in the service upon the carpet.

But let us see that, after you have taken great pains to bring so
honorable a banquet to an end, for the multitude of those come to
see it, there arise no disturbance to dishonor everything for you;
and hereof we shall say briefly what is fit to be done.

CHAPTER XV. Showing the steward how and in what manner it would
be possible to quell tumults at great banquets.

Since it is the ordinary custom of peoples that where some merry
spectacle of games or nuptials or other such pleasant feasts is
made, the concourse of folk is great — and it is a hard thing to
expressly forbid them that entry, most of all where there is a
number of fair ladies — I find no other remedy to flee this
uproar than to order that in the house or palace where the nuptials
shall be made there be but one single entrance, and that it be
guarded by a guard that may easily withstand them against every
great press; otherwise remedies are scant.

But since many times this disorder is born by fault of the
servants or gentlemen of the house, who privily seek to bring in
their friends, you must order that none of them give lodging or
feast to any — though it were his brother — whereby their chambers
come to be filled with foreign courtiers and a number of masks,
making infinite banquets through the chambers, as is the custom in
this court of ours. And hence is born the ruin of the banquet; for
your first dishes being served, no sooner are they lifted than
they are vanished, and everything put to sack — they meaning with
them to entertain and honor their friends; and when they have well
filled their bellies, they go off a-chasing, and fill the banquet
hall without any respect — this one playing the nymph and that
the Ganymede, one in a mask and the other muffled; this one makes
love, and that shows himself love-sick; that one sets himself in
view to show his
person disposed to the ladies, thinking they will fall in love
with him.

Other swaggering and presumptuous ones thrust themselves so upon
the table that not only the steward cannot serve, but neither the
cupbearers nor the carvers, with all the rest of those who serve. I give you this for one of the
most useful warnings I can give you; and if by ill luck you forget
it, you will find there shall befall you what I have told you.

And if by chance [a tumult] befalling you, and you could give
yourself no other remedy, hasten with courtesy to those who most
disturb you; and those too whom you know to be the best-qualified
gentlemen, drawing them aside, entertaining them while the banquet
is kept up in those rooms where the guests diverted themselves
before supper, or elsewhere; and having drawn them there,
entertain them — or to say it better, see that they be entertained,
when you have no time to do it yourself — by having drink given
them, and some fruit, until your banquet is furnished; promising
them every courtesy, and then praying them to tarry here a while,
till the dancing is
sounded. And with this courtesy, if such men be not worse than
asses, they will not only offer to stand at obedience with their
persons, but will willingly keep the other common folk behind.
Nor make mock of this diligence, for I have proved it many times.

But I tell you plainly that when the banquet is finished, and the
tables cleared, and the plate put away, with great noise of sounds
see that all the tables be lifted and the doors flung open; and if
the doors suffice not for the crowd desirous to enter, let them in
by the windows too. Nor care you a whit — though your lady were
there, and were ogled and jeered at — it sufficing you only to have
brought your banquet to a good end. Nor for the present have I
more to tell you.

[Here ends the Fifteenth and last Chapter of the First Book — the
steward's treatise. What follows in the 1637 printing is the
opening of the Second Book, "the true seasons" of meats and
fishes, which lies outside this rescue.]
